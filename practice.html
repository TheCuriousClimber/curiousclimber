Contact Strength & Finger Power — full breakdown

Who it's for: intermediate-plus climbers who feel “not strong enough” on small holds and powerful moves, and want more force without adding weight.

Why buy it — the transfer: hard moves are limited by how much force you can produce right now (contact strength) and how completely you recruit your muscles. This block trains both through low-rep, high-intensity work — the stimulus that drives neural gains, so your strength-to-weight ratio improves. Stronger fingers and a more forceful pull show up directly as the ability to latch smaller holds and stick bigger moves.

💡
Evidence in plain terms: maximal, low-rep loading improves force output largely through better muscle recruitment rather than size — ideal for climbers. Progressive finger loading also builds tendon and pulley resilience over time. (See the strength-to-weight and finger anatomy articles.)

Anaerobic Capacity for Long Routes — full breakdown

Who it's for: sport climbers who feel strong on single moves but get “pumped” and fall off long, sustained routes.

Why buy it — the transfer: the forearm pump is largely about how long your muscles can keep contracting when blood flow is restricted by gripping. This program trains your capacity to buffer fatigue and resist the pump, so you can keep pulling through the back half of a pitch and still have something at the anchors.

💡
Evidence in plain terms: sport climbing draws heavily on anaerobic energy and is limited by local forearm endurance and blood-flow occlusion during sustained gripping. Targeted intervals improve tolerance to that stress. (See the members' “energy systems” deep-dive.)

Antagonist & Shoulder Resilience — full breakdown (free)

Who it's for: every climber. Climbing is a pulling sport; this keeps the rest of you balanced so you can keep training.

Why it matters — the transfer: healthy elbows and shoulders are what let you train consistently, and consistency is what drives long-term gains. Antagonist and rotator-cuff work directly reduces the overuse strain that sidelines climbers.

💡
Evidence in plain terms: balanced loading of pushing muscles, wrist extensors and the rotator cuff helps counter the pulling-dominant demands of climbing and supports joint health. (See the anatomy article on the pulling chain.)
