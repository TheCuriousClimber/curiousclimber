The Durable Climber — full breakdown (free)

Who it's for: the recreational climber who also skis, runs, swims or cycles and wants to keep doing all of it for decades — improving on the wall while staying injury-free.

Why it matters — the transfer: climbing already saturates pulling, gripping and finger loading, so piling on more of the same invites overuse. This plan deliberately builds what climbing under-trains — legs and posterior chain, pressing and overhead strength, external rotation and wrist-tendon health — the balance the injury-prevention research links to fewer overuse problems. Heavier loads at lower reps chase force per kilogram of bodyweight (strength-to-weight) rather than size.

💡
Evidence in plain terms: strength training roughly halves overuse injuries; relative finger strength and strength-to-weight discriminate climbing ability; tendons adapt to heavy, sustained loads slowly. You don't need to train to failure, and long rests build strength. (See the strength-to-weight and periodization articles, and the full write-up for citations.)

Contact Strength & Finger Power — full breakdown

Who it's for: intermediate-plus climbers who feel “not strong enough” on small holds and powerful moves, and want more force without adding weight.

✅ This block is for you if…

  • you've climbed consistently for ~2+ years and can crimp pain-free;
  • you've plateaued on repeaters and want a structured block, not another one-off protocol;
  • you have access to a hangboard and want to add load without guessing.

⚠️ It's not for you (yet) if…

  • you're under 16, have climbed under a year, or are new to hangboarding;
  • you currently have finger pain, or any recent pop, swelling or bowstringing;
  • you'd rather build a base first — start with the free Antagonist & Shoulder Resilience or Durable Climber programs and the readiness screen.

Why buy it — the transfer: hard moves are limited by how much force you can produce right now (contact strength) and how completely you recruit your muscles. This block trains both through low-rep, high-intensity work — the stimulus that drives neural gains, so your strength-to-weight ratio improves. Stronger fingers and a more forceful pull show up directly as the ability to latch smaller holds and stick bigger moves.

💡
Evidence in plain terms: maximal, low-rep loading improves force output largely through better muscle recruitment rather than size — ideal for climbers. Progressive finger loading also builds tendon and pulley resilience over time. (See the strength-to-weight and finger anatomy articles.)

Before you buy

What equipment do I need?

A hangboard with a 20 mm edge is essential. Added weight comes in during the strength and peak phases — a pulley system, weight belt or a loadable backpack is enough. The load tables start from bodyweight, so you can begin with just the board.

Is loading my fingers this hard safe?

Finger tendons and pulleys adapt to progressive load, but slowly — so the block adds only a small increment at a time and changes one variable per week. The rule throughout: loading is acceptable at up to about 3/10 discomfort that settles within 24 hours and doesn't trend upward week to week — never train through sharp pain. Pain that persists beyond 48 hours, or any pop, swelling or bowstringing, means stop and see a physiotherapist or sports physician before loading again. This is a training program, not treatment for an injury.

How much time does it take each week?

Three sessions a week. Each is short on actual work but built around long rests between a handful of hard, high-quality efforts, so budget roughly 30–45 minutes including a full warm-up. It's designed to sit alongside your climbing, not replace it.

Is this beginner-friendly?

No — and that's deliberate. It assumes about two years of consistent climbing and pain-free crimping. If that's not you yet, you'll get more from building a base first: start with the free Antagonist & Shoulder Resilience program and the finger-anatomy article.

How is this different from the free articles?

The free articles explain the why — the recruitment vs. density and strength-to-weight pieces cover the principles. The guide is the how: every session written out across the eight weeks, load tables by bodyweight, the safety gates, and the tracking sheet — the structure that turns the principles into a block you can actually follow.

Antagonist & Shoulder Resilience — full breakdown (free)

Who it's for: every climber. Climbing is a pulling sport; this keeps the rest of you balanced so you can keep training.

Why it matters — the transfer: healthy elbows and shoulders are what let you train consistently, and consistency is what drives long-term gains. Antagonist and rotator-cuff work directly reduces the overuse strain that sidelines climbers.

💡
Evidence in plain terms: balanced loading of pushing muscles, wrist extensors and the rotator cuff helps counter the pulling-dominant demands of climbing and supports joint health. (See the anatomy article on the pulling chain.)
