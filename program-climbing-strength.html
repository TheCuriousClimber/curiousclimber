The Durable Climber

A 12-week, 3-day-per-week progressive resistance program for the recreational climber who wants to climb harder and keep skiing, running, swimming and cycling for decades — built from peer-reviewed sport science.

By David McWeeny, MSc Kinesiology·Full program + rationale·Each session ≤ 90 min
⚠️ Read first. This is educational content, not medical advice. It assumes you are healthy and pain-free. If you have a current injury, unexplained pain, or a medical condition, clear resistance training with a qualified professional before you start. Progress load only when the previous load felt controlled and symptom-free.

Who this is built for

This program was designed for a specific athlete profile: a 34-year-old recreational climber who already climbs three days a week (a mix of highwall and bouldering), runs or swims once or twice a week, and wants to lift two to three days a week. The goals, in priority order, are to keep improving on the wall, stay injury-free, build athletic longevity, and preserve the capacity to enjoy other sports for many years.

Those goals shape every choice below. Because climbing already delivers an enormous dose of pulling, gripping and finger loading, the smartest resistance program does not pile more of the same on top. Instead it deliberately builds the tissues and qualities climbing under-trains — legs and posterior chain, pressing and overhead strength, external rotation and the muscles that balance a grip-dominant sport — because that balance is precisely what the injury-prevention literature associates with fewer overuse problems.1,2

The one-paragraph summary

Three full-body sessions per week, each capped at 90 minutes and built as RAMP warm-up → prehab/activation → main strength work → mobility cool-down. Twelve weeks split into three four-week blocks — Base & tissue prep → Maximal strength → Strength-power expression — with a planned deload at the end of each block. Load rises through double progression and is auto-regulated by reps-in-reserve (RIR) so it flexes around your climbing, running and swimming fatigue.

The evidence, in plain terms

A handful of well-replicated findings do most of the work in this design:

  • Strength training reduces injuries. A meta-analysis of controlled trials found strength training roughly halved overuse injuries and cut acute injuries substantially — a far larger protective effect than stretching or proprioception work alone.1 For a climber whose stated top priority is staying healthy, resistance training is the injury-prevention program.
  • What limits climbing is mostly finger strength and strength-to-weight — not muscle size. Systematic review evidence points to relative finger strength and forearm endurance as the strongest discriminators of climbing ability.3 That's why this plan chases force per kilogram of bodyweight through heavier loads and lower reps, rather than bodybuilding-style volume.4,5
  • Tendons adapt to high, sustained loads — slowly. Connective tissue responds to heavy, relatively long-duration loading and adapts more slowly than muscle, which is why the first block prioritizes controlled tempo and why we never spike volume.6
  • You don't need to train to failure, and long rests build strength. Stopping a rep or two shy of failure produces comparable strength and hypertrophy with less fatigue and lower injury risk7; 2–3 minute rests beat short rests for strength.8 Both matter when you're also climbing hard three days a week.
  • Periodized, progressively-overloaded programs beat doing the same thing forever, and roughly 10+ hard sets per muscle per week is a sensible dose for the average trainee — but that total has to include the work your sport already does.4,9
  • Warm up dynamically, stretch statically afterward. A task-specific dynamic warm-up (the RAMP model) prepares performance; long static holds before heavy work can transiently reduce force, so we park them in the cool-down.10,11
  • Concurrent endurance and strength can interfere — so this plan is scheduled to protect your heavy leg days from your hard run/swim days, and keeps total lower-body volume moderate.12

How the three days fit your week

You choose the calendar, but the relationships between sessions matter more than the exact days. Two rules keep interference and overuse in check:

  1. Separate the heavy-leg day (Day A) from your hardest running day by at least a day where you can. Doing maximal lower-body strength work and hard running within a few hours of each other is where the concurrent-training interference effect bites hardest.12
  2. Don't stack a lifting day's heavy pulling right before a limit bouldering session. Your fingers and elbows are the bottleneck; give them the freshest days for the wall.

The 12-week progression

The same three session templates run for all twelve weeks — what changes is the dose. Each four-week block shifts the intensity/rep/rest prescription and ends with a deload so accumulated fatigue drains and adaptations surface.4,9

RIR = reps in reserve — how many more reps you could have done with good form. "RIR 2" means you stop with two clean reps left in the tank. It's the simplest way to auto-regulate load around the fatigue of a busy climbing/endurance week.7

How to add load — double progression

Within a block, don't chase a heavier bar every session. Instead:

  1. Pick a load you can lift for the bottom of the rep range at the target RIR across all sets.
  2. Each week, try to add reps toward the top of the range at the same load.
  3. When you can hit the top of the range on every set at the target RIR, add the smallest load increment (2.5–5 kg lower body, 1–2.5 kg upper body) next session and drop back to the bottom of the range.

This is the minimum-friction way to guarantee progressive overload — the non-negotiable driver of adaptation — without ego-lifting into an injury.4,9 If a lift feels off, your RIR was optimistic, or you slept poorly, hold the load or reduce a set. Missing a small increment costs nothing; a tweaked pulley or elbow costs weeks on the wall.

A note on your fingers

You'll notice there is no hangboarding or dedicated max-finger work in this plan. That's deliberate. First, your listed equipment has no fingerboard/edge — max-hangs on an improvised edge are a reliable route to a pulley strain. Second, and more importantly, three climbing days already provide a large finger-loading stimulus; adding heavy isolated finger training on top is a classic overuse trap for a masters recreational athlete whose top priority is staying healthy.3 This program instead builds everything around the fingers — grip support strength, wrist-extensor and forearm tendon health, and shoulder/elbow balance — so your fingers get stronger from your climbing while the rest of you stops being the weak link. If you later want structured finger strength, do it as its own carefully-loaded session at the climbing gym, not squeezed onto a lifting day.

Day C — Power, carries & single-leg athleticism

The "athlete" day: fast, forceful hip extension for rate-of-force-development, unilateral leg work for balance and durable knees, loaded carries, and rotational/anti-rotation core. This is where max strength becomes usable, explosive movement — the quality that transfers to dynamic climbing, skiing and sprinting. Lower joint stress than the heavy days, so it slots well before a rest day. Total time ≈ 60–80 min.

The prehab & mobility library

The warm-ups above rotate through these. If a body part is grumbling, add a couple of its sets to any warm-up — this is your standing "physio" menu.

Elbow & forearm (climber's elbow / epicondylitis)
  • Wrist-extensor eccentrics — light KB or landmine end, 3–4 s lowering, 2–3 × 15. The best-evidenced defense against forearm tendinopathy; load is medicine for tendons.
  • Wrist-flexor eccentrics — same protocol, palm up, for the medial (inside) elbow.
  • Pronation / supination with an off-center load (KB by the horn, or a hammer-grip landmine), 2 × 12/side.
  • Reverse (pronated) band curls, 2 × 15 — brachioradialis & extensor endurance.
Shoulder & scapula (rotator cuff, overhead health)
  • Band external rotation (elbow pinned to side), 2 × 15 — supraspinatus/infraspinatus.
  • Prone or band Y-T-W raises, 2 × 8 each — lower trap & scapular upward rotation.
  • Face-pulls, 2 × 15 — rear delt & external rotation to balance all the pulling.
  • Scapular pull-ups & scapular push-ups, 2 × 8 — control at the shoulder blade, not just the arm.
  • Bottoms-up KB carry / press, 2 × 20 s or 6 reps — reflexive cuff stability.
Hips, knees & ankles (skiing, running, longevity)
  • Copenhagen plank — progressive adductor/groin strength; start bent-knee, build to full.
  • Spanish squat / band-resisted terminal knee extension, 2 × 12 — patellar-tendon-friendly quad loading.
  • Single-leg calf raises, 2 × 12 (add a slow eccentric) — Achilles & ankle resilience.
  • 90/90 hip switches & deep-squat holds — hip internal/external rotation range.
  • Banded lateral walks & glute bridges — glute-med for knee tracking.
Spine & trunk (brace, don't crunch)
  • Dead bug & bird dog, 2 × 8/side — anti-extension/anti-rotation control.
  • Pallof press & suitcase carry — resist rotation and lateral flexion under load.
  • Cat-camel & open-book rotations — segmental spine mobility.
  • Thoracic extension over foam roller — counter the climbing/desk hunch.

Recovery, auto-regulation & the long game

This program only works if it bends around the rest of your athletic life. Three habits make that automatic:

  • Auto-regulate every session by RIR and bar speed. On a day after hard bouldering or a big run, keep the same exercises but shave a set and pick loads that hit the higher end of the RIR target. Consistency across months beats heroics in any one week.7
  • Respect the deloads. Weeks 4, 8 and 12 are not optional. Backing off on purpose is when connective tissue catches up and strength surfaces — skipping deloads is the fastest route to the overuse injuries you're trying to avoid.6,9
  • Protect sleep and protein. They're the substrate for every adaptation here. Aim for consistent sleep and ~1.6 g protein per kg bodyweight per day to support recovery from concurrent training.

After week 12, you can re-run the block with heavier loads from your retest, swap in fresh exercise variations to keep the stimulus novel, or rotate toward whatever the coming season demands (more single-leg and eccentric work heading into ski season, for instance). The template is durable; the details should keep evolving — that's exactly what periodization means.
