The big idea

Strength-to-weight, not size

Well-programmed resistance training builds force output largely through neural adaptations — recruiting more motor units and firing them faster — with only modest muscle mass gain. You get stronger fingers, a more powerful pull, and a body that resists injury, without dragging extra weight up the wall.

Clearing the biggest myth in climbing

“Lifting weights will make me bulky and slow”

It's the single most common reason climbers avoid the weight room. It's also wrong for the way climbers actually train. Here's the short version.

✕ The myth

Any resistance training adds large amounts of muscle, which increases bodyweight, lowers your strength-to-weight ratio, and makes you worse at climbing.

✓ The evidence

Large muscle growth requires a specific recipe — high training volume, a calorie surplus, and hypertrophy-style rep ranges — that climbing-focused programs deliberately avoid. Low-rep, high-force training raises strength mostly through neural adaptations, improving your strength-to-weight ratio rather than hurting it.
