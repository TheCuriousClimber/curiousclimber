# Hips, High-steps & Mobility

### A 6-Week Evidence-Based Hip Mobility & Strength-Through-Range Program for Rock Climbers

*The Kinesiology of Climbing — Curious Climber*

---

> **Read this first.** This is educational strength-and-conditioning content, **not medical advice** and **not rehabilitation**. It assumes you are healthy, pain-free, and cleared for vigorous exercise. Loaded end-range mobility work places real demand on the hip joint, the groin (adductors), and the surrounding connective tissue. If you have a current hip, groin, low-back, knee, or SI-joint injury, unexplained pain, hip impingement (FAI), or a medical condition, clear resistance and mobility training with a qualified professional before you begin. Complete a Physical Activity Readiness screening (PAR-Q+) first. Progress range and load only when the previous week felt controlled and completely symptom-free — and never force cold, ballistic, or painful range.

## 1. Overview

**Hip mobility** — and, more precisely, **usable range of motion at the hip** — is the quality that decides whether you can plant a foot at hip height and actually stand up on it, drop a knee deep enough to lock a heel and de-pump, flag a leg across your body for balance, rock over onto a high foot without your hips wandering off the wall, or stem a wide corner without your pelvis tipping you out. It is the difference between *seeing* the high foot and *using* it.

This program develops that quality through a combination of **mobility drills, loaded strength-through-range work, and active-flexibility protocols**, layered over six weeks and then applied directly to climbing movement. It moves you through three deliberate phases — a mobility base that raises and maps your available range, a strength-through-range block that teaches you to *control and produce force* at the ends of that range, and a climbing-specific application block that welds the new range into high-steps, drop-knees, flags, rock-overs, and stems on the wall.

**How this differs from its siblings.** This is **not** a finger-strength program (that is *Contact Strength & Finger Power*, which trains how *hard* your fingers pull in a brief effort), and it is **not** an endurance program (that is *Anaerobic Capacity for Long Routes*, which trains how long you can tolerate the forearm pump). This program trains almost none of the forearm. It trains your **hips and legs** — specifically *usable range of motion and control at end range*. Where the strength siblings ask "how much force?" and "for how long?", this one asks a different question: "**can you reach the position at all, and can you generate force once you are in it?**" You can be immensely strong and hopelessly pumped-proof and still fail a move because your hip will not open far enough to bring the foot up — and this is the program for that.

**Who it's for:** climbers at any level who feel *blocked by their body position* rather than by raw strength or endurance — those who watch a taller or bendier partner flow through a high-step or drop-knee they simply cannot get into, whose knees flare wide in a deep squat, or who feel their hips "fall off" the wall on steep terrain. It is scalable through range (how deep you go), load (bodyweight to lightly loaded), and support (assisted to unassisted).

**Expected outcome (with consistent, frequent execution):** a measurable increase in *usable* hip range — external rotation, abduction, and flexion — that you can actually **stand on and pull from**, better control at the ends of that range, and a direct transfer into higher, more secure high-steps, deeper drop-knees, more stable flags, and calmer, more efficient hips on the wall. Mobility responds relatively quickly to frequent, patient work — but it also regresses if you stop, so it is best treated as an ongoing habit rather than a one-time fix.

> **What this program is *not*.** It is not a rehab plan, not a contortionist/extreme-flexibility routine, not a substitute for climbing and drilling movement on real terrain, and not a way to "stretch out" a painful or impinging hip. It is one focused mobility-and-control block that sits *inside* a broader climbing life, and it develops range you can **use under load**, not passive party tricks.

## 2. Prerequisites & Readiness

Mobility work is often assumed to be risk-free — it is not. Its injury risk is not the dramatic single overload of a max effort; it is the quieter danger of **forcing range the joint isn't ready for**, loading the **groin/adductors** aggressively at long lengths, or grinding into a **joint that is pinching rather than stretching**. Gating who runs this, and how patiently, is the most important safety decision in the program.

### Minimum standards before dedicated loaded mobility work

| Criterion | Minimum standard before starting |
|---|---|
| **Pain-free hips & groin** | No current or recent **hip, groin, low-back, SI-joint, or knee** pain, and no known **hip impingement (FAI) or labral issue**. End-range loading is exactly what aggravates an irritated hip — it needs to be quiet first. |
| **Basic movement competence** | Able to perform a **bodyweight squat and a lunge** with reasonable control and no pain, even if the depth or width is limited. This program *improves* those patterns; it assumes you can perform a scaled version of them safely. |
| **Willingness to train frequently** | Mobility responds to **frequency**, not to occasional heroic sessions. You need to be able to do **short, near-daily** mobility work. If you can only train once a week, expect slow progress. |
| **General readiness for exercise** | Cleared for vigorous exercise, or able to complete the PAR-Q+ below without flags. |
| **Patience with range** | The willingness to progress range *gradually* and to stop at a pinch. This is a temperament prerequisite as much as a physical one — the impatient injure the groin. |

### Readiness reminder (PAR-Q)

Before any new training block, complete a **Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q+)**. If you answer "yes" to any item — or you are over 40 and inactive, pregnant/postpartum, or have a heart, blood-pressure, metabolic, joint, or bone condition — clear vigorous exercise with a physician or qualified professional first. Hip, groin, and low-back joint conditions in particular deserve professional clearance before loaded end-range work.

### Who should NOT run this yet

> **Do not start this program if you:**
> - Have **any** current or recent hip, groin, low-back, SI-joint, or knee injury or pain — even mild. Loaded end-range mobility work drives directly into the tissues that need to be quiet to heal. Seek a qualified professional; return to end-range loading only when cleared and symptom-free.
> - Have known or suspected **hip impingement (FAI), a labral tear, or hip dysplasia**. Deep flexion, adduction, and rotation are precisely the positions that provoke these; this generic program cannot be individualized around your joint's shape. Get assessed first.
> - Feel a **sharp pinch in the front or deep in the crease of the hip** at end range (as opposed to a stretch in the muscle). That is a stop signal, not a "tightness" to push through — see §8.
> - Are in an unusually stressful, sleep-deprived, or heavily loaded training period where you cannot recover from added leg and connective-tissue work.
> - Are hypermobile / very lax-jointed. You likely need **control and strength at range far more than more range** — bias this program heavily toward its loaded and active-control work, keep passive end-range gentle, and consider professional guidance.
>
> There is no rush. A strained groin or an irritated hip can nag for months. A conservative, patient ramp costs a few weeks.

## 3. The Science in Plain Language

### What "mobility" actually is

Climbers often use "flexibility" and "mobility" interchangeably. They are not the same, and the difference is the whole point of this program.

- **Flexibility** is *passive* range: how far a joint can be moved by an outside force — gravity, your hands pulling a leg up, a partner pushing. It is what a static stretch trains.
- **Mobility** is *usable* range: how far you can move a joint **under your own control, actively, with the muscles that cross it able to produce force there**. It is flexibility **plus** strength and control at the end of the range.

> **The one-sentence version:** flexibility is how far you can be *put*; mobility is how far you can *go and then do something useful* — and climbing needs the second one.

### Active range vs. passive range

Picture lying on your back and having a partner lift your straight leg toward your chest until the hamstring stops it — that farthest point is your **passive range**. Now have them let go: the height you can *hold the leg at on your own* is your **active range**. For almost everyone, the active number is smaller — sometimes much smaller. The gap between them is range you technically "have" but cannot use, because the muscles that would hold you there aren't strong or coordinated at that length.

**Climbing lives in the active number.** A high foot is not a passive stretch — it is you actively flexing and rotating your hip to *place* the foot, then producing force through that fully-flexed hip to *stand up*. If the range only exists passively, the foot goes on but you cannot rock over it. Closing the gap between passive and active range — turning "flexibility" into "mobility" — is the core job of this program, and it is why passive stretching alone is not enough.

### Why passive stretching alone falls short

Static stretching can increase how far a joint *can be moved* (partly by making you more tolerant of the stretch sensation, partly through tissue change). But it does relatively little to build **strength and control at the new end range** — and that is exactly what standing on a high foot demands. Worse, range you cannot control is range your body's protective reflexes will resist giving you when it matters, and end range you cannot stabilize is where tissues are most vulnerable. So this program uses stretching as one tool among several, and always pairs new range with **loading and active control** so the range becomes usable and defended, not just accessible on a good day on the floor.

### A quick tour of the hip (the legible version)

The hip is a deep **ball-and-socket** joint — the ball of the thigh bone (femur) sitting in the socket of the pelvis. Being a ball-and-socket, it moves in every direction, and climbing exploits three of those directions heavily:

- **Flexion** — bringing the thigh up toward the torso. This is your **high-step**: the higher the foot, the deeper the flexion. Deep squats and rock-overs live here too.
- **External rotation** — rotating the thigh so the knee turns outward. This is the **drop-knee** and the **frog / 90-90** position, and it is what lets you turn a hip into the wall on steep ground so your centre of gravity comes in close.
- **Abduction** — taking the thigh out to the side, away from the midline. This is **stemming** a wide corner, **flagging** a leg out for balance, and the width of a **deep squat** or straddle.

Most climbing positions blend these — a high drop-knee is flexion *and* external rotation; a wide high-step is flexion *and* abduction. That is why this program trains blended, climbing-shaped positions (90-90, frog, deep squat, Cossack) rather than isolating one direction at a time.

### The ranges climbing actually demands

A handful of floor positions capture most of what climbing asks of the hips, and they recur throughout this program as both drills and tests:

- **90-90** — sitting with front and back legs each bent 90°, one hip in external rotation and one in internal rotation. The most direct trainer of the rotational range behind drop-knees.
- **Frog** — on all fours (or on your front) with knees wide and shins out, sinking the hips back. Trains external rotation and abduction together — the base of a wide, hips-in position.
- **Deep squat** — a full-depth squat with the hips below the knees. Trains flexion, and the ability to *live* in a deeply-flexed hip, which underpins rock-overs and high-steps.
- **Straddle / pancake** — seated with legs wide, hinging forward. Trains abduction and the adductor length behind stemming and wide flags.

### Why range without control is a liability

Two related qualities live under "mobility," and this program deliberately builds both, in order:

- **Range** — how far the joint can go. Raised in Weeks 1–2 (and maintained throughout) with frequent, gentle mobility work.
- **Control and strength at range** — the ability to *stabilize and produce force* once you're there. Built in Weeks 3–4 with loaded end-range work, and applied in Weeks 5–6 on the wall.

Range without control isn't just useless for climbing — it's where tissues get hurt, because the end of an uncontrolled range is where a joint is least protected. This is why the program never simply chases a bigger number on the floor; every gain in range is paired with the strength to own it.

> **The takeaway:** you get a usable high-step by *raising* your available range with frequent mobility work, then *claiming* it with strength and control at the new end range, then *applying* it to real movement on the wall — in that order.

## 4. Equipment Needed

This is the most minimal-gear program in the series. Its main requirements are floor space, a wall, and patience.

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Floor / mat space** | Enough clear floor to sit in a wide straddle and lie down — roughly a 2 m × 2 m area. A **yoga or exercise mat** makes floor positions (90-90, frog, pancake) far more tolerable and is strongly recommended. |
| **A wall (ideally a climbing wall)** | For the high-step, drop-knee, flag, and rock-over application drills in Phase 3, and for the standardized high-step test. A **spray wall, systems board, or low-angle bouldering wall** with adjustable feet is ideal; a plain wall with a marked target height also works for the drills. |
| **A sturdy support** | A **doorframe, squat rack upright, wall edge, or the edge of the bouldering wall** to hold for balance during standing mobility (hip CARs, hip airplanes, assisted Cossack squats). Balance support lets you focus on range instead of not falling over. |
| **A raised surface / box / step** | A **box, bench, chair, or a marked series of climbing footholds** to progress high-step height. Any stable, incrementally-adjustable height works. |
| **Light weights or a kettlebell** *(optional but recommended)* | A **light dumbbell, kettlebell, or plate (~2–8 kg)** to load Cossack squats, deep split squats, and adductor work. Loading is what turns range into *usable* range — even a light load matters. Bodyweight-only is a valid starting point. |
| **A light resistance band** *(optional)* | For **banded distraction / assistance** on floor positions, contract-relax drills, and light adductor/abductor strength work. Not essential; useful for scaling. |
| **A logbook or app + a way to measure** | To record range at your test positions (see §9) and how they change. A **tape measure** (for pancake reach and high-step height) and ideally **a phone to film** your positions from the same angle each time — mobility change is slow and easy to miss without a record. |

> **Setup note.** The single most useful asset is **consistent, low-friction access to floor space** — because this program lives or dies on *frequency*. If your mat is out and your floor is clear, you'll do the five-minute daily sessions that drive the adaptation. If setting up is a chore, you won't. Make the daily work easy to start.

## 5. The Warm-Up Protocol (Every Session)

Never drive into end-range mobility work cold. Cold tissue is stiffer, less compliant, and far more likely to be strained or to hit a joint pinch instead of a muscular stretch. A mobility warm-up is shorter than a strength warm-up (about 8–12 minutes) and has a specific job: **raise tissue temperature and blood flow, then move the hips actively through progressively larger ranges** so that when you reach end range, the tissue is warm and the joint is prepared. It has three stages: raise the temperature, mobilize actively, then progressively approach end range.

### Stage 1 — Temperature raise (3–5 min)
Get blood moving and body temperature up: easy cardio (brisk walk, light jog, skipping, jumping jacks, a few minutes on a bike) or a couple of minutes of easy climbing/traversing. You want to feel genuinely warm — light sweat — before you load any range. **Never stretch aggressively into a cold hip.** For end-range hip work, this temperature raise is not optional garnish; it is the single biggest thing separating a productive session from a strained groin.

### Stage 2 — Active mobilization (3–4 min)
Move the hips through their *current* comfortable range, actively and under control, before asking for more:
- **Hip CARs (Controlled Articular Rotations)** — 2–3 slow, deliberate circles per hip, taking the joint through its full pain-free range (detailed in §7). The best single hip warm-up in this program.
- **Leg swings** — forward/back and side-to-side, 10 each direction per leg, controlled (not ballistic), gradually letting the range grow.
- **Bodyweight squats and gentle lunges** — 8–10 reps, sinking a little deeper each rep within comfort.
- **Cat-cow / pelvic tilts** — to wake up the low back and pelvis that the hips work with.

### Stage 3 — Progressive approach to end range (2–3 min)
Rather than dropping straight into your deepest position, sneak up on end range:
- Enter your key positions (90-90, frog, deep squat) at a **shallow, easy version first**, breathe, and only then explore gently deeper.
- Move *in and out* of the position several times rather than holding your deepest range immediately — each entry can go a touch further as the tissue gives.
- The first time you reach a genuine stretch, it should feel like a **warm, workable muscular stretch — never a sharp pinch**.

> **Warm-up rule.** By the end of the warm-up, moving into your key positions should feel *open and warm*, not cold and blocked. If a hip feels stiff, catchy, or pinchy during the warm-up — as opposed to a normal easing muscular tightness — treat it as information. Reduce the day's depth and load, keep everything gentle, or stop. Cold, pinchy, or "catching" hips are a reason to back off, not to push through.

## 6. The 6-Week Periodized Schedule

### Phase logic at a glance

| Phase | Weeks | Primary goal | Loading character | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1 — Mobility base** | 1–2 | Raise available range and map your baseline; grease the key positions | **High-frequency, low-intensity.** Near-daily short mobility work — CARs, 90-90, frog, adductor/pancake — plus gentle active-range work | Mobility responds to *frequency*, not to occasional hard sessions. This phase raises range and re-familiarizes the hips with end-range positions so the loaded work in Weeks 3–4 has range to strengthen. |
| **Phase 2 — Strength-through-range** | 3–4 | Turn new passive range into *usable, controllable* range | **Loaded end-range work** — Cossack squats, deep split squats, adductor/abductor strength, hip airplanes — plus active-flexibility (contract-relax / PAILs-RAILs) | Range you cannot control is not usable and not safe. Loading the end range builds strength and control there, closing the gap between passive and active range — the whole point. |
| **Phase 3 — Climbing-specific application** | 5–6 | Weld the new range into climbing movement | **On-wall movement drills** — high-step, drop-knee, flag, rock-over, stem — at low intensity, integrating range under real climbing demand | Range gained on the floor does not automatically show up on the wall. This phase practises the exact movements so the new mobility becomes reflexive climbing, then re-tests. |

**Sessions per week.** Unlike its strength and endurance siblings, this program is **higher-frequency and lower-intensity** — because that is how mobility adapts. Aim for **short mobility work 5–6 days per week** (as little as 8–15 minutes each), with **2 slightly longer loaded or application sessions per week** layered in during Phases 2–3. A little, often, beats a lot, rarely. The daily "greasing" of range is the engine; the twice-weekly loaded sessions are what make the range usable.

> **Fit around climbing, not on top of a stressed system.** The short daily mobility work is low-intensity and can sit alongside your normal climbing and training — it is closer to a warm-up habit than a workout. The loaded Phase 2 sessions and on-wall Phase 3 drills carry more leg and connective-tissue cost; do those when reasonably fresh, ideally as part of or just before a climbing session (for the application drills) rather than after you're exhausted. If a week is unusually stressful or under-slept, keep the daily mobility and drop the loaded volume — never force range into a fatigued, poorly-recovering body.

### Week 1 — Phase 1: Mobility base (assess & grease)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–6 | Daily mobility (short) | Warm-up → **Hip CARs**, **90-90 transitions**, **frog**, **adductor/pancake** (gentle, see §7) → 8–15 min total, low intensity |
| One of these days | **Baseline testing** | Full warm-up → record your **baseline range tests** from §9 (deep-squat quality, pancake reach, 90-90, standardized high-step) under controlled conditions |
| 1 rest/easy day | Optional off day | Full rest or gentle movement is fine; mobility tolerates frequency but you don't have to force all 6 days if fatigued |

**Focus:** re-familiarize the hips with the key positions and establish your **baseline** early in the week. Keep every position **gentle — an easy, warm muscular stretch, never a pinch**. Resist the urge to "win" a deep position on day one; you are greasing the groove and gathering data, not maxing out.

### Week 2 — Phase 1: Mobility base (progressed)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–6 | Daily mobility (short) | Warm-up → same positions as Week 1, exploring **slightly deeper range** and adding a few seconds to holds → 10–15 min total |
| 1 rest/easy day | Optional off day | As Week 1 |

**Progression:** add *depth and time*, not force — sink a little deeper into the same positions as the tissue allows, and lengthen holds modestly. Keep intensity low (this is still an easy muscular stretch). If Week 1 left a hip or groin achy beyond a normal easing tightness, repeat Week 1's gentler range instead of progressing.

### Week 3 — Phase 2: Strength-through-range

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–5 (short) | Daily mobility (maintain) | Warm-up → CARs + 2–3 key positions, brief → keeps the range you built while Phase 2 loads it |
| Day A (longer) | **Loaded mobility** | Thorough warm-up → **Cossack squat**, **deep split squat**, **adductor strength**, **hip airplanes** (see §7) → optional light **contract-relax** on a key position |
| Day B (longer) *(48h+ after Day A)* | **Loaded + active flexibility** | Warm-up → loaded mobility (as Day A, may vary the lifts) → **contract-relax / PAILs-RAILs** on 90-90 or pancake |

**Shift:** intensity rises and the work becomes *loaded and active*. You are now strengthening the range you opened in Weeks 1–2. Load is **light-to-moderate** and every rep is controlled through the **full available range** — depth before weight. Target an effort where the last rep of a set is hard but clean, never a grinding failure at end range.

### Week 4 — Phase 2: Strength-through-range (progressed)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–5 (short) | Daily mobility (maintain) | As Week 3 |
| Day A (longer) | **Loaded mobility** | Warm-up → loaded work, **+ a little load OR a little more depth** vs. Week 3 (not both) → contract-relax option |
| Day B (longer) *(48h+ after Day A)* | **Loaded + active flexibility** | Warm-up → loaded work progressed → **PAILs-RAILs** on a key position |

**Progression:** add *one* variable at a time — a little more depth, or a little more load, or one more set — never several at once, and never depth *and* load in the same jump. If Week 3 left the groin or hip achy, hold Week 3's numbers. Owning a controlled, loaded end range is the win; a deeper number you can't control is not.

### Week 5 — Phase 3: Climbing-specific application

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–5 (short) | Daily mobility (maintain) | Warm-up → CARs + key positions, brief → keep the range primed |
| Day A (on wall) | **Application drills** | Warm-up (include on-wall mobilization) → **high-step drills**, **rock-over drills** (see §7) on easy terrain, focusing on using the new range |
| Day B (on wall) *(48h+ after Day A)* | **Application drills** | Warm-up → **drop-knee drills**, **flag drills**, **stemming practice** on easy terrain |

**Focus:** take the range you built and *use it on the wall*. Keep the climbing **easy** — the goal is clean movement quality and integrating the new range, not hard sends. Deliberately choose or set problems that *demand* high feet, drop-knees, and flags so you're forced to use the mobility. Maintain the daily short mobility so the range doesn't slip while you drill it.

### Week 6 — Phase 3: Application, consolidate & re-test

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–4 (short) | Daily mobility (maintain) | Warm-up → key positions, brief |
| Day A (on wall) | Application + light load | Warm-up → **integrate all movements** (high-step, drop-knee, flag, rock-over, stem) into easy climbing → light loaded mobility to maintain |
| *(2–3 days lighter)* | Ease off | Keep the daily mobility gentle; let any accumulated leg fatigue settle before re-testing |
| Day B | **Re-test** | Full warm-up → repeat the **standardized range tests** from §9 under identical conditions → record |

> **Week 6 consolidates rather than piles on.** Mobility does not need a big "peaking" effort — it needs the new range rehearsed under real movement and then measured. Keep loaded volume modest this week, let the legs settle before the re-test, and let the range you built show up cleanly on the benchmarks.

### Auto-regulation rule (all weeks)

Prescriptions are targets, not orders. Each session, gate depth, load, and volume on how the warm-up felt and your recent sleep/stress:
- **Warm, open, control good, no pinch:** train the prescribed work (progress if scheduled).
- **Stiff / cold / under-recovered / range feels blocked:** keep everything gentle, reduce depth and load, hold your position shallower, or do only the daily mobility.
- **Any sharp joint pinch, groin pain, or "catching" in the hip:** stop that movement immediately. Back off to pain-free range, reassess next session. See §8.

## 7. Detailed Prescriptions by Method

Intensity here is gauged not by weight on a bar but by **depth of range** and the **quality of your control** at that depth, plus (for loaded work) a light-to-moderate load. "End range" means the deepest position you can reach with **a muscular stretch, control, and no joint pinch** — not the deepest position you can be forced into.

> **How to read effort here:** the target sensation is a **strong but workable muscular stretch** you can breathe into and hold with control (roughly a 5–7 out of 10 stretch intensity), *never* a sharp pinch, and *never* a stretch so intense you're bracing or holding your breath. **We do not chase pain or maximal depth.** Range earned gently and controlled is range you keep and can use; range forced is range that strains tissue.

### Method 1 — Hip CARs (Controlled Articular Rotations) — all phases, near-daily

The single best hip-mobility staple: slow, active, full-range circles that take the joint through everything it's got, build control at the edges, and serve as both warm-up and daily maintenance.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Position | On all fours, or standing holding a support for balance |
| Movement | Draw the knee up (flexion), out to the side (abduction + external rotation), around and back (extension), and return — the biggest **slow, controlled circle** the hip can make, pain-free |
| Tempo | **Slow** — ~5–10 seconds per full circle. Speed hides range; slowness reveals and builds it |
| Reps | **2–3 circles each direction, per hip** |
| Effort | Low. Actively drive the range with your own muscles; keep the rest of the body still |
| Frequency | **Daily** (also part of every warm-up) |
| Progression | Bigger, slower circles as control improves; keep every degree pain-free and pinch-free |

### Method 2 — 90-90 transitions (external + internal rotation) — Phases 1–2

The most direct trainer of the rotational range behind **drop-knees**. Sitting in 90-90 and, later, actively transitioning side to side builds both range and control in rotation.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Position | Seated, front leg bent 90° (shin across, hip externally rotated), back leg bent 90° (hip internally rotated). Sit tall |
| Static hold | Sit at a depth where you feel a **glute/hip stretch, not a pinch**; hinge forward gently over the front shin to progress |
| Hold time | **20–40 s per side**, breathing into the position |
| Active transition *(progression)* | Lift both knees and **rotate to the other side using only your hips** (hands off the floor as control improves) — 5–8 slow transitions |
| Sets | 2–3 per side |
| Frequency | Daily to most days in Phase 1; maintained in Phases 2–3 |
| Progression | Deeper forward lean; hands-off active transitions; longer holds. Stop at any front-of-hip pinch |

### Method 3 — Frog & pancake/straddle (abduction + adductor length) — Phases 1–2

Opens external rotation, abduction, and adductor length together — the range behind **wide high-steps, stemming, and wide flags**.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| **Frog** | On all fours, knees wide, shins out, feet in line with knees; sink hips back toward the heels to a **muscular stretch in the inner thigh/groin** |
| Frog dose | Gentle rocks in and out, then a **20–40 s hold** at a workable depth; 2–3 rounds |
| **Pancake / straddle** | Seated, legs wide (only as wide as you can sit tall), hinge **forward from the hips** with a long spine — chest leads, not a rounded back |
| Pancake dose | **20–40 s holds**, 2–3 rounds; explore side-to-side reaches to bias one adductor at a time |
| Effort | Low-to-moderate muscular stretch; **groin is a high-risk area — never bounce or force** |
| Frequency | Daily to most days in Phase 1; maintained after |
| Progression | A little wider, a little deeper forward, slightly longer holds — one small change at a time |

### Method 4 — Loaded mobility: Cossack squat & deep split squat (strength through range) — Phase 2

The core of Phase 2: strengthening the deep, wide, and long positions so range becomes *usable*. Load is light; **depth and control come before weight**.

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Loading / depth | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cossack squat** | 2–3 × 5–8 per side | Bodyweight → light DB/KB held at chest; sink to your **controlled end depth** on the bent leg, other leg straight | Trains loaded abduction + deep flexion — the base of wide high-steps and rock-overs. Heel down, chest up |
| **Deep split squat / deep lunge** | 2–3 × 6–8 per side | Bodyweight → light load; drop the back knee toward the floor, sink the **front hip deep** | Loaded deep hip flexion — the high-step pattern. Control the depth; don't collapse into it |
| **Assisted Cossack (regression)** | 2–3 × 6–8 per side | Hold a support to offload; go only as deep as control allows | Use this if depth is limited or balance is the limiter |

Rest **60–90 s** between sets. Every rep moves *through* the range under control — no bouncing at the bottom, no dropping into end range.

### Method 5 — Loaded mobility: adductor/abductor strength & hip airplanes (control at range) — Phase 2

Targeted strength for the groin (adductors) and the rotators/abductors — the tissues most exposed by wide positions and most prone to strain if weak at length.

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Loading | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Copenhagen adductor (scaled)** | 2–3 × 5–8 per side | Start **short-lever** (knee on bench) before long-lever | Builds groin strength at length — directly protective against adductor strain. **Progress very conservatively; the groin is easily overdone** |
| **Adductor squeeze / banded adduction** | 2–3 × 8–12 | Light band or ball squeeze | Gentler adductor strength option; good early-Phase-2 entry |
| **Hip airplane** | 2–3 × 5–8 per side | Bodyweight, hold a support | Single-leg hinge with controlled internal/external rotation of the stance hip — trains rotational control and balance behind drop-knees and rock-overs |
| **Banded hip abduction / clamshell** | 2 × 12–15 per side | Light band | Abductor/rotator endurance; supports a stable hip on the wall |

Rest **60–90 s** between sets. Introduce Copenhagens especially gently — a day-after groin soreness that lingers means back off.

### Method 6 — Active-flexibility: contract-relax / PAILs-RAILs (usable end range) — Phase 2

Active-flexibility techniques use **contractions at end range** to safely nudge range further *and* build strength there at the same time — the fastest route from passive to usable range. Powerful, but easy to overdo; follow the cautions.

**Contract-relax (the simple version):**
1. Move into a key position (e.g., pancake, 90-90, frog) to a **gentle** end-range stretch.
2. **Contract** the stretched muscles *against the floor or your own body* — gently push into the ground — at about **20–30% effort** for **5–10 seconds**. Do not move; this is an isometric.
3. **Relax**, breathe out, and ease a little deeper into the now-more-tolerant range.
4. Repeat **2–3 times**, then hold the new depth for 20–30 s.

**PAILs / RAILs (Progressive/Regressive Angular Isometric Loading — the loaded version):**
- At your end range, **PAILs**: gradually build a contraction *pushing further into* the stretch (into the floor/support) over ~10 s, ramping to a moderate ~50–70% effort, hold ~10 s.
- Then **RAILs**: gradually build a contraction *pulling out of* the stretch — actively trying to lift/pull yourself *deeper using the muscles on the other side* — over ~10 s, hold ~10 s. This is the active-control piece.
- **1–2 rounds per position.** These are demanding; a little goes a long way.

| Caution | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Warm first** | Only after a full warm-up and never on cold tissue |
| **Ramp effort gradually** | Build and release contractions **smoothly** — never a sudden hard clench at end range, which is how tissue strains |
| **Groin caution** | Be especially gentle applying these to wide/adductor positions |
| **Frequency** | **1–2 sessions/week**, not daily — active-flexibility work needs recovery like any strength stimulus |
| **Stop signals** | Any sharp pinch, joint pain, or groin twinge = stop that position (see §8) |

### Method 7 — On-wall application drills (transfer to climbing) — Phase 3

Range doesn't transfer to climbing by itself — you have to *rehearse the movements*. Keep the climbing easy so all attention goes to using the new range well.

| Drill | How to do it | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| **High-step drill** | On easy terrain, deliberately choose the **highest reasonable foot** each move; plant it, then rock weight over it and stand up *with the hip*, hands light | Loaded deep hip flexion; using new range under bodyweight |
| **Drop-knee drill** | On slightly steep terrain, set up drop-knees: turn the hip in, drop the knee, feel the heel/foot lock and the hips come to the wall | External rotation applied; getting the centre of gravity in |
| **Flag drill** | Practise inside, outside, and back flags: extend a leg across or behind for balance instead of matching or bringing it up | Abduction + control; balance from the hip |
| **Rock-over drill** | Plant a high foot, then **shift your whole centre of mass over it** smoothly and stand, keeping the hip in | Combining flexion + rotation under control; the "stand up on the high foot" skill |
| **Stemming practice** | Find or set a wide corner/two facing walls; press out wide with both feet and move up | Abduction under load; a stable, wide, hips-in position |

Dose: **10–20 minutes** of focused drilling within an easy climbing session, 2× per week in Phase 3. Quality over quantity — a few clean, deliberate high-steps beat many sloppy ones.

### Method 8 — Antagonist & supportive work (optional, all phases)

The hips work with the low back, glutes, and core. A little general lower-body and trunk strength supports the mobility work and stabilizes the new range on the wall.

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Glute bridge / hip thrust** | 2–3 × 10–15 | Glutes, hip extension | Balances all the flexion/rotation work; supports a strong hip on the wall |
| **Dead bug / anti-rotation core** | 2–3 × 8–12 | Trunk control | A stable trunk lets the hips move freely under it |
| **Calf & ankle mobility** | 2 × 10–15 | Ankle dorsiflexion | Stiff ankles sabotage deep squats and high-steps — worth including |

## 8. Injury Prevention & Tissue Health

> **This is the most important section in the program. Read it fully before your first session, and re-read it whenever a hip or the groin feels off. The failure mode of mobility training is not a dramatic pop — it's forcing range a joint isn't ready for, or overloading the groin at length. No high-step is worth an irritated hip or a strained groin.**

### The core principle: stretch is not pinch

The single most important distinction in this entire program is between two very different sensations:

- **A muscular STRETCH** — a broad, tolerable, "pulling" or "lengthening" feeling in the *belly of a muscle* (inner thigh, hamstring, glute). It eases as you breathe and warm up. **This is normal, safe, and the target.** 🟢
- **A joint PINCH or PAIN** — a **sharp, pointed, or "blocked" sensation deep in the front or crease of the hip**, often felt as *bone-on-bone* rather than muscle. It does not ease with breathing; it worsens as you push deeper. **This is a stop signal** — it can indicate impingement or that you've hit a bony/structural limit that force will only aggravate. 🔴

If you feel a pinch, **back out of the range until it disappears** and work there instead. You cannot stretch your way through a bony block; you can only irritate the joint. When in doubt, treat a sharp deep-hip sensation as a pinch and back off.

### Rules that keep the tissue safe

- **Never force cold or ballistic range.** Always warm up fully (§5). No bouncing, no jerking, no "pulsing" hard into a stretch. End range is approached **slowly and under control**, every time.
- **Progress range gradually.** Add a little depth *or* a little load at a time — never both, never a big jump. Mobility gained slowly is kept; mobility forced is strained.
- **Respect the groin.** The **adductors** are the most strain-prone tissue in this program. Aggressive end-range loading (deep wide Cossacks, long-lever Copenhagens, forced pancakes) is exactly how a groin strain happens. Introduce adductor loading gently, progress it conservatively, and treat any groin twinge as a stop.
- **Depth before weight, always.** In loaded work, never add load at the expense of control through the range. A controlled shallower rep beats a deep rep you're collapsing into.
- **Frequency, not intensity.** This program works through frequent gentle exposure, not occasional hard forcing. If you find yourself gritting your teeth into a stretch, you've mistaken this for a strength session.

### Warning signs — what a problem feels like

- A **sharp pinch deep in the front/crease of the hip** at end range (see above) — the classic sign you're driving into a joint's structural limit, possibly impingement.
- **Groin/inner-thigh pain** during or the day after wide or loaded end-range work — especially a sharp or localized ache, as opposed to broad muscular soreness. A warning of adductor strain.
- **Clicking, catching, or a "blocked" feeling** in the hip that's painful (painless clicks are usually benign; *painful* ones are not).
- **Low-back or SI-joint pain** creeping in — often a sign you're forcing range from the spine/pelvis instead of the hip, or over-hinging with a rounded back.
- Soreness that **takes longer each session to settle**, or a position that feels *worse*, not better, week over week.

**A hip or groin niggle is a yellow flag — treat it seriously and early.** Hip and groin irritation, once established, can nag for months and is far easier to prevent than to resolve.

### The green / amber / red pain rule

Use this every session — and within every stretch — to decide whether to continue, modify, or stop:

| Signal | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 🟢 **Green** | A broad, tolerable **muscular stretch** in the muscle belly (inner thigh, hamstring, glute) that eases with breathing and warm-up. No joint sensation. | Train as prescribed. This is the target sensation. |
| 🟡 **Amber** | Mild ache, stiffness, or "awareness" in a hip, the groin, or the low back that isn't sharp and settles quickly; a position that feels tighter than usual; day-after soreness that lingers a little. | Reduce depth and load, keep everything gentle, cut the loaded/active-flexibility work, do only the daily mobility. Do **not** progress. Reassess next session; if it persists, treat as red. |
| 🔴 **Red** | A **sharp pinch deep in the hip crease**; sharp or localized **groin pain**; a painful click/catch; low-back or SI pain; any pain that lingers or worsens after sessions or changes how you walk or squat. | **Stop that movement immediately.** Back out to fully pain-free range. No end-range or loaded mobility work until symptom-free. Seek a qualified professional. Do not self-diagnose or push through. |

### Mandatory recovery spacing

- Keep **48+ hours between the loaded Phase 2 sessions** and between hard active-flexibility (PAILs-RAILs) sessions — these are strength stimuli and need recovery.
- The **short daily mobility work is low-intensity and can be near-daily** — but if the hips or groin feel persistently tired, stiff, or achy across several days, that's under-recovery: keep it gentle or take a day fully off.
- Don't stack aggressive end-range loading on top of a heavy leg day (squats, deadlifts, a big slab/high-step-heavy climbing session) — that's a lot of connective-tissue load on the same joints.

### A brief note on returning to loading

> This program is for healthy, pain-free hips, groins, and low backs and is **not** a rehabilitation protocol. If you've had a groin strain, hip impingement (FAI), a labral issue, hip or low-back injury, or any joint problem, return to loaded end-range mobility work **only** when a qualified professional has cleared you and you are symptom-free. When you do return, restart at **Phase 1** (gentle, unloaded range only), progress even more conservatively than usual, keep everything well inside a pinch-free range, and treat any recurrence as an immediate stop. Genuine hip or groin rehab is individual and progressive and belongs with a physiotherapist — not with a generic download.

## 9. Testing & Benchmarking

A standardized set of range tests at the **start** and **end** of the block turns "I feel a bit bendier" into numbers and photos you can trust. Keep every variable identical across both tests: same warm-up, same surface, same measuring method, same camera angle and distance, same time of day, same rested state.

> **Prerequisite:** only test after a complete warm-up (§5), and only if the hips feel warm and open with no pinch. If anything feels off — a catch, a pinch, a stiff groin — postpone. A test is never worth forcing a cold or cranky hip.

Run **all four** tests and record each. Filming each from a fixed angle makes small, real changes visible that a tape measure alone might miss.

**Test A — Deep-squat quality.**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Task | From standing, feet at a fixed stance width, squat as **deep as you can with control** — heels down, chest reasonably upright |
| Score | Record **depth (hip crease relative to knee height)**, whether heels stay down, and how wide the knees track. Film from the front and side |
| Conditions | Same stance width, footwear (or barefoot), and camera position at start and re-test |

**Test B — Seated straddle / pancake reach.**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Task | Sit with legs at a **fixed, marked width**; hinge forward from the hips with a long spine and reach as far as control allows |
| Score | Measure **how far a fixed point (e.g., sternum or fingertips) travels forward**, or the reach distance to a tape on the floor. Note the leg width used |
| Conditions | Identical leg width, spine cue, and measuring point at start and re-test |

**Test C — 90-90 rotation.**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Task | Sit in 90-90; note how upright you can sit and how far you can hinge forward over the front shin **without a pinch** — test both sides |
| Score | Record **forward-lean depth** (film from the side) and any left/right asymmetry |
| Conditions | Same setup and camera angle both sides, at start and re-test |

**Test D — Standardized max high-step (on the wall).**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Task | Standing square to a wall/board with hands at a **fixed height**, place a foot on the **highest hold/mark you can load and control**, and rock weight onto it |
| Score | The **height of the highest foothold you can plant and stand on** with control (measure from the floor, or record the hold/mark used) |
| Conditions | Same wall, same hand height, same starting stance at start and re-test — this is the most climbing-specific benchmark |

**Record** each score plus how each felt. **Re-test in Week 6** under identical conditions. More depth, more reach, a more upright 90-90, or a higher usable high-step is your objective result. No change is still useful data — it tells you whether to repeat the block, train more frequently, or seek guidance if something isn't moving.

> **A test is a controlled effort, not a max-out.** Take each test to your *controlled, pinch-free* limit — never force past a pinch to score a bigger number. A benchmark forced past a joint's limit is both invalid and a way to get hurt.

## 10. Recovery Essentials

Mobility adaptations — both the range and the strength at range — are built between sessions, not during the stretch. Brief, evidence-aligned basics:

- **Frequency over intensity.** For mobility specifically, *consistent, gentle, frequent* exposure drives more adaptation than occasional hard sessions — and it's also gentler on the tissue. The daily short work is the engine; protect the habit.
- **Sleep.** The highest-leverage recovery tool. Aim for **7–9 hours** of quality sleep. Tissue remodelling and adaptation depend on it; under-sleeping blunts adaptation and raises injury risk, and a tired body defends its range more (you'll feel stiffer).
- **Session spacing for loaded work.** Keep **48+ hours** between the loaded Phase 2 sessions and hard active-flexibility sessions — those are strength stimuli and need real recovery even though they feel like "just stretching."
- **Fuel and protein.** The loaded strength-through-range work is muscle-building work; support tissue repair with adequate total energy and **protein** (a common general guideline for active people is roughly **1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight per day**, spread across meals). This is general educational information, not individualized dietary advice.
- **Hydration.** Well-hydrated connective tissue and muscle move and tolerate range better; be well-hydrated for sessions.
- **Gentle movement on off days.** Light walking, easy CARs, and warm easy range keep hips feeling open between harder sessions — mobility likes to be moved.
- **Listen to trend, not just the day.** A position that's tightening week over week, a lingering groin ache, or a hip that's slow to warm up means back off. Connective-tissue and joint irritation accumulate quietly.

## 11. After Week 6

Finish the block, bank the gains — and understand the one thing that separates mobility from strength: **mobility must be maintained, or it regresses.** Unlike the relatively durable gains of a strength block, range and control at range fade if you stop working them. How you follow up should reflect that.

**Option A — Maintain (the default, and non-negotiable if you want to keep your range).** Keep the **daily short mobility work** (CARs + 2–3 key positions, 8–12 minutes) as a permanent habit, and keep **one loaded/active session per week** to preserve strength at range. This is not optional garnish — it is the price of keeping the high-step you earned. Fold the CARs and key positions into your climbing warm-up so they cost you almost nothing. **Mobility left untrained slowly slides back toward where you started.**

**Option B — Keep applying it on the wall.** Now that the range exists, keep *using* it: deliberately choose high feet, drop-knees, and flags in your regular climbing so the movements become reflexive and the range stays claimed. Applying it is itself a form of maintenance.

**Option C — Repeat the block, progressed.** After a lighter week, re-run the 6 weeks from your **new** baseline (re-test numbers). Progress by exploring a little more depth, adding a little load to the strength-through-range work, or advancing the active-flexibility protocols — but keep the same patient, one-variable-at-a-time, pinch-free discipline. Chase control, not just a deeper number.

> Whichever you choose, keep the daily mobility habit and the warm-up year-round — the hips (and the groin) will thank you. And re-test periodically under the same standard: with mobility especially, the honest measure of whether your training is working — or whether you've quietly let it slip — is the benchmark, retested.

## 12. Sources & Further Reading

Genuine, well-known work in mobility, strength-and-conditioning, and climbing training. Consult the original sources directly; the notes below are plain-language pointers, not exact quotations or specific numeric claims.

- **Dave MacLeod, *9 out of 10 Climbers Make the Same Mistakes* and *Make or Break*** — accessible, well-regarded books on climbing movement, technique, body positioning, and injury prevention, including the role of flexibility and hip mobility in efficient movement (high-steps, drop-knees, flagging).
- **Eric Hörst, *Training for Climbing* and the Training4Climbing resources** — widely used practical guides to climbing training, including movement skill, flexibility, warm-ups, and periodized programming.
- **General strength-and-conditioning texts on flexibility and range of motion** (e.g., the mobility/flexibility chapters of standard NSCA-style *Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning* material) — for the distinction between passive and active range, the principle of training strength at end range, and safe stretching practice.
- **Research and practitioner literature on end-range strength and loaded stretching** — the broad, well-established principle that combining loading with range (eccentric and isometric work at long muscle lengths, contract-relax methods) builds *usable*, controllable range more effectively than passive stretching alone. Functional-range and joint-CARs approaches (as popularized in the mobility-training community) apply this idea practically; use them within the pain-free, pinch-free cautions in this manual.
- **Copenhagen adductor and groin-strengthening research** (e.g., the body of sports-medicine work on adductor-strengthening protocols for groin-injury prevention in field-sport athletes) — the basis for conservative, progressive adductor loading and its protective value.
- **Volker Schöffl and colleagues** — the leading body of clinical research on climbing injuries; useful background on lower-limb and overuse injury patterns in climbers and on training conservatively.
- **PAR-Q+ (2023) and the ePARmed-X+, © the PAR-Q+ Collaboration** — the physical-activity readiness screening referenced throughout this program.
- **CSEP (Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology)** — evidence-based physical activity, resistance-training, and recovery guidance underpinning the general training and recovery principles here.

> These are starting points for going deeper — not a claim that this program reproduces any specific study's protocol. Where you see a number in this manual, treat it as an educational default to individualize, not a research finding.
