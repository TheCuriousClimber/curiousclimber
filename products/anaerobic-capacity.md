## 1. Overview

**Anaerobic capacity** — often called **power-endurance** by climbers — is the ability to keep pulling hard through a long, sustained, forearm-intensive sequence: to *tolerate* the pump, keep clearing it as it builds, and recover enough between hard sections to finish the route. It is the quality that decides whether you cruise the top of a pumpy 25-move pitch or peel off the last hard clip with your forearms lit up and unresponsive.

This program develops that quality through **structured, progressive interval and continuous climbing**, layered from a local-endurance base up through work-capacity intervals to route-specific peaking. Over six weeks it moves you through three deliberate phases — aerobic/local-endurance base, anaerobic-capacity intervals, and power-endurance peaking — so that your ability to *supply and clear metabolites* is built before you ask the system to buffer maximal, repeated pump.

**How this differs from its siblings.** This is **not** a maximal finger-strength program (that is *Contact Strength & Finger Power*, which trains how *hard* your fingers can pull in a brief effort). Nor is it a pure aerobic base plan. Anaerobic capacity sits in the middle: it trains how *long and how repeatedly* you can express near-hard climbing before the forearms shut down. Think of finger strength as your ceiling, aerobic base as your foundation, and power-endurance as the bridge that lets you use your strength for the *whole* route, not just the first few moves.

**Who it's for:** intermediate-and-up climbers with an existing strength and aerobic base who keep getting shut down by the pump on long routes, linked boulder problems, or sustained gym pitches. It is scalable through wall angle, hold size, move density, and work:rest ratio.

**Expected outcome (with consistent execution and adequate recovery):** a measurably higher tolerance for sustained forearm loading, faster recovery between hard sections, a later and more manageable pump onset, and a repeatable framework you can re-run before a route season. Endurance qualities respond faster than maximal strength — but they also detrain faster, so they are best built in a focused block ahead of when you need them.

> **What this program is *not*.** It is not a rehab plan, not a maximal finger-strength or contact-strength protocol, not a campus/power block, and not a substitute for climbing on real routes. It is one focused power-endurance block that sits *inside* a broader climbing life.

## 2. Prerequisites & Readiness

Power-endurance training is high in **cumulative volume**. Its injury risk is not the single dramatic pulley pop of max hangboarding — it is the slow accumulation of forearm and elbow tendon load across many pumpy sessions. Gating who runs this, and on what base, is the most important safety decision in the program.

### Minimum training age before dedicated power-endurance work

| Criterion | Minimum standard before starting |
|---|---|
| **Consistent climbing history** | At least **12 months** of regular climbing (2+ sessions/week). High-volume forearm loading needs tissue that has already adapted to regular climbing. |
| **Grade base** | Comfortably climbing routes around **5.10+–5.11 / V3–V4** or harder, and able to climb continuously for several minutes on moderate terrain. This signals your forearms already tolerate sustained loading. |
| **Strength base** | An existing base of **maximal strength** (ideally a prior strength/finger block, or equivalent climbing). Power-endurance expresses strength you already have — building it on a weak base just makes you good at climbing easy moves for a long time. |
| **Aerobic base** | Able to complete easy continuous ("ARC"-style) climbing for **15–20 minutes** without a shutdown pump. If you can't yet sustain easy movement, start with the base phase and progress slowly. |
| **Symptom-free** | No current finger, hand, wrist, elbow, or shoulder pain or "niggles" — especially no medial or lateral **elbow tendinopathy**, which high forearm volume readily aggravates. |
| **Age** | Skeletally mature. High-volume finger and forearm loading is **not** appropriate for still-growing adolescents. |

### Readiness reminder (PAR-Q)

Before any new training block, complete a **Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q+)**. If you answer "yes" to any item — or you are over 40 and inactive, pregnant/postpartum, or have a heart, blood-pressure, metabolic, joint, or bone condition — clear vigorous exercise with a physician or qualified professional first.

### Who should NOT run this yet

> **Do not start this program if you:**
> - Have less than a year of consistent climbing, or have not yet built a strength and aerobic base.
> - Are an adolescent who has not finished growing.
> - Have **any** current or recent elbow, forearm, finger, pulley, wrist, or shoulder injury or pain — even mild. Power-endurance work is high-volume forearm loading; it aggravates exactly the tissues that need to be quiet to heal. Seek a qualified professional; return to loading only when cleared and symptom-free.
> - Are already deep in a high-volume climbing peak or competition prep, or in an unusually stressful, sleep-deprived life period. This block adds meaningful systemic and tissue fatigue.
> - Have a history of elbow tendinopathy that flares with volume — build tolerance more gradually, get it assessed first, and treat any early forearm/elbow ache as a stop signal.
>
> There is no rush. Chronic forearm or elbow overuse can nag for months. A conservative ramp costs weeks.

## 3. The Science in Plain Language

### What "power-endurance" actually trains

When you climb a long, pumpy route, your forearm muscles contract hard and often. Those contractions are powered by three overlapping energy systems, and power-endurance is fundamentally about how well you can run — and recover — the middle one.

### The three energy systems (the legible version)

- **ATP-PCr (the immediate system).** For the first several seconds of a hard effort, muscles run on stored **ATP** and **phosphocreatine (PCr)**. This is what powers a boulder crux or a single explosive move. It's potent but tiny — it's spent in roughly the first 6–10 seconds of near-maximal effort and needs rest to recharge.
- **Anaerobic glycolysis (the capacity system — this program's main target).** For efforts lasting from several seconds up to a couple of minutes — exactly the timescale of a sustained hard route section — muscles break down carbohydrate *without* enough oxygen to keep up. This produces energy fast, but it also generates metabolic by-products (notably **hydrogen ions, H⁺**, which make the muscle more acidic). This is the system that gets you *through* the pumpy middle of a route, and its by-products are what create "the pump."
- **Aerobic contribution (the recovery system).** With oxygen, muscles produce energy cleanly and help *clear* the by-products of glycolysis. A better aerobic base means you clear the pump faster on rests, recover between hard sections, and lean on the anaerobic system less for a given pace. This is why endurance climbers train an aerobic base *first* — it's the engine that lets the anaerobic capacity be used and recovered.

> **The one-sentence version:** finger strength decides how hard a single move can be; anaerobic capacity decides how many hard moves you can *string together* before the forearm chemistry forces you off.

### What "the pump" actually is

The pump is not just fatigue — it's a specific, partly mechanical phenomenon in the forearm:

- **Forearm occlusion.** Gripping hard is a sustained (isometric) contraction. When the forearm muscle contracts near-maximally, its own internal pressure **squeezes shut the small blood vessels** running through it. Fresh, oxygenated blood can't get in and metabolite-laden blood can't get out. The harder and more continuously you grip, the more blood flow is choked off.
- **Metabolite and H⁺ accumulation.** With flow occluded and glycolysis running hard, by-products — H⁺ and others — build up in the muscle faster than they can be cleared. The muscle becomes acidic, swells, stiffens, and progressively loses its ability to contract. That tight, swollen, "can't-close-my-hand" feeling is the pump.
- **Why rests matter.** Shaking out on a jug or a rest stance lets the forearm relax, blood flow returns, oxygen arrives, and the by-products get flushed and buffered. Training improves both *how much* by-product you can tolerate and *how fast* you clear it on those rests.

### Capacity vs. power (they're different)

Two related but distinct qualities live under "power-endurance," and this program deliberately trains both, in order:

- **Anaerobic capacity** — the *total amount* of hard, sub-maximal work you can do before shutting down. Built with **higher-volume interval and continuous work**. This is the base of power-endurance, and Weeks 1–4 emphasize it.
- **Anaerobic power** — the *intensity* you can sustain while still buffering the pump: hard climbing, near your limit, held together across a route. Built with **shorter, harder, more route-specific intervals**. Weeks 5–6 sharpen this on top of the capacity base.

### Buffering and "lactate tolerance"

Part of the adaptation to this training is chemical: the muscle gets better at **buffering** the acidity that hard glycolysis produces, and the whole system gets better at clearing and reusing by-products. Climbers often call this "lactate tolerance" — the trained ability to keep performing while deeply pumped, and to recover from that state faster. (Lactate itself is largely a fuel and a marker, not the villain; the performance-limiting story is more about acidity, occlusion, and by-product accumulation.) This is a real, trainable adaptation, and it's why the same route that shut you down in Week 1 can feel manageable by Week 6.

### Why long routes tax this system specifically

A boulder problem is over before glycolysis becomes the limiter. A long route is different: it demands **repeated, sustained, sub-maximal forearm contractions with imperfect rests**, for minutes at a time. That is precisely the anaerobic-glycolytic timescale — so the route is won or lost on how much pump you can generate-and-clear, not on your single-move maximum.

> **The takeaway:** you build a bigger, more resilient "pump budget" by training the exact demand — sustained and repeated hard climbing — while an aerobic base underneath lets you refill that budget faster between efforts.

## 4. Equipment Needed

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **A wall you can climb continuously on** | A **circuit board, spray wall, systems board**, or any wall that lets you climb long, linked sequences and **lap** routes/problems without long resets. Continuity is the stimulus — you need to keep moving. |
| **A range of accessible grades** | You must be able to dial difficulty finely: easy terrain for base/ARC work, moderate for intervals, and hard-but-doable sequences for peaking. A gym with many routes/problems across grades, or an adjustable board, is ideal. |
| **Auto-belay or a reliable belay partner** *(for rope work)* | For lapping routes and redpoint intervals on a rope. An auto-belay makes continuous lapping easy and repeatable. |
| **A moderate hangboard** | For **endurance repeaters** — comfortable, positive edges (~20 mm+). This program uses the board for *endurance*, not max hangs, so you don't need tiny edges. |
| **A reliable interval timer** | An app or watch that clearly signals work and rest windows. Work:rest timing *is* the prescription in this program — you must be able to hit it consistently. |
| **A logbook or app** | Record grade/holds, number of laps or moves, work:rest, RPE, pump level, and recovery. Endurance progress is invisible without a log to compare weeks. |
| **Chalk & good skin care kit** | High-volume climbing is hard on skin. File, moisturize, and manage tips — a split tip ends a session as surely as a pump does. |
| **A watch/heart-rate option** *(optional)* | Useful for pacing base/ARC work by keeping effort genuinely easy. Not required. |

> **Setup note.** The single most useful asset is **a way to control difficulty precisely and lap it repeatedly** — a circuit or board where you can pick sequences of a known, tunable difficulty and climb them back-to-back. Everything in the schedule below is built around that.

## 5. The Warm-Up Protocol (Every Session)

Never launch into pumpy intervals cold. A full warm-up takes about 15–20 minutes and, for this quality, has a specific job: it must not only prepare the tissue but **prime the pump system** so your first hard interval isn't a shock. It has three stages: raise the pulse, mobilize and activate, then progressively ramp intensity and continuity.

### Stage 1 — Pulse raise (3–5 min)
Get blood moving and body temperature up: easy cardio (skipping, brisk walk/jog, jumping jacks) or a few minutes of very easy traversing. You want to feel warm, not tired. For an endurance session, a slightly longer pulse raise than a strength session is helpful — you're waking up the circulatory system you're about to lean on.

### Stage 2 — Mobility & activation (3–5 min)
- Wrist circles and gentle wrist flexion/extension stretches.
- Finger flexion/extension: make fists and fully open the hands, 15–20 times; splay fingers wide.
- Shoulder and scapular activation: band pull-aparts, scapular pulls / shrugs on the bar, a few slow scap pull-ups.
- Light forearm/wrist **extensor** activation (light band wrist curls or rubber-band finger extensions) to wake the antagonists — doubly important in a high-volume block, where the extensors and elbow tendons take a beating.

### Stage 3 — Progressive "pump-priming" ramp (8–10 min)
This is the endurance-specific part. Rather than the short, heavy ramp of a strength session, you build **intensity *and* continuity** gradually — get a light, controllable pump going and clear it, so the system is primed rather than ambushed.

| Ramp step | Terrain / effort | Duration | Rest after |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Very easy traversing or big holds, continuous, ~40% effort | 2–3 min | Shake out ~1 min |
| 2 | Easy climbing, continuous, ~50% effort — a light pump appears | 2–3 min | Shake out fully (~2 min) |
| 3 | Moderate — a few short sequences at ~65–70% effort, clearing between | 3–4 short bursts | ~1–2 min each |
| 4 | One near-working interval at ~80% of the day's target intensity | 1 short interval | Full recovery (3–5 min) before work sets |
| *(then)* | Begin the session's prescribed work | — | — |

> **Warm-up rule.** The first working interval should feel *hard but clean*, not like a cold shock. If your forearms feel oddly tight, flat, or achy (as opposed to a normal light pump) during the ramp — or an elbow or finger complains — treat it as information. Reduce the day's intensity or stop. A warm-up that doesn't come around cleanly is a reason to back off, not to push through.

## 6. The 6-Week Periodized Schedule

### Phase logic at a glance

| Phase | Weeks | Primary goal | Loading character | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1 — Aerobic / local-endurance base** | 1–2 | Build capillary/aerobic base, forearm work capacity, and pump-clearing ability | High-**volume**, low-**intensity** continuous climbing ("ARC"-style), long sustained efforts | You can only *use and recover* anaerobic capacity if the aerobic engine underneath it can clear the pump. This phase also conditions the tissue for the harder weeks. |
| **Phase 2 — Anaerobic capacity** | 3–4 | Raise total hard-work capacity and buffering / pump tolerance | Interval work: linked boulders / 4×4-style sets and endurance repeaters, defined work:rest | Repeated hard-but-submaximal intervals train the glycolytic system to produce, tolerate, and clear more by-product. |
| **Phase 3 — Power-endurance peaking** | 5–6 | Sustain near-limit intensity across a route; peak, then taper | Harder, more route-specific intervals (redpoint intervals); lower total volume; Week-6 taper/deload | Convert raw capacity into the specific ability to hold hard climbing together on a route, then shed fatigue so it shows up. |

**Sessions per week:** **2–3 sessions**, depending on phase. In Phase 1 (base), up to **3** lower-intensity sessions are fine because intensity is low. In Phases 2–3, keep it to **2 hard sessions** separated by at least **48 hours** (e.g., Tue/Fri), because the intensity — and the tissue cost — is much higher.

> **Fit around climbing, don't stack on top of it.** These sessions *are* your hard climbing for the week in Phases 2–3 — don't also do a maximal bouldering session on top. Do the intervals when reasonably fresh. Keep an eye on total forearm and elbow *exposure*: this block is defined by volume, and volume is exactly what overuses tendons. If a week is unusually stressful or under-slept, take the lighter option or an extra rest day.

### Week 1 — Phase 1: Aerobic / local-endurance base

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Base A | Full warm-up → **ARC / continuous climbing** (see §7), long and easy → light supportive pulling → antagonist/prehab |
| *(48h+ rest, or an easy day)* | | |
| Day 2 | Base B | Full warm-up → **ARC / continuous climbing**, matched → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 3 *(optional)* | Base C | Full warm-up → shorter **ARC** block **or** easy volume climbing → antagonist/prehab |
| Other days | Climb / rest | Easy-to-moderate climbing OK; avoid maximal, pumpy, or crimp-heavy sessions. At least 2 full rest days. |

**Focus:** long, continuous, *easy* climbing that keeps a faint pump without ever shutting the forearms down (RPE ~4–5). Establish your baseline capacity test at the start of the week (see §9). Resist the urge to climb hard — this phase is deliberately unglamorous.

### Week 2 — Phase 1: Base (progressed)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Base A | Warm-up → **ARC**, +2–5 min total continuous time or +1 lap vs. Week 1 → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 2 | Base B | Warm-up → **ARC**, matched → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 3 *(optional)* | Base C | Warm-up → easy volume or a light **ARC** block → antagonist/prehab |

**Progression:** add duration/continuity, **not** intensity — extend total time-on-wall or add a lap; keep it easy (RPE ~5). If Week 1 left forearms or elbows achy beyond a normal pleasant fatigue, repeat Week 1 volume instead.

### Week 3 — Phase 2: Anaerobic capacity

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Capacity A | Thorough warm-up → **Linked-boulder / 4×4 intervals** (see §7) → optional light **endurance repeaters** → antagonist/prehab |
| *(48h+ rest)* | | |
| Day 2 | Capacity B | Warm-up → **Interval circuit** (defined work:rest, see §7) → antagonist/prehab |
| Other days | Easy climb / rest | Keep other climbing easy. At least 2 full rest days. Skip separate hard bouldering. |

**Shift:** intensity rises, and the work becomes intervals. Climb hard-but-submaximal sequences, generate a real pump, take defined rests, and repeat. Target RPE ~7–8 across a set with the *last* interval genuinely hard but completable. Never grind to a full-body failure that leaves you unable to hold the position.

### Week 4 — Phase 2: Anaerobic capacity (progressed)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Capacity A | Warm-up → **Linked-boulder / 4×4 intervals**, +1 set **or** slightly harder problems vs. Week 3 → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 2 | Capacity B | Warm-up → **Interval circuit**, +1 rep per set **or** modestly shortened rest vs. Week 3 → antagonist/prehab |

**Progression:** add *one* variable at a time — one more set, slightly harder sequences, **or** a modestly tighter work:rest — never several at once. If Week 3 left you flat or the forearms/elbows achy, hold Week 3's numbers. Maintaining clean, hard interval work is a win.

### Week 5 — Phase 3: Power-endurance peaking

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Peak A | Warm-up → **Route / redpoint intervals** (see §7) on a hard, route-length sequence → antagonist/prehab |
| *(48h+ rest)* | | |
| Day 2 | Peak B | Warm-up → **Route / redpoint intervals** on a *different* route-length sequence, **or** short hard **linked-boulder** sets → antagonist/prehab |

**Focus:** convert capacity into route-specific power-endurance. Climb near-limit, route-length efforts with substantial rest between, so each effort is high quality. Total volume comes *down* from Week 4; intensity and specificity go *up*. Do the hard efforts while fresh; stop while you can still climb well.

### Week 6 — Phase 3: Peak, taper & re-test

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Sharpen | Light-to-moderate warm-up + **1–2 short, sharp route intervals** at peak intensity to stay primed. **Low volume. No pump-to-failure grinding.** |
| *(2–3 days rest)* | | Rest, eat, and sleep well. This is the taper — accumulated fatigue drops, capacity surfaces. |
| Day 2 | **Re-test** | Full warm-up → repeat the **standardized capacity test** from §9 under the same conditions → record. Then a couple of easy climbing days. |

> **Week 6 is a deload built into the plan.** Do not add volume this week. High-volume endurance blocks generate a lot of cumulative fatigue; the point of the taper is to *shed* it so the capacity you built in Weeks 1–5 shows up in the re-test — and so you finish the block fresh and route-ready, not frayed.

### Auto-regulation rule (all weeks)

Prescriptions are targets, not orders. Each session, gate intensity and volume on how the warm-up ramp felt and your recent sleep/stress:
- **Feels strong, pump clears normally, elbows quiet:** train the prescribed work (progress if scheduled).
- **Feels flat / under-recovered / pump lingers oddly:** hold the load, cut a set, lengthen rests, or drop to the easier option.
- **Any forearm-tendon, elbow, or finger pain, or the ramp feels "off":** stop. Rest, reassess next session. See §8.

## 7. Detailed Prescriptions by Method

Intensity in this program is gauged mostly by **RPE and pump level** on warmed-up forearms, and — crucially — by **work:rest ratios**, which are as much the prescription as the climbing itself. "Hard-but-submaximal" means you could complete the interval with good form; it does not mean climbing to a falling-off failure.

> **How to read effort here:** RPE 7–8 ≈ hard, pumped, but in control — you finish the interval, you don't fall off it. **We do not train endurance intervals to repeated full failure.** Occasional near-failure on a peaking effort is fine; a whole session of falling-off-pumped is how you overreach and overuse tissue.

### Method 1 — ARC / continuous climbing (Phase 1: aerobic & local-endurance base)

Long, continuous, *easy* climbing that keeps a light, sustainable pump — builds the aerobic base and pump-clearing capacity. "ARC" = Aerobic Restoration and Capacity.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Terrain | Easy, big holds / low-angle or vertical; movement you can sustain indefinitely |
| Effort | **Low — RPE 4–5.** A faint, steady pump you can hold without shutting down. If you get shut-down pumped, it's too hard |
| Work interval | **Continuous climbing for 10–20 min per rep** (build toward the longer end over Phase 1) |
| Reps (sets) | 2–3 continuous blocks per session |
| Rest between blocks | Full recovery — **~5 min**, until the pump fully clears |
| Continuity | Keep moving; use easy sections or big holds to "rest" without stopping, rather than getting off the wall |
| Progression | Wk 1 → Wk 2: add total continuous time or one block; keep effort easy (do **not** make it harder) |

### Method 2 — Endurance repeaters (Phase 1–2: forearm-specific local endurance)

Hangboard repeaters used for **endurance**, not strength — longer total work at a moderate, sustainable intensity on comfortable holds.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Grip / edge | Half-crimp or open-hand on a **comfortable ~20 mm+ edge** |
| Work : rest per rep | **7 s hang / 3 s rest** |
| Reps per set | 6–10 reps (build the number over the block) |
| Sets | 3–6 sets |
| Intensity | **Moderate — RPE 6–7, bodyweight or lightly assisted.** The pump builds across a set; the last rep is hard but clean |
| Rest between sets | **1–2 minutes** (shorter than strength repeaters — the incomplete recovery is part of the endurance stimulus) |
| Progression | Add reps per set, then sets; keep it sustainable, never a maximal grind |

### Method 3 — Linked-boulder / 4×4 intervals (Phase 2: anaerobic capacity)

The classic capacity builder: climb several boulder problems back-to-back to force a deep pump, rest, and repeat. "4×4" = four problems, four times through.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Structure | Pick **4 problems** you can climb cleanly *when fresh* — moderate, **not** limit (e.g., a few grades below your max). Climb all 4 back-to-back with minimal rest = **one set** |
| Work per set | ~**3–4 minutes** of near-continuous climbing (the 4 linked problems) |
| Sets | **4 sets** (the second "4") — start with 3 in Wk 3 if new to this |
| Intensity | **Hard-but-submaximal — RPE 7–8.** You should be deeply pumped by the end of each set but able to complete the moves |
| Work : rest ratio | Roughly **1:1 to 1:2** — e.g., ~3–4 min work : ~4–6 min rest between sets |
| Rest between sets | **~4–6 minutes** (enough to partly, not fully, clear the pump) |
| Regression / progression | Easier: bigger holds, fewer problems, longer rest. Harder (Wk 4): +1 set, harder problems, **or** shorter rest — one change at a time |

### Method 4 — Interval circuit (Phase 2: anaerobic capacity, work:rest focus)

A route-length continuous sequence on a circuit/spray wall/auto-belay, climbed as timed on:off intervals — the most controllable way to dial the work:rest stimulus.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Terrain | A continuous circuit or lapped route of **route length** (~15–30 moves) at a moderate, sustained difficulty |
| Work interval | **Climb ~2–3 minutes continuously** (one long lap or linked laps) at RPE 7–8 |
| Reps | **4–6 intervals** per session |
| Work : rest ratio | **~1:1** (e.g., 2–3 min on : 2–3 min off) — tighten toward the harder end across Phase 2 |
| Rest between reps | Equal to work time (see ratio); shake out and clear partially |
| Intensity | Hard, pumped, completable — not falling off every rep |
| Progression | Wk 3 → Wk 4: add one interval **or** modestly shorten the rest (tighten the ratio); not both |

### Method 5 — Route / redpoint intervals (Phase 3: power-endurance peaking)

Near-limit, route-length efforts with generous rest — the most specific method, trains sustaining *hard* climbing across a whole route.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Terrain | A **hard, route-length sequence** near your redpoint level (sustained, pumpy, but climbable when fresh) |
| Work interval | One full route-length effort — **~2–4 minutes** of hard climbing |
| Reps (efforts) | **3–5 per session** |
| Intensity | **High — RPE 8–9**, route-specific and near-limit; the goal is *quality* hard climbing, not accumulated junk volume |
| Work : rest ratio | **~1:3 to 1:4** — long rests so each effort is high quality |
| Rest between efforts | **~6–10 minutes** (near-full recovery) |
| Progression | Wk 5 → Wk 6: reduce volume, keep intensity; sharpen, don't pile on |

### Method 6 — Supportive strength maintenance

A little heavy strength work keeps the ceiling you built in a prior block from eroding while you train endurance. Kept low-volume; done when fresh, not after a shut-down interval session.

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Intensity | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weighted or bodyweight **pull-ups** | 2–3 × 3–5 | RPE 8 (RIR ~2), crisp, full ROM | 2–3 min |
| **Short max/near-max hangs** *(optional, only if experienced & symptom-free)* | 2–3 × 7–10 s | Heavy but clean half-crimp, ~20 mm edge | 3 min |
| **Ring rows / heavy inverted rows** | 2 × 6–8 | RPE 7–8 | 2 min |

> Keep this **maintenance, not development** — one short dose a week is plenty. Its job is to stop your strength detraining, not to compete with the endurance work for recovery. If it's eating your recovery, cut it.

### Method 7 — Antagonist & prehab (every session, ~8–10 min)

Doubly important in a high-volume block: the sheer amount of gripping loads the elbow and forearm tendons, and antagonist/extensor work is your first line of defense against the tendinopathies this kind of training can provoke. Do at the **end** of every session (fine on rest days too).

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wrist extensor curls** (light dumbbell/band) | 2–3 × 12–15 | Forearm extensors | Slow, full range; key for elbow-tendon balance |
| **Finger extensions** (rubber band around fingertips) | 2–3 × 15–20 | Finger extensors / intrinsics | Balances the heavily worked flexors |
| **Reverse wrist curls / pronation-supination** | 2 × 12–15 | Forearm balance | Light load |
| **Scapular / rotator-cuff work** (band external rotations, scap pull-aparts) | 2–3 × 12–15 | Shoulder health | Protects the whole chain |
| **Elbow-tendon prehab** (e.g., slow wrist extension/flexion loading, "Rice-bucket" work) | 2 × 12–15 | Medial/lateral elbow tendons | Especially valuable given this block's volume |

## 8. Injury Prevention & Tendon Health

> **This is the most important section in the program. Read it fully before your first session, and re-read it whenever the forearms or elbows feel off. The failure mode of endurance training is not a dramatic pop — it's slow, cumulative overuse. No route goal is worth a season of elbow tendinopathy.**

### The core principle: manage cumulative volume

Where a max-strength block's danger is a single overload event, a power-endurance block's danger is **volume** — a lot of forearm and elbow loading, session after session, that can outpace tissue recovery and tip into chronic overuse. Every rule below exists to keep total forearm/elbow exposure inside what your tissues can adapt to:

- **Ramp volume conservatively.** Add one variable at a time (duration, sets, intensity, or tighter rest) — never several. When in doubt, hold a week rather than jumping.
- **Honor the phase order.** Weeks 1–2 build the aerobic base and tissue tolerance that make Weeks 3–6 survivable. Don't skip the base because it feels easy.
- **Respect the 48-hour spacing** between hard (Phase 2–3) sessions, always.
- **Count *all* your forearm loading** — these sessions plus any other climbing plus supportive hangs. It's the total that overuses tendons.

### Overuse injuries this block can provoke

High-volume, pumpy climbing tends to aggravate:

- **Medial elbow tendinopathy ("climber's elbow" / golfer's elbow)** — pain on the *inner* elbow where the wrist/finger flexors attach. The classic overuse injury of high-volume gripping.
- **Lateral elbow tendinopathy (tennis elbow)** — pain on the *outer* elbow, often when the extensors are under-trained relative to the flexors (hence the mandatory antagonist work in §7).
- **A2 pulley aggravation from crimpy volume** — if you run these intervals on small, crimpy holds, you stack a lot of pulley load. **Bias toward bigger, more open holds** for high-volume intervals; save crimping for lower-volume work.
- **Skin failure** — flappers and split tips. Not an "injury" in the tissue sense, but the most common thing that ends an endurance session. Manage it (below).

### Bias holds and grips to protect tissue
- **Use bigger, more positive, more open holds** for your high-volume interval and ARC work. The point is *sustained forearm loading*, not maximal finger tension — you don't need small edges to get pumped.
- **Avoid loading up on small crimps for volume.** Crimpy endurance sessions are how a healthy A2 pulley becomes an irritated one.
- **Vary grip types** across a session so no single tendon or pulley takes all the load.

### Warning signs — what overuse feels like
Overuse announces itself slowly; learn to catch it early:
- A **dull ache or tenderness at the inner or outer elbow**, or along the forearm, that shows up during or after sessions and takes longer each week to settle.
- **Morning stiffness** in the forearm/elbow, or pain gripping a coffee cup or turning a doorknob.
- A pump that **takes much longer than usual to clear**, or forearms that feel chronically tight and tired across days.
- Any **sharp finger/pulley pain** — treat exactly as a strength-block pulley warning: stop immediately (see below).

**A nagging elbow or forearm ache is a yellow flag — treat it seriously and early.** Overuse tendinopathy is far easier to prevent than to resolve, and it can linger for months once established.

### The green / amber / red pain rule
Use this every session to decide whether to train, modify, or stop:

| Signal | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 🟢 **Green** | Normal forearm pump and muscular fatigue. No joint, tendon, elbow, or pulley pain. | Train as prescribed. |
| 🟡 **Amber** | Mild ache/stiffness/"awareness" in an elbow, forearm tendon, or finger that is not sharp and settles quickly; a pump that's slow to clear. | Reduce volume and intensity, use bigger holds, lengthen rests, add extra recovery days. Do **not** progress. Reassess next session; if it persists across sessions, treat as red. |
| 🔴 **Red** | Sharp or localized elbow/forearm/finger pain; a pulley pop; swelling; pain that lingers or worsens after sessions; pain that changes how you grip or persists into daily tasks. | **Stop the session immediately.** No pumpy training until fully symptom-free. Seek a qualified professional. Do not self-diagnose. |

### Mandatory rest & recovery spacing
- **At least 48 hours** between hard (Phase 2–3) sessions — always.
- Keep **at least 2 full rest days** per week during the block.
- Don't stack these intervals on top of maximal bouldering or a heavy finger session — that's a volume overload on the same tissues.
- If forearms/elbows feel persistently tired, stiff, or achy across several days, that's under-recovery — take extra rest. Missing a session costs nothing; a chronic elbow costs a season.

### A brief note on return-to-loading
> This program is for healthy, pain-free forearms, elbows, and fingers and is **not** a rehabilitation protocol. If you've had elbow tendinopathy, a pulley injury, or any forearm overuse issue, return to high-volume loading **only** when a qualified professional has cleared you and you are symptom-free. When you do return, restart at **Phase 1** volumes (or lower), progress even more conservatively than usual, bias toward big open holds, and treat any recurrence of symptoms as an immediate stop. Genuine tendinopathy rehab is individual and progressive and belongs with a physiotherapist — not with a generic download.

## 9. Testing & Benchmarking

A standardized test at the **start** and **end** of the block turns "I think I have more staying power" into a number. Keep every variable identical across both tests: same wall/holds, same grade, same protocol, same warm-up, same time of day, same rested state.

### Standardized capacity test (choose one and keep it fixed)

> **Prerequisite:** only test after a complete warm-up (§5), and only if the pump-priming ramp felt clean. If anything feels off — especially an elbow or finger — postpone. A test is not worth an injury.

**Option A — Continuous-climbing benchmark (recommended for most).**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Terrain | A fixed circuit / route / set of holds at a **moderate, sustained difficulty** you can climb continuously |
| Task | Climb continuously until you must stop (technical failure / can't hold the pump), at a **fixed, controlled pace** |
| Score | **Total time** (or total moves / laps) climbed continuously before stopping |
| Conditions | Same holds, pace, angle, warm-up, and rest state at start and re-test |

**Option B — Boulder-interval benchmark.**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Structure | A fixed set of **4 problems** (fixed grade), linked back-to-back = one set, as in the 4×4 method |
| Task | Complete as many **clean sets** as possible with a fixed rest (e.g., 4 min) between sets, until you can no longer complete a set cleanly |
| Score | **Number of complete clean sets** (and note where the first failure fell) |
| Conditions | Identical problems, rest, and warm-up at start and re-test |

**Option C — Endurance-repeaters-to-failure benchmark.**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Grip / edge | Fixed comfortable ~20 mm+ edge, fixed grip, at bodyweight |
| Task | 7 s on / 3 s off repeaters at a fixed intensity, continued until you can no longer complete a rep cleanly |
| Score | **Total reps (or total time-under-tension)** completed |
| Conditions | Identical edge, grip, and warm-up at start and re-test |

**Record** your score plus how it felt and your pump/recovery notes. **Re-test in Week 6** after the taper, under identical conditions. More time, more sets, or more reps is your objective result. No change is still useful data — it tells you whether to repeat the block, adjust recovery, or change the stimulus.

> **Testing is a genuinely hard, pumpy effort.** Count the initial test as your first loaded session of Week 1, and don't also do a full interval session the same day.

## 10. Recovery Essentials

Endurance adaptations are built during recovery, not during the pump. Brief, evidence-aligned basics:

- **Sleep.** The highest-leverage recovery tool. Aim for **7–9 hours** of quality sleep. Tissue repair, glycogen restoration, and adaptation all depend on it; under-sleeping blunts adaptation and raises overuse-injury risk.
- **Session spacing.** Covered above and worth repeating: **48+ hours** between hard sessions, 2+ rest days per week. High volume needs real recovery even when you feel fine.
- **Fuel the work.** Endurance/glycolytic training draws heavily on **carbohydrate** — under-fuelling flattens pumpy sessions and slows recovery. Eat enough total energy, include adequate carbohydrate around training, and support tissue with adequate **protein** (a common general guideline for active people is roughly **1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight per day**, spread across meals). This is general educational information, not individualized dietary advice.
- **Hydration.** Be well-hydrated for sessions; sustained gripping and long efforts are dehydrating, and hydration supports the circulatory clearing your forearms rely on.
- **Skin.** High-volume climbing destroys skin faster than tissue. File glassy calluses, moisturize, and stop before a hot-spot becomes a flapper — skin, not fitness, ends most endurance sessions.
- **Listen to trend, not just the day.** Persistent fatigue, poor sleep, a pump that won't clear, or lingering elbow/forearm ache across several days means back off. Overuse deficits accumulate silently in tendon.

## 11. After Week 6

Finish the block, bank the gains, and choose a deliberate next step. Endurance qualities detrain relatively quickly, so how you follow up depends on *when you need this fitness*.

**Option A — Maintain (peak for a route season).** Drop to **one power-endurance session per week** (a set of route/redpoint intervals) and go climb the long routes you built this for. Endurance detrains faster than strength, so a weekly maintenance dose matters if your route objectives are ongoing. This is the ideal follow-on if you built this block *for* a trip or season.

**Option B — Take a down-week, then a new focus.** Run a lighter/recovery week (easy climbing, minimal intervals), then pivot to a complementary quality — maximal finger strength (see the sibling *Contact Strength* program) or power — so the forearms get a relative volume break while you raise the ceiling that power-endurance expresses.

**Option C — Repeat the block, progressed.** After at least a **light/recovery week**, re-run the 6 weeks from your **new** baseline (re-test numbers). Progress by climbing the intervals on harder sequences, tightening work:rest, or adding volume in Phase 1 — but keep the same conservative, one-variable-at-a-time ramp discipline, and keep biasing high-volume work toward big open holds. Don't run high-volume pumpy blocks continuously; cycle them with strength, power, and lighter periods across your training year.

> Whichever you choose, keep antagonist/prehab work and warm-ups in your routine year-round — the elbows especially will thank you. And keep testing periodically under the same standard — long-term trend is what tells you your training is working.

## 12. Sources & Further Reading

Genuine, well-known work in climbing science and training. Consult the original sources directly; the notes below are plain-language pointers, not exact quotations or specific numeric claims.

- **Nicolas Fanchini and colleagues** — applied research and writing on **energy systems and endurance training for climbing**, including how aerobic and anaerobic qualities are structured and periodized for route climbers.
- **Rômulo Bertuzzi and colleagues** — research on the **energetics and physiological demands of rock climbing**, including the relative contributions of the aerobic and anaerobic energy systems during climbing.
- **Nikolay Michailov and colleagues** — research on the **physiological determinants of climbing performance and forearm endurance**, including energy-system contributions and local muscular endurance in climbers.
- **Jerónimo Aragón / Eva López-Rivera and colleagues** — applied climbing-science work on **forearm endurance, intermittent forearm loading, and structured hangboard/endurance methods**. (López-Rivera's work also underpins structured hangboarding generally — see the sibling *Contact Strength* program.)
- **Dave MacLeod, *9 out of 10 Climbers Make the Same Mistakes*** and ***Make or Break*** — accessible, well-regarded books on climbing performance, endurance/pump management, and injury prevention, including the elbow and forearm.
- **Eric Hörst, *Training for Climbing* and the Training4Climbing resources** — widely used practical guides to periodized climbing training, including ARC/aerobic-base work, power-endurance intervals (4×4s), and warm-ups.
- **Volker Schöffl and colleagues** — the leading body of clinical research on **climbing injuries**, including forearm and elbow overuse and the epidemiology of climbing-related tendinopathy and pulley injury.
- **Lattice Training and related applied-science practitioners** — publicly available educational material on **endurance assessment, energy-system training, and structured programming** for climbers.
- **PAR-Q+ (2023) and the ePARmed-X+, © the PAR-Q+ Collaboration** — the physical-activity readiness screening referenced throughout this program.
- **CSEP (Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology)** — evidence-based physical activity, resistance-training, and sleep guidance underpinning the general recovery and training principles here.

> These are starting points for going deeper — not a claim that this program reproduces any specific study's protocol. Where you see a number in this manual, treat it as an educational default to individualize, not a research finding.
