## 1. Overview

**Power-endurance for redpoints** is the ability to fire off a short series of *very hard, powerful* moves — and to keep firing them as fatigue sets in — so that a bouldery crux or a short, brutally hard redpoint section doesn't spit you off. It is the quality that decides whether you punch through the four-move crux at two-thirds height with your fingers still snapping shut on the crimps, or whether your power "fades" mid-sequence and you grease off the very move you'd done a dozen times fresh.

This program develops that quality through **short, high-intensity, near-limit climbing** — limit-boulder linkups, short hard power-endurance intervals with generous rest, and redpoint/crux-simulation burns — organized so that maximal recruitment and short-burst power are built first, then trained to *resist fade*, then sharpened onto the specific crux you're trying to send. Over six weeks it moves through three deliberate phases — strength-power base, power-endurance intervals, and redpoint-specific peaking — so that raw power is established before you ask it to hold up under fatigue.

**How this differs from its siblings.** This is the **high-intensity, lower-volume** member of the endurance family, and it is deliberately the *opposite emphasis* from the capacity manual:

- *Contact Strength & Finger Power* trains how **hard a single move** can be — your maximal finger force, the **ceiling**. It answers "can I hold this hold at all?"
- *Anaerobic Capacity for Long Routes* trains **long-route pump tolerance** through **high-volume, lower-intensity** work (ARC, long intervals) — how *long and how repeatedly* you can climb sub-maximal terrain before the forearms shut down. It answers "can I last 25 pumpy moves?"
- **This program** trains **anaerobic power** — short-burst power-endurance. It sits above the capacity work in intensity and below it in volume: how much **near-limit power you can sustain across a short, savage sequence** of roughly **10–45 seconds** before your force output collapses. It answers "can I keep pulling *at my limit* through the crux?"

Put simply: contact strength is your peak force on one move; anaerobic capacity is your staying power on a long pumpy pitch; **anaerobic power is your ability to string together a handful of desperate moves without fading.** Redpoint cruxes are lost to *power fade*, not to long-route pump — and that is exactly what this block targets.

**Who it's for:** intermediate-and-up climbers with an existing max-strength base who keep getting shut down not by the *length* of a route but by a **short, hard crux** — a bouldery section, a powerful roll into a rest, a burly redpoint sequence they can do fresh but blow when it counts. It is scalable through wall angle, hold size, move intensity, and work:rest ratio.

**Expected outcome (with consistent execution and adequate recovery):** a measurably higher ability to sustain hard, powerful moves as fatigue accumulates, a slower and more controllable power fade, a repeatable framework for peaking onto a specific redpoint project, and a clearer sense of how to time your hardest efforts. Power-endurance responds quickly, but it also detrains quickly and carries meaningful tissue cost — so it is best built in a focused, well-recovered block ahead of when you need it.

> **What this program is *not*.** It is not a rehab plan, not a maximal finger-strength or contact-strength protocol, not a long-route aerobic/pump-tolerance plan, and not a substitute for climbing on your actual project. It is one focused anaerobic-power block that sits *inside* a broader climbing life.

## 2. Prerequisites & Readiness

Power-endurance training is high in **intensity and force**. Its injury risk is not the slow, cumulative overuse of a high-volume capacity block — it is the **acute strain** of many near-limit efforts: a pulley loaded hard on a small crimp, a shoulder wrenched on a powerful throw, an elbow tendon shock-loaded on a lock-off. Gating who runs this, and on what base, is the most important safety decision in the program.

### Minimum training age before dedicated anaerobic-power work

| Criterion | Minimum standard before starting |
|---|---|
| **Consistent climbing history** | At least **12–18 months** of regular climbing (2+ sessions/week), including experience with intentional hard bouldering. Near-limit, powerful loading needs tissue and technique that have already adapted to hard climbing. |
| **Grade base** | Comfortably bouldering around **V4–V5** or harder, and redpointing routes with definable cruxes. You need a genuine "limit" to train near — this quality only exists relative to a real max. |
| **Strength base** | An existing base of **maximal finger and pulling strength** (ideally a prior *Contact Strength* block, or equivalent hard climbing). Power-endurance *expresses* strength you already have; training it on a weak ceiling just teaches you to fade at a low absolute force. |
| **Capacity base** | Some existing pump tolerance (a prior *Anaerobic Capacity* block or regular route climbing). You should be able to recover between hard efforts — not shut down after one burst. |
| **Symptom-free** | No current finger, hand, wrist, elbow, or shoulder pain or "niggles" — especially no history of **A2/A4 pulley** strain or shoulder instability, which powerful near-limit moves readily expose. |
| **Age** | Skeletally mature. Near-limit finger and powerful dynamic loading is **not** appropriate for still-growing adolescents. |

### Readiness reminder (PAR-Q)

Before any new training block, complete a **Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q+)**. If you answer "yes" to any item — or you are over 40 and inactive, pregnant/postpartum, or have a heart, blood-pressure, metabolic, joint, or bone condition — clear vigorous exercise with a physician or qualified professional first.

### Who should NOT run this yet

> **Do not start this program if you:**
> - Have less than a year of consistent climbing, or have not yet built a maximal-strength base. Near-limit power work on a shaky strength base is high-risk and low-reward.
> - Are an adolescent who has not finished growing. Growth-plate and developing-pulley loading at near-limit intensity is a serious injury risk.
> - Have **any** current or recent finger, pulley, wrist, elbow, or shoulder injury or pain — even mild. Powerful, near-limit moves load exactly the tissues that must be quiet to heal. Seek a qualified professional; return to loading only when cleared and symptom-free.
> - Are already deep in a high-volume climbing peak or competition prep, or in an unusually stressful, sleep-deprived life period. Near-limit work demands full freshness to be both effective and safe.
> - Have a history of pulley injury that flares with crimpy, powerful moves — get it assessed first, bias toward larger and more open holds, and treat any early finger pain as an immediate stop signal.
>
> There is no rush. A single pulley rupture on a powerful crimp move can cost months. A conservative ramp costs weeks.

## 3. The Science in Plain Language

### What "power-endurance" actually trains here

When you hit a hard, bouldery crux part-way up a route, your muscles have to produce **a lot of force, fast, several times in a row**, while already partly fatigued. Power-endurance in *this* sense is the ability to keep producing near-maximal force across that short, brutal sequence before your output collapses. It is a fundamentally different demand from cruising a long pumpy pitch — and it is powered by a different corner of your energy metabolism.

### The energy systems on the short, hard timescale

- **ATP-PCr (the immediate, high-power system — this program's foundation).** For the first several seconds of an all-out effort, muscles run on stored **ATP** and **phosphocreatine (PCr)**. This is the system behind a boulder crux, an explosive throw, or a hard lock-off. It produces force at the **highest rate of any system**, but the tank is tiny — near-maximal output drains it in roughly the first **6–10 seconds**, and it needs rest (and oxygen) to recharge. Redpoint cruxes live right here.
- **Early anaerobic glycolysis (the bridge — this program's main sustaining target).** When a hard effort runs past those first seconds — say a **10–45-second** crux sequence, or a series of hard bursts with short shakes — the muscle leans on breaking down carbohydrate *fast, without enough oxygen*. This keeps force up beyond the PCr window, but it produces metabolic by-products (notably **hydrogen ions, H⁺**) that acidify the muscle and begin to **degrade its ability to produce force**. The overlap and hand-off between ATP-PCr and early glycolysis is exactly the physiology of a hard redpoint crux — and training it is the core of this block.
- **Aerobic contribution (the recharge system — here it's about *recovery between bursts*).** With oxygen, muscles resynthesize PCr and clear by-products. In *this* program its role is narrower than in the capacity manual: it's what **refills your power tank on the shake before the next hard section**, and what lets you take multiple quality burns in a session. A decent aerobic base means each hard effort starts closer to full power.

> **The one-sentence version:** contact strength decides how hard *one* move can be; anaerobic power decides how many *near-limit* moves you can string together before your force output fades; anaerobic capacity decides how long you can sustain *sub-maximal* climbing before the pump shuts you down.

### Why redpoint cruxes actually fail: power fade, not long-route pump

The failure you're training against here is **specific and different** from the long-route pump:

- **Rate of force development (RFD) collapses under fatigue.** Powerful climbing depends not just on *how much* force you can make but on **how fast** you can make it — snapping onto a crimp, generating a throw. As the ATP-PCr system drains and H⁺ accumulates, your **RFD drops first**: the move that felt snappy fresh now feels slow and heavy, and you can't generate force quickly enough to latch or lock. This is "power fade," and it is why a crux you send easily on a warm burn spits you off on the redpoint go.
- **The crux comes when you're *already* partly fatigued.** On a redpoint, you rarely reach the crux fresh. You arrive with PCr partly spent and some acidity already present — so the crux is really a test of **near-limit force production in a fatigued state**. Training that exact scenario — hard moves *after* a hard approach, or hard bursts with incomplete recovery — is the whole point.
- **It's over before "pump" is the limiter.** A long-route pump is a minutes-long, occlusion-and-metabolite story (that's the capacity manual's domain). A redpoint crux is decided in **seconds to tens of seconds**, on the high-power systems, before glycolytic pump becomes the main limiter. You lose the crux to *fading power and RFD*, not to a slowly filling forearm.

### Forearm occlusion still matters — but the emphasis is different

Gripping hard is a near-maximal isometric contraction, and it still **squeezes shut the forearm's small blood vessels**, slowing the delivery of oxygen and the clearing of by-products. In a *capacity* block, managing that occlusion over many minutes is the whole game. Here, the occlusion matters mostly on a shorter clock: it's why **RFD and power fade so quickly** inside a hard sequence, and why **generous rest between efforts** is non-negotiable in this program — you must let flow return and PCr recharge so each burn is high quality, not junk.

### Capacity vs. power (they're different — and this is the power end)

Two related qualities live under "power-endurance," and this program deliberately sits at the **power** end of the spectrum, the mirror image of the capacity manual:

- **Anaerobic capacity** — the *total amount* of hard, sub-maximal work you can do before shutting down. Built with **higher-volume, lower-intensity** interval and continuous work. (That's the sibling manual.)
- **Anaerobic power** — the *intensity and force* you can sustain while resisting fade: near-limit, powerful climbing held together across a short, hard sequence. Built with **short, high-intensity, near-limit efforts and generous rest** — quality over quantity. **That is this program.**

### What the training actually changes

The adaptations you're chasing here are mostly about **producing and maintaining high force under fatigue**:

- **Better recruitment and RFD under fatigue** — your nervous system learns to fire near-maximally, faster, even when partly cooked.
- **A larger, faster-recharging PCr contribution** and improved buffering of the early H⁺ accumulation — so power holds up for a few more seconds and a few more moves.
- **Faster recovery of power between bursts** — so your second, third, and fourth hard efforts fade less.

This is a real, trainable set of adaptations, and it's why the crux that fades you in Week 1 can feel like it still has *snap* in it by Week 6.

> **The takeaway:** you build a bigger, more fade-resistant "power budget" by training the exact demand — short, near-limit, powerful climbing repeated with quality rest — while enough strength underneath sets the ceiling and enough aerobic base underneath refills the tank between burns.

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **A steep board or wall you can set hard sequences on** | A **spray wall, systems board, or adjustable board** (e.g., a commercial training board) is ideal — it lets you build short, hard, *repeatable* sequences at a known, tunable intensity. Overhanging terrain is where powerful crux climbing lives. |
| **Access to real cruxes / route-length hard sections** | For the peaking phase you need **hard, bouldery route sections** — a project, a hard link on a rope, or a set problem that mimics a crux. A gym with steep routes and definable cruxes, or your actual project, is ideal. |
| **A reliable belay partner or auto-belay** *(for rope burns)* | For redpoint/crux burns on a rope. An auto-belay makes repeat burns on a set line easy and repeatable. |
| **A quality hangboard** *(for supportive max-strength work)* | Comfortable, positive edges (~20 mm) for short **maximal** hangs used to *maintain* the strength ceiling. This block uses the board sparingly and heavily, not for volume. |
| **A reliable interval timer** | An app or watch with clear work/rest signals. In this program the **rest** is as much the prescription as the work — generous, honest rest is what keeps efforts near-limit. |
| **Crash pads / good landing** *(for limit-boulder work)* | Powerful near-limit bouldering means falls. A safe landing is a prerequisite, not an optional extra. |
| **A logbook or app** | Record problems/grades, moves, work:rest, RPE, and — crucially — *which effort you started to fade on*. Power-fade progress is invisible without a log to compare weeks. |
| **Chalk & skin care kit** | Powerful crimping shreds tips. Manage skin so a flapper doesn't end a session before the training does. |

> **Setup note.** The single most useful asset is **a steep, tunable wall where you can build a short, hard sequence and repeat it** — a spray or board wall where you can dial a 6–12-move crux to a known difficulty and take burn after burn on it. Everything in the schedule below is built around near-limit efforts you can reproduce and rest between.

## 5. The Warm-Up Protocol (Every Session)

**Never launch into near-limit power work cold — this is the single most important safety habit in the entire block.** Because every hard effort here is close to maximal, the warm-up's job is not just to raise temperature but to **fully prepare the pulleys, tendons, and nervous system to produce peak force safely**. A cold pulley snapped on a powerful crimp is the classic acute injury this training risks. A full warm-up takes about 20–25 minutes — deliberately longer and more thorough than for lower-intensity work — and has three stages: raise the pulse, mobilize and activate, then progressively ramp toward peak intensity.

### Stage 1 — Pulse raise (5 min)
Get blood moving and body temperature up: easy cardio (skipping, brisk walk/jog, jumping jacks) plus a few minutes of very easy traversing on big holds. You want to feel genuinely warm — cold tissue produces and tolerates high force poorly.

### Stage 2 — Mobility & activation (5–7 min)
- Wrist circles and gentle wrist flexion/extension stretches.
- Finger flexion/extension: make fists and fully open the hands, 15–20 times; splay fingers wide; gentle traction on each finger.
- **Thorough shoulder and scapular prep** — doubly important here, because powerful dynamic moves load the shoulder hard: band pull-aparts, external rotations, scapular pulls/shrugs on the bar, slow scap pull-ups, a few controlled shoulder circles.
- Light forearm **extensor** activation (light band wrist curls or rubber-band finger extensions) to wake the antagonists and prep the elbow tendons for high force.

### Stage 3 — Progressive intensity ramp (10–12 min)
This is the power-specific part. Unlike an endurance warm-up (which builds *continuity*), here you build **intensity in careful steps** so your fingers and shoulders meet peak force only after they're fully ready. Never jump from easy climbing straight onto a limit crimp.

| Ramp step | Terrain / effort | Volume | Rest after |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Easy climbing / big holds, relaxed movement | 3–4 min | ~1 min |
| 2 | Moderate problems, controlled, ~60% effort — feel the fingers engage | 2–3 problems | ~1–2 min each |
| 3 | Harder problems, ~75–80% effort, including a couple of powerful moves and one crimpy move | 2–3 problems | ~2 min each |
| 4 | One near-working-intensity boulder at ~85–90% — a genuine "prime," not a max | 1 problem | Full recovery (**3–5 min**) before work sets |
| *(then)* | Begin the session's prescribed near-limit work | — | — |

> **Warm-up rule.** Your first working effort should feel *powerful and sharp*, not like a cold shock and not like a struggle. If your fingers feel flat or achy, a shoulder feels loose or pinchy, or an elbow complains during the ramp — treat it as information. Extend the warm-up, reduce the day's intensity, or stop. **With near-limit work, an incomplete warm-up is a genuine injury risk, not just a performance dip.** When in doubt, add another ramp step; never subtract one.

## 6. The 6-Week Periodized Schedule

### Phase logic at a glance

| Phase | Weeks | Primary goal | Loading character | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1 — Strength-power base** | 1–2 | Maximize recruitment, RFD, and raw short-burst power; re-prime the strength ceiling | **Very high intensity, very low volume, full recovery** — short limit boulders and max-recruitment work | You can't sustain power you haven't built. This phase sharpens peak force and RFD *fresh* so that the later phases have something real to defend against fade. |
| **Phase 2 — Power-endurance intervals** | 3–4 | Resist power fade: hold near-limit force across a short, hard sequence and across repeated bursts | **High intensity, moderate volume** — short hard linkups, hard 4×4 on near-limit problems, on-the-minute hard boulders, ~30–60 s max-effort intervals with generous rest | Repeated near-limit bursts with quality rest train the ATP-PCr↔glycolytic hand-off, buffering, and the ability to reproduce high force while fatigued. |
| **Phase 3 — Redpoint-specific peaking** | 5–6 | Convert fade-resistance into sending your actual crux; peak, then taper | **Highest specificity, lower volume** — project/crux-simulation burns, linked crux sequences; Week-6 taper/deload | Point the trained power at the exact sequence you want to send, in the exact fatigued state you'll meet it, then shed fatigue so peak power shows up on the go. |

**Sessions per week:** **2 hard sessions**, in every phase, separated by at least **48–72 hours** (e.g., Tue/Fri). This is a **low-frequency, high-intensity** block — the opposite of the capacity manual, where lower-intensity work allowed a third session. Near-limit power work demands full freshness; a third hard session buys fatigue, not fitness. Light, easy climbing or skills work on other days is fine.

> **Fit around climbing, don't stack on top of it.** These sessions *are* your hard climbing for the week — do **not** also do a separate maximal bouldering or limit-hangboard session on top. Near-limit efforts are only useful when you're fresh enough to actually be near your limit; done tired, they're just risky junk volume. If a week is unusually stressful or under-slept, take the lighter option or an extra rest day. Quality is the entire point of this block.

### Week 1 — Phase 1: Strength-power base

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Power-base A | Thorough warm-up → **Limit-boulder linkups** (short, 2–4 move powerful problems at near-limit, full rest — see §7) → light **supportive max-strength** work → antagonist/prehab |
| *(48–72h rest)* | | |
| Day 2 | Power-base B | Warm-up → **Limit boulders** on *different* moves/angles → optional **cautious power work** (if experienced & symptom-free) → antagonist/prehab |
| Other days | Easy climb / rest | Easy-to-moderate climbing or skills only; avoid additional maximal or crimp-heavy sessions. At least 2 full rest days. |

**Focus:** produce peak force and RFD **fresh, with full recovery** between every effort. Keep total efforts low — this phase is about *quality*, not fatigue. Establish your baseline benchmark at the start of the week (see §9). Resist the urge to link long, pumpy sequences yet — that comes in Phase 2.

### Week 2 — Phase 1: Strength-power base (progressed)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Power-base A | Warm-up → **Limit boulders**, slightly harder moves *or* +1 problem vs. Week 1 (keep full rest) → supportive max-strength → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 2 | Power-base B | Warm-up → **Limit boulders** on different angles → optional cautious power work → antagonist/prehab |

**Progression:** add a little *intensity* (slightly harder single moves) or **one** more effort — never more volume at the cost of quality, and never shorten the full rests. If Week 1 left fingers or shoulders achy beyond normal fatigue, repeat Week 1 rather than progressing. Peak-power work is only valuable when it's genuinely peak.

### Week 3 — Phase 2: Power-endurance intervals

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Power-endurance A | Thorough warm-up → **Short hard power-endurance intervals** (~30–60 s near-limit bursts with generous rest, or on-the-minute hard boulders — see §7) → antagonist/prehab |
| *(48–72h rest)* | | |
| Day 2 | Power-endurance B | Warm-up → **Hard 4×4 on near-limit problems** *or* **short hard linkups** (6–12 hard moves) → antagonist/prehab |
| Other days | Easy climb / rest | Keep other climbing easy. At least 2 full rest days. Skip separate hard bouldering. |

**Shift:** now you train **fade-resistance**. The moves stay hard and powerful, but you repeat them under incomplete recovery — bursts of near-limit climbing with rest generous enough to keep each burst *high quality*, not sub-maximal grinding. Target the *last* burst of a set feeling genuinely hard but still powerful. **Never grind linkups to a flailing, sloppy, high-injury-risk failure** — that trains bad movement and cooks tissue.

### Week 4 — Phase 2: Power-endurance intervals (progressed)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Power-endurance A | Warm-up → **Short hard intervals**, +1 rep per set *or* slightly harder bursts vs. Week 3 (keep rest generous) → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 2 | Power-endurance B | Warm-up → **Hard 4×4 / short linkups**, +1 set *or* modestly harder problems vs. Week 3 → antagonist/prehab |

**Progression:** add *one* variable at a time — one more burst, slightly harder moves, **or** a modestly tighter (but still generous) rest — never several at once, and never let the moves get sloppy. If Week 3 left you flat, or fingers/shoulders/elbows achy, hold Week 3's numbers. Reproducing clean, powerful bursts under fatigue *is* the adaptation.

### Week 5 — Phase 3: Redpoint-specific peaking

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Peak A | Warm-up → **Redpoint / crux-simulation burns** on your actual project or a crux-mimicking sequence, with long rests → antagonist/prehab |
| *(48–72h rest)* | | |
| Day 2 | Peak B | Warm-up → **Linked crux sequences** (crux + the moves into/out of it) *or* burns on a *second* crux-length sequence → antagonist/prehab |

**Focus:** point the trained power at the **exact sequence you want to send**, entered in the **fatigued state** you'll actually meet it (e.g., after climbing into the crux). Take **long rests** so each burn is a genuine, high-quality attempt — you're rehearsing sending, not accumulating junk. Total volume comes *down* from Week 4; specificity and quality go *up*. Do the hard burns while fresh; stop while you can still climb the crux well.

### Week 6 — Phase 3: Peak, taper & re-test

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Sharpen | Light-to-moderate warm-up + **1–2 short, sharp crux burns** at peak intensity to stay primed. **Very low volume. No grinding, no fade-to-failure.** |
| *(2–3 days rest)* | | Rest, eat, and sleep well. This is the taper — accumulated fatigue drops and peak power surfaces. **Prime redpoint/send attempts belong here.** |
| Day 2 | **Re-test** *(or send burn)* | Full warm-up → repeat the **standardized benchmark** from §9 under identical conditions → record. On a trip, this is your **send window** — the crux should feel like it has snap again. Then a couple of easy days. |

> **Week 6 is a deload built into the plan.** Do not add volume this week. Near-limit blocks generate real neural and tissue fatigue; the taper's job is to *shed* it so the power you built in Weeks 1–5 shows up on the re-test — and on the redpoint. This is precisely when a well-timed project attempt should happen: freshest, most powerful, fade-resistant, and skin-recovered.

### Auto-regulation rule (all weeks)

Prescriptions are targets, not orders. Each session, gate intensity and volume on how the warm-up ramp felt and your recent sleep/stress:
- **Feels strong and snappy, fingers/shoulders quiet, power holding across efforts:** train the prescribed work (progress if scheduled).
- **Feels flat / under-recovered / power fading unusually early / RFD feels slow:** hold the load, cut efforts, lengthen rests, or drop to the easier option. Near-limit work done flat is risky and low-value — better to bank a clean, lighter session.
- **Any finger/pulley, elbow, or shoulder pain, or the ramp feels "off":** stop. Rest, reassess next session. See §8.

## 7. Detailed Prescriptions by Method

Intensity in this program is near-limit, so it is gauged by **RPE and movement quality** on fully warmed-up tissue, and — crucially — by **work:rest ratios that favor quality over volume**. Rest here is *generous by design*: the whole point is that each hard effort is genuinely near your limit and powerfully executed, not a fatigued, sloppy imitation.

> **How to read effort here:** RPE 8–9 ≈ near-limit, powerful, but *controlled and clean* — you complete the moves with force to spare on the good efforts, and only the last effort of a set flirts with your ceiling. **We do not train power-endurance to repeated sloppy, flailing failure.** Occasional near-failure on a peaking burn is fine; a whole session of greasing off cooked and sloppy is how you overreach *and* how you rupture a pulley or tweak a shoulder.

### Method 1 — Limit-boulder linkups (Phase 1: strength-power base)

Short, powerful, near-limit boulder problems climbed with **full recovery** — the base of raw power and RFD that everything else defends against fade.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Structure | **Short problems of 2–4 hard, powerful moves** at or near your bouldering limit; steep terrain preferred |
| Intensity | **Very high — RPE 9.** Genuinely hard single moves; you're training peak force and RFD, not endurance |
| Efforts | **6–10 quality attempts** across the session (across 2–3 different problems) |
| Work per effort | **Short — a few seconds** (the 2–4 moves); it's over before glycolysis becomes the limiter |
| Rest between efforts | **Full — 3–5 minutes.** Complete recovery so every attempt is genuinely near-limit |
| Movement quality | Precise, powerful, controlled. Stop an effort if it turns into a desperate, sloppy flail |
| Regression / progression | Easier: bigger holds, less steep, fewer moves. Harder (Wk 2): slightly harder moves *or* +1 effort — never at the cost of full rest |

### Method 2 — Short hard power-endurance intervals (Phase 2: anaerobic power, work:rest focus)

Near-limit bursts repeated under **incomplete but generous** recovery — the core fade-resistance stimulus. Two interchangeable formats:

**Format A — ~30–60 s max-effort intervals.**

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Terrain | A short, **hard, sustained** sequence (~6–12 powerful moves) on steep terrain you can complete when fresh |
| Work interval | **Climb ~30–60 s of near-limit, powerful moves** at RPE 8–9 |
| Reps | **3–5 bursts** per set |
| Work : rest ratio | **~1:3 to 1:5** — *generous* rest (e.g., 45 s work : 2.5–4 min rest) so each burst stays powerful |
| Sets | 2–3 sets, with **5+ min** between sets |
| Intensity | Hard, powerful, *completable with good movement* — not falling off every burst |
| Progression | Wk 3 → Wk 4: +1 burst per set, slightly harder sequence, *or* modestly tighter (still generous) rest — one change only |

**Format B — On-the-minute (OTM) hard boulders.**

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Structure | Pick a **hard boulder** (~3–6 moves) you can send fresh. At the top of each minute, climb it; rest the remainder of the minute |
| Work per rep | A single hard problem (~10–20 s), then rest to the next minute |
| Reps | **5–8 minutes** (one problem per minute); build the count over the phase |
| Intensity | RPE 8–9; you should feel power fade creeping in over the reps — resisting that fade is the training |
| Rest | The remainder of each minute (built-in ~40–50 s); take a full rest and repeat for a 2nd round if prescribed |
| Progression | Add minutes (reps), a slightly harder problem, *or* a 2nd round — stop the round the moment movement gets sloppy |

### Method 3 — Hard 4×4 on near-limit problems / short hard linkups (Phase 2)

The classic 4×4, but pushed toward the **power** end — harder problems, fewer of them, more rest than a capacity-style 4×4 — to train fade-resistance across a linked hard sequence rather than long-route pump.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Structure | Pick **4 hard-but-doable problems** (nearer your limit than a capacity 4×4 — e.g., 2–4 grades below max, powerful). Climb all 4 back-to-back = **one set** |
| Work per set | ~**1–2 minutes** of hard, linked climbing (shorter/harder than a capacity 4×4) |
| Sets | **3–4 sets** (start with 3 in Wk 3) |
| Intensity | **Hard, powerful — RPE 8–9.** Fingers and power fade across the set; the last set is genuinely hard but still clean |
| Work : rest ratio | **~1:2 to 1:3** — more rest than a capacity 4×4, to keep intensity high (e.g., ~1.5 min work : 3–4 min rest) |
| Rest between sets | **~3–4 minutes** — enough to keep the next set powerful, not to fully clear |
| Regression / progression | Easier: less steep, fewer/easier problems, longer rest. Harder (Wk 4): +1 set *or* harder problems — one change at a time |

### Method 4 — Redpoint / crux-simulation burns (Phase 3: redpoint peaking)

The most specific method — **near-limit efforts on your actual crux or a faithful simulation**, entered fatigued, with **long rests** so each burn is a real send attempt.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Terrain | Your **project crux** (or a set sequence that mimics it) — the exact powerful moves you want to send, ideally entered from a few moves below so you arrive slightly fatigued |
| Work interval | One full crux effort — **~10–45 s** of near-limit climbing (the crux plus its entry/exit moves) |
| Reps (burns) | **3–5 quality burns** per session |
| Intensity | **Near-limit — RPE 9.** The goal is *quality* send-rehearsal, not accumulated volume |
| Work : rest ratio | **~1:5 to 1:10** — long rests so each burn is fresh and truly represents a send go |
| Rest between burns | **~8–15 minutes** (near-full recovery) |
| Progression | Wk 5 → Wk 6: reduce volume, keep intensity; sharpen the exact sequence, don't pile on. In Wk 6 these *become* your prime send attempts |

### Method 5 — Supportive max-strength maintenance (all phases, low dose)

A little heavy strength work keeps the **ceiling** (from a prior *Contact Strength* block, or your climbing) from eroding while you train power-endurance. Kept low-volume and heavy; done when fresh, near the *start* of a session, never after a fatiguing interval set.

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Intensity | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Short max hangs** (half-crimp or open, ~20 mm) | 3–4 × 7–10 s | Heavy but clean, symptom-free; *only* if experienced with hangboarding | 3 min |
| Weighted or bodyweight **pull-ups** | 2–3 × 3–5 | RPE 8 (RIR ~2), crisp, full ROM | 2–3 min |
| **Ring rows / heavy inverted rows** | 2 × 6–8 | RPE 7–8 | 2 min |

> Keep this **maintenance, not development** — a small dose keeps your ceiling up so your trained power-endurance has something high to express. If it's eating recovery from the near-limit work, cut it. Do it *fresh*, never on cooked fingers.

### Method 6 — Optional cautious power work (Phase 1–2, experienced climbers only)

Explosive, dynamic work (limited campus moves, dynos, powerful throws) can sharpen RFD — but it is **high-risk** and strictly optional. **Only** for experienced, fully symptom-free climbers with a solid strength base, always fresh, always after a thorough warm-up.

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Intensity | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Powerful dynamic boulder moves / dynos** | 4–6 × 1–2 moves | Explosive, controlled landing | Prefer *big, positive* holds. Full rest between |
| **Basic campus moves** *(large rungs, matching / up-down)* | 3–5 × short ladder | Controlled, no small rungs, no maximal reaches | **Advanced only.** Never on small edges; never to failure; stop at any finger/shoulder pain |
| **Rest between all power efforts** | — | Full (**3–5 min**) | Power work is worthless — and dangerous — when fatigued |

> **Campus and dynamic work carry the highest acute injury risk in this program.** If you have any pulley or shoulder history, **skip this method entirely.** It is optional garnish, not a required ingredient — the limit-boulder and interval work drive the adaptation on their own.

### Method 7 — Antagonist & prehab (every session, ~8–10 min)

Essential in a near-limit block: powerful pulling and dynamic moves load the elbows and shoulders hard, and antagonist/rotator-cuff work is your first line of defense against the tendon and shoulder problems this training can provoke. Do at the **end** of every session (fine on rest days too).

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wrist extensor curls** (light dumbbell/band) | 2–3 × 12–15 | Forearm extensors | Slow, full range; key for elbow-tendon balance |
| **Finger extensions** (rubber band around fingertips) | 2–3 × 15–20 | Finger extensors / intrinsics | Balances the heavily worked flexors |
| **Rotator-cuff external rotations** (band) | 2–3 × 12–15 | Shoulder stabilizers | Powerful dynamic moves load the shoulder — protect it |
| **Scapular work** (band pull-aparts, scap pull-ups) | 2–3 × 12–15 | Scapular control | Protects the whole pulling chain |
| **Elbow-tendon prehab** (slow wrist extension/flexion loading, "rice-bucket") | 2 × 12–15 | Medial/lateral elbow tendons | Valuable given the high forces here |

## 8. Injury Prevention & Tendon Health

> **This is the most important section in the program. Read it fully before your first session, and re-read it whenever the fingers, elbows, or shoulders feel off. The failure mode of near-limit power work is not slow overuse — it's the sudden, acute strain: a pulley pop on a hard crimp, a shoulder tweak on a powerful throw. No redpoint is worth a ruptured A2 or a season-ending shoulder.**

### The core principle: manage peak strain, not just volume

Where a capacity block's danger is *cumulative volume*, this block's danger is **acute, high-force strain**. Every hard, powerful, near-limit move is a moment where a pulley, tendon, or shoulder meets a large force — and where a cold, fatigued, or poorly positioned tissue can fail suddenly. Every rule below exists to keep those peak forces inside what your tissues can safely handle:

- **Warm up completely and progressively.** The single biggest acute-injury lever. Never meet peak force cold — see §5. When in doubt, add a ramp step.
- **Ramp intensity conservatively.** Add difficulty in small steps, one variable at a time. A crimp that's a hair too small, met with full power, is exactly how pulleys go.
- **Honor the phase order.** Weeks 1–2 build the recruitment and RFD (fresh, full-recovery) that make the fatigued near-limit work of Weeks 3–6 survivable. Don't jump to fatigued hard linkups without the base.
- **Respect the 48–72-hour spacing** between hard sessions, always. Near-limit tissue needs full recovery.
- **Never grind sloppy.** The most dangerous moves are the desperate, flailing, cooked ones. Stop an effort — or the session — when movement quality breaks down.

### Injuries this block can provoke

Near-limit, powerful climbing tends to cause **acute** strains:

- **Finger pulley injury (A2, A4) — the signature risk.** Hard crimping at near-limit force, especially a full-crimp or a foot cutting on a small hold, can strain or **rupture a finger pulley** (often felt/heard as a "pop" at the base or middle of a finger). This is the classic acute climbing injury and the single greatest risk of powerful crimp work. **Bias toward larger, more open holds** for near-limit efforts wherever possible; be especially cautious with full-crimp under high load.
- **Shoulder strain / instability.** Powerful dynamic moves, big throws, and hard lock-offs load the shoulder near its limit. Under-prepared or fatigued shoulders can strain the rotator cuff, labrum, or biceps tendon (hence the mandatory rotator-cuff and scapular work in §7).
- **Elbow tendon strain (medial/lateral).** High-force pulling and lock-offs shock-load the elbow tendons; a sudden hard move on a cold or under-prepared elbow can strain them acutely (distinct from the slow overuse tendinopathy of a volume block).
- **Skin failure.** Powerful crimping tears tips and pops flappers. Manage it (below) so skin doesn't force a rushed, sloppy, injury-prone finish.

### Bias holds, grips, and moves to protect tissue
- **Prefer larger, more positive, more open holds** for near-limit efforts. You can train powerful movement and fade-resistance without loading tiny, pulley-punishing crimps.
- **Be very cautious with full-crimp under high load** — it's the highest-strain grip for the A2 pulley. Favor half-crimp and open grips where the moves allow.
- **Warm the shoulders thoroughly** before any dynamic/campus work, and skip that work entirely if you have shoulder history.
- **Control your landings** on limit boulders — a bad fall from a powerful move injures ankles, wrists, and shoulders as readily as a hold does.

### Warning signs — what acute strain feels like
Unlike overuse, acute strain often announces itself *sharply and suddenly*:
- A **"pop," "crack," or sudden sharp pain** in a finger (especially at the base — a pulley), elbow, or shoulder **during** a hard move. This is a red-stop, no exceptions.
- **Localized swelling or tenderness** over a pulley or tendon after a session.
- **Pain that changes how you grip, pull, or reach overhead**, or that lingers or worsens after a session.
- A **shoulder that feels loose, "clunky," or apprehensive** on a dynamic move.

**A sudden sharp finger, elbow, or shoulder pain during a powerful move is a stop-now signal — do not "test it" with another burn.** Acute pulley and shoulder injuries get dramatically worse with continued loading.

### The green / amber / red pain rule
Use this every session to decide whether to train, modify, or stop:

| Signal | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 🟢 **Green** | Normal muscular fatigue and a powerful, clean feeling. No joint, tendon, pulley, or shoulder pain. | Train as prescribed. |
| 🟡 **Amber** | Mild ache/stiffness/"awareness" in a finger, elbow, or shoulder that is not sharp and settles quickly; power fading unusually early; movement getting sloppy. | Reduce intensity, use bigger/more open holds, lengthen rests, cut efforts, or end early. Do **not** progress, and do **not** push near-limit crimps. Reassess next session; if it persists, treat as red. |
| 🔴 **Red** | Sharp or sudden finger/pulley/elbow/shoulder pain; a "pop" or "crack"; swelling; a shoulder that feels unstable; pain that lingers, worsens, or changes how you grip or reach. | **Stop the session immediately.** No near-limit training until fully assessed and symptom-free. Seek a qualified professional. Do not self-diagnose, and do not "test" a suspected pulley or shoulder with another effort. |

### Mandatory rest & recovery spacing
- **At least 48–72 hours** between hard sessions — always. Near-limit work needs full neural and tissue recovery.
- Keep **at least 2 full rest days** per week during the block.
- Don't stack these sessions on top of separate maximal bouldering or heavy hangboarding — that's a peak-force overload on the same tissues.
- If fingers, elbows, or shoulders feel persistently tired, stiff, or achy across several days, that's under-recovery — take extra rest. Missing a session costs nothing; a ruptured pulley costs a season.

### A brief note on return-to-loading
> This program is for healthy, pain-free fingers, elbows, and shoulders and is **not** a rehabilitation protocol. If you've had a pulley injury, an elbow tendinopathy, or any shoulder issue, return to near-limit loading **only** when a qualified professional has cleared you and you are symptom-free. When you do return, restart at **Phase 1** intensities (or lower), bias hard toward big open holds, avoid full-crimp under load, ramp even more conservatively than usual, and treat any recurrence of symptoms as an immediate stop. Genuine pulley or shoulder rehab is individual and progressive and belongs with a physiotherapist — not with a generic download.

## 9. Testing & Benchmarking

A standardized test at the **start** and **end** of the block turns "I think I hold power better" into a number. Keep every variable identical across both tests: same wall/holds, same problems, same protocol, same warm-up, same time of day, same rested state.

### Standardized power-endurance benchmark (choose one and keep it fixed)

> **Prerequisite:** only test after a complete warm-up (§5), and only if the intensity ramp felt clean and powerful. If anything feels off — especially a finger, elbow, or shoulder — postpone. A near-limit test is not worth an injury.

**Option A — Fixed hard sequence, repeats-to-fade (recommended for most).**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Terrain | A **fixed, hard, powerful sequence** (~6–12 moves) on a board/spray wall you can send fresh |
| Task | Climb the sequence, take a **fixed short rest** (e.g., 90 s), repeat, until you can no longer complete it *cleanly* |
| Score | **Number of clean completions** (and note which effort you started to fade on) |
| Conditions | Identical holds, sequence, rest, angle, and warm-up at start and re-test |

**Option B — On-the-minute hard boulder, reps-to-fade.**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Structure | A fixed **hard boulder** (~3–6 moves) you can send fresh; climb it at the top of each minute |
| Task | Repeat on the minute until you can no longer complete it cleanly |
| Score | **Number of successful minutes** completed |
| Conditions | Identical problem, timing, and warm-up at start and re-test |

**Option C — Hard 4×4 sets-to-fade.**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Structure | A fixed set of **4 hard problems** linked back-to-back = one set, with a fixed rest (e.g., 3 min) between sets |
| Task | Complete as many **clean sets** as possible before you can no longer complete a set cleanly |
| Score | **Number of complete clean sets** (note where the first fade fell) |
| Conditions | Identical problems, rest, and warm-up at start and re-test |

**Record** your score plus how it felt, your RPE, and *where the power faded*. **Re-test in Week 6** after the taper, under identical conditions. More clean completions, more minutes, or more sets is your objective result. No change is still useful data — it tells you whether to repeat the block, adjust recovery, or change the stimulus.

> **Testing is a genuinely hard, near-limit effort.** Count the initial test as your first loaded session of Week 1, and don't also do a full interval session the same day. In Week 6, the re-test doubles neatly as a prime send attempt on a project of similar demand.

## 10. Recovery Essentials

Power adaptations are built during recovery, not during the effort — and near-limit work is *especially* recovery-hungry. Brief, evidence-aligned basics:

- **Sleep.** The highest-leverage recovery tool. Aim for **7–9 hours** of quality sleep. Neural recovery, tissue repair, and adaptation all depend on it; under-sleeping blunts peak power and RFD *directly* and raises acute-injury risk (fatigued tissue fails more easily under high force).
- **Session spacing.** Covered above and worth repeating: **48–72 hours** between hard sessions, 2+ rest days per week. Near-limit power needs full freshness even when you feel okay — flat power work is both useless and risky.
- **Fuel the work.** Short, high-intensity efforts draw on **phosphocreatine and carbohydrate** — under-fuelling flattens power and RFD. Eat enough total energy, include adequate carbohydrate around training, and support tissue with adequate **protein** (a common general guideline for active people is roughly **1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight per day**, spread across meals). This is general educational information, not individualized dietary advice.
- **Hydration.** Be well-hydrated for sessions; performance and force output drop when dehydrated.
- **Skin.** Powerful crimping destroys tips fast. File glassy calluses, moisturize, and stop before a hot-spot becomes a flapper — a torn tip forces sloppy, injury-prone climbing, and skin (not fitness) ends many power sessions.
- **Listen to trend, not just the day.** Persistent fatigue, poor sleep, power fading unusually early across sessions, or lingering finger/elbow/shoulder ache means back off. With near-limit work, pushing through under-recovery is where acute injuries happen.

## 11. After Week 6

Finish the block, bank the gains, and choose a deliberate next step. Power-endurance detrains relatively quickly, so how you follow up depends on *when you need this fitness*.

**Option A — Peak and send (redpoint window).** If you built this block *for* a project or trip, the days right after the Week-6 taper are your **best send window**: freshest, most powerful, most fade-resistant. Drop to **one short power-endurance session per week** to hold the quality, and spend your energy on prime redpoint attempts. Power-endurance detrains faster than max strength, so use the window while it's open.

**Option B — Take a down-week, then a new focus.** Run a lighter/recovery week (easy climbing, minimal near-limit work), then pivot to a complementary quality — maximal finger strength (see the sibling *Contact Strength* program) to raise the ceiling, or long-route pump tolerance (see the sibling *Anaerobic Capacity* program) to broaden the base your power sits on. Alternating power-endurance with those qualities across the year is exactly how the three siblings fit together.

**Option C — Repeat the block, progressed.** After at least a **light/recovery week**, re-run the 6 weeks from your **new** baseline (re-test numbers). Progress by using harder sequences, slightly tighter (but still generous) rests, or more specific crux simulations — but keep the same conservative, one-variable-at-a-time ramp discipline, keep biasing toward big open holds, and never sacrifice movement quality for volume. Don't run near-limit power blocks back-to-back indefinitely; cycle them with strength, capacity, and lighter periods across your training year.

> Whichever you choose, keep antagonist/prehab work and thorough warm-ups in your routine year-round — the shoulders and elbows especially will thank you, and the warm-up is your main defense against the acute injuries this quality risks. And keep testing periodically under the same standard — long-term trend is what tells you your training is working.

## 12. Sources & Further Reading

Genuine, well-known work in climbing science and training. Consult the original sources directly; the notes below are plain-language pointers, not exact quotations or specific numeric claims.

- **Jerónimo Aragón / Eva López-Rivera and colleagues** — applied climbing-science work on **maximal and intermittent finger loading and structured hangboard methods**, underpinning both the max-strength maintenance and interval-structuring ideas here. (López-Rivera's work also underpins structured hangboarding generally — see the sibling *Contact Strength* program.)
- **Nicolas Fanchini, Rômulo Bertuzzi, Nikolay Michailov and colleagues** — research on the **energy-system contributions and physiological demands of climbing**, including the anaerobic (ATP-PCr and glycolytic) contributions to short, hard efforts and the determinants of forearm power and endurance.
- **Dave MacLeod, *9 out of 10 Climbers Make the Same Mistakes*** and ***Make or Break*** — accessible, well-regarded books on climbing performance, power-endurance and redpoint tactics, and injury prevention, including fingers, elbows, and shoulders.
- **Eric Hörst, *Training for Climbing* and the Training4Climbing resources** — widely used practical guides to periodized climbing training, including power and power-endurance intervals (4×4s, limit bouldering), RFD, and warm-ups.
- **Volker Schöffl and colleagues** — the leading body of clinical research on **climbing injuries**, including **finger pulley (A2/A4) injuries**, shoulder injuries, and elbow tendinopathy, and their mechanisms and grading.
- **Lattice Training and related applied-science practitioners** — publicly available educational material on **power-endurance assessment, energy-system training, and structured programming** for redpoint climbers.
- **PAR-Q+ (2023) and the ePARmed-X+, © the PAR-Q+ Collaboration** — the physical-activity readiness screening referenced throughout this program.
- **CSEP (Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology)** — evidence-based physical activity, resistance-training, and sleep guidance underpinning the general recovery and training principles here.

> These are starting points for going deeper — not a claim that this program reproduces any specific study's protocol. Where you see a number in this manual, treat it as an educational default to individualize, not a research finding.
