## 1. Overview

**Footwork, balance, and single-leg stability** are the quiet engine of good climbing. Precise foot placement, a calm centre of mass, and the ability to stand up on one leg and *trust it* are what let you rest weight on your feet instead of hanging off your fingers. It is the quality that decides whether a slab feels like a delicate, readable staircase or a greasy, terrifying slide — and whether, on any route, you arrive at the crux with fresh forearms or already half-pumped from over-gripping easy ground.

This program develops that quality through **structured, progressive motor-skill practice layered with lower-body stability strength**. Over six weeks it moves you through three deliberate phases — a foundation phase (balance, ankle/foot proprioception, and precise-placement technique drills), a strength-and-dynamic-balance phase (single-leg strength, calf/tibialis conditioning, unstable-surface and harder footwork drills), and a climbing-integration phase (technical slab and precise-feet circuits under mild pressure) — so that the *skill* of accurate, trusting footwork and the *physical capacity* to hold single-leg positions are built together and then expressed on the wall.

**How this differs from its siblings.** This is **not** a finger or upper-body program. It does not train how hard you can pull (*Contact Strength & Finger Power*), how long you can pull (*Anaerobic Capacity for Long Routes*), or how well you can hold the wall with your core (body-tension work). It trains the **lower body and the motor skill of movement** — precise foot placement, balance, proprioception, and single-leg control. That focus matters because footwork is the cheapest strength gain in climbing: every kilogram you can genuinely stand on through a foot is a kilogram your fingers don't have to hold. Think of finger strength as your ceiling, endurance as your fuel tank, and footwork as your **fuel economy** — the skill that makes everything else go further.

**Who it's for:** climbers of any grade who over-grip, whose feet "pop" unexpectedly, who avoid slabs and vertical technical terrain, or who feel unstable reaching from small footholds. Because much of this is skill practice rather than maximal loading, it suits a wide range — from newer climbers building fundamentals to experienced climbers whose footwork never got deliberate attention. It is scalable through hold size, wall angle, balance-surface difficulty, and single-leg loading.

**Expected outcome (with consistent, attentive practice):** quieter, more accurate foot placements; more weight confidently transferred onto the feet; better single-leg balance and control; measurably longer single-leg balance times and cleaner single-leg squats; and — the real prize — climbing that feels less frantic, more precise, and less grip-dependent on technical terrain. Motor skills are durable once learned, but they are built by *quality attention*, not just repetitions — so this program is as much about *how* you practise as how much.

> **What this program is *not*.** It is not a rehab plan for a sprained ankle or an injured knee, not a lower-body max-strength or plyometric power block, not a substitute for climbing on real, varied terrain, and not a "balance tricks" novelty routine. It is one focused motor-skill-and-stability block that sits *inside* a broader climbing life.

## 2. Prerequisites & Readiness

Footwork and balance training is **lower in tissue-overload risk** than finger training — you are not stacking repeated maximal load on small connective tissue. Its distinctive risks are different: **falls** (from balance and unstable-surface work), **ankle sprains**, and **knee strain** from single-leg loading done with poor control or excessive depth. Gating who runs this, and in what environment, is the key safety decision.

### Minimum readiness before dedicated footwork & stability work

| Criterion | Minimum standard before starting |
|---|---|
| **Basic movement competence** | Able to squat, lunge, and stand on one leg for a few seconds without pain. You don't need to be strong — you need to be *pain-free and stable enough to learn*. |
| **Any climbing experience** | This block suits all grades. Newer climbers use it to build fundamentals; experienced climbers use it to fix ingrained sloppy footwork. No minimum grade. |
| **A safe practice environment** | Balance and unstable-surface work must be done where a stumble is harmless — clear floor, mats/crash pads nearby, a wall or rail to touch, and (for higher balance work) a spotter. On-wall work stays low and above pads. |
| **Symptom-free lower body** | No current ankle, knee, hip, or low-back pain, instability, or "giving way." Balance and single-leg loading provoke exactly the joints that need to be quiet to heal. |
| **No balance/vestibular red flags** | No dizziness, vertigo, fainting, or a diagnosed balance disorder. Eyes-closed and unstable-surface drills are unsafe with these until cleared. |
| **Cleared, apparently healthy adult** | If you're returning from a lower-limb injury, have osteoporosis/low bone density, or a neurological condition affecting balance, get individual clearance first. |

### Readiness reminder (PAR-Q)

Before any new training block, complete a **Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q+)**. If you answer "yes" to any item — or you are over 40 and inactive, pregnant/postpartum, or have a heart, blood-pressure, metabolic, joint, bone, or balance condition — clear vigorous exercise with a physician or qualified professional first.

### Who should NOT run this yet

> **Do not start this program if you:**
> - Have **any** current or recent ankle, knee, hip, or low-back injury, pain, instability, or a joint that "gives way" — even mild. Single-leg loading and unstable-surface work stress exactly those structures; they belong in guided rehab, not a generic download. Return to loading only when a qualified professional has cleared you and you are symptom-free.
> - Experience **dizziness, vertigo, fainting, or have a balance/vestibular disorder**. Eyes-closed and unstable-surface drills are genuinely unsafe until this is assessed.
> - Have **no safe space to practise** — nowhere with clear floor, pads, and something to touch for support. Do not do balance or on-wall footwork drills where a fall could injure you.
> - Have osteoporosis, significant low bone density, or a condition where a fall carries outsized risk, without individual clearance and modification.
>
> There is no rush. A rolled ankle or a tweaked knee can cost months — and, unlike a pump, a bad landing happens in an instant. A conservative, well-spotted ramp costs nothing.

## 3. The Science in Plain Language

### What "footwork" actually trains

Good footwork looks like it's about the feet, but it is really about two things working together: a **motor skill** (accurately placing and weighting a foot, then balancing over it) and the **physical capacity** to hold and control that position. This program trains both, because either one alone falls short — perfect balance strength is useless if you can't place a toe precisely, and perfect placement is useless if the leg can't hold the load.

### Balance and proprioception — your body's position sense

**Proprioception** is your sense of where your body is in space without looking — the constant stream of information from receptors in your muscles, tendons, joints, and the soles of your feet that tells your brain the angle of your ankle, how much a foot is weighted, and whether you're tipping. Balance is what your nervous system *does* with that information: tiny, fast, mostly unconscious muscle adjustments (especially around the ankle and hip) that keep your centre of mass over your base of support.

- **It's trainable.** Proprioception and balance improve with practice, and quickly. When you stand on one leg, or on an unstable surface, you challenge the system to sense small perturbations and correct them faster and more efficiently. Over weeks, the corrections become smaller, quicker, and more automatic — you wobble less and "find" a stance sooner.
- **It's task-specific.** Balance trained standing on the ground transfers *somewhat* to the wall, but balance trained *on the wall*, on climbing-relevant positions, transfers best. That's why this program pairs off-wall stability drills with on-wall footwork practice — the off-wall work builds the raw capacity, the on-wall work makes it climbing-specific.
- **Vision, then feel.** Beginners rely heavily on their eyes to balance. Training with eyes closed (only where safe) forces the system to lean on proprioception instead — which is exactly what you need when your gaze is up-route looking for the next hold rather than glued to your feet.

### Why precise footwork conserves grip and energy

This is the heart of why footwork is worth training. Climbing is a fight against gravity, and your legs are far larger and stronger than your forearms. Every time you genuinely stand on a foothold, you shift load off your fingers and onto your legs — the muscles built to carry your bodyweight all day.

- **Standing on feet unloads the fingers.** A well-weighted foot can carry a large share of your bodyweight. Poor footwork — feet placed vaguely, weighted timidly, or slipping — dumps that load back onto your hands, and you grip harder to compensate. Over a route, thousands of small over-grips add up to an early pump.
- **Precision saves re-tries.** A foot placed accurately the first time stays put. A foot placed sloppily has to be adjusted, re-placed, or trusted anxiously — each fidget costs energy, time, and grip while you hang on to reposition.
- **Quiet feet = controlled feet.** A foot that lands softly and precisely is one your nervous system has *aimed and controlled*. A foot that slaps or skitters onto a hold arrived without control — and an uncontrolled placement is one that's more likely to slip. "Silent feet" isn't an aesthetic; it's an audible readout of motor control.
- **Balance lets you stand still and rest.** The better your balance, the more positions become *rests* — places you can pause, breathe, shake out, and read the route — instead of moments you have to rush through because you can't hold them.

> **The one-sentence version:** your legs are your biggest, cheapest source of holding power, and footwork is the skill that lets you actually use them — every kilogram you can stand on is a kilogram your fingers don't have to.

### Single-leg stability and the foot / ankle / calf / tibialis

Much of climbing happens on **one foot** — stepping up, rocking over, standing on a single small hold while the other leg flags or reaches. That demands single-leg strength and control, which draws on a specific chain:

- **The foot and toes.** On small footholds you stand on the very front of the foot, often just the big-toe side of the shoe. The small muscles of the foot and the toe flexors help grip and stabilise on tiny edges and smears.
- **The ankle and its stabilisers.** The ankle is the primary balance joint. The muscles that run around it — especially the **peroneals** on the outer shin/ankle — resist the ankle rolling outward (the classic sprain mechanism) and make constant micro-adjustments to keep you balanced on a small hold.
- **The calf (gastrocnemius and soleus).** These power the plantarflexed, "on-your-toes" position you hold constantly while standing on footholds and smears. Calf endurance is why your feet stop feeling secure late in a pitch — a fatigued calf can't hold a precise high-heel smear.
- **The tibialis anterior (front of the shin).** Often neglected, it controls the foot as you place it and balances the strong calf behind it. Building it improves foot control and ankle-joint balance and is protective for the shin and ankle.

Training these gives you a foot and ankle that can *hold* a precise placement under load — the physical half of good footwork.

### Motor learning — how footwork precision is actually built

Footwork improves the way any fine motor skill improves, and understanding this changes how you practise:

- **Deliberate practice beats mindless repetition.** Skill is built by focused, attentive practice with a clear intention on each rep — not by hammering out sloppy reps on autopilot. Ten placements done with full attention teach more than a hundred careless ones.
- **Attention and external focus.** Skill learning tends to be faster when you focus on the *effect* of your movement (the foot landing silently on the exact edge) rather than the body part itself. Aim your attention at the foothold and the sound/precision of contact.
- **Quality reps, managed fatigue.** Motor learning degrades when you're fatigued — tired reps are sloppy reps, and sloppy reps ingrain sloppy patterns. This is why footwork practice belongs early in a session while you're fresh, and why skill drills are kept low-fatigue.
- **Feedback and self-correction.** You learn faster when you can tell good reps from bad. That's why this program uses concrete, *measurable* markers of quality — silence, no re-adjusts, holding a placement still for a beat — so every rep gives you honest feedback.
- **Frequency helps.** Because skill practice is low-fatigue, it can be done *often* — short, frequent, high-quality doses (even daily warm-up drills) build motor patterns faster than one big weekly session. This is a key difference from the strength work in this program, which needs recovery days between sessions.

> **The takeaway:** you build great footwork by pairing an attentive, high-quality *skill* practice (precise, quiet, trusted placements, done fresh and often) with the *physical stability* (balance, single-leg strength, calf and tibialis conditioning) that lets the leg hold what the skill sets up.

## 4. Equipment Needed

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **A wall with varied footholds** | A **slab or vertical wall** is ideal — a gym slab, a low-angle spray/systems board, an outdoor slab above pads, or even a moderate top-rope wall. You want lots of feet of different sizes (jugs, edges, smears, dishes, small chips) so you can practise precise placement across a range. |
| **A range of foothold sizes and a way to make feet harder** | Progress by choosing smaller, worse, or more spread-out footholds. Foot-only or feet-restricted problems (e.g., "hands stay, feet only on the small ones") are the core practice tool. |
| **Clear floor space** | A few square metres of stable, non-slip floor for single-leg balance, step-ups, split squats, and calf/tibialis work. Nothing to trip on; something (wall, rail, doorframe) within arm's reach to touch for support. |
| **A step, box, or bench** | For step-ups, elevated split squats, calf raises off an edge, and pistol-squat progressions to a box. A staircase step works. |
| **Optional balance implement** | A **balance beam / 2×4 on the floor, slackline (low & short), wobble board, BOSU, or folded mat/pillow.** Adds graded instability. Entirely optional — a firm floor and one leg get you most of the way. Any raised balance implement needs pads and, when higher, a spotter. |
| **Optional light load** | A backpack, a couple of dumbbells/kettlebells, or a weight vest to progress single-leg strength once bodyweight is controlled. Load progresses *after* control, never before. |
| **Climbing shoes that fit** | Precise footwork needs shoes that let you *feel* the hold and stand on small edges. You don't need aggressive shoes — you need ones that fit snugly and that you trust. Practising in vague, loose shoes teaches vague footwork. |
| **A logbook or app** | Record balance times, single-leg squat quality, calf-raise counts, foothold sizes used, drill quality notes, and how placements felt. Skill progress is easy to miss without notes to compare weeks. |
| **A phone (optional but valuable)** | Film your footwork drills. Watching yourself is the single fastest feedback tool for footwork — you'll see the re-adjusts and slaps you can't feel. |

> **Setup note.** The single most useful asset is **a slab or vertical wall with lots of varied footholds you can practise on repeatedly, above a safe landing** — plus a bit of clear floor for the stability work. Everything in the schedule below is built around those two things and needs almost no gear.

## 5. The Warm-Up Protocol (Every Session)

Never launch into single-leg loading or balance work cold. A full warm-up takes about 10–15 minutes and, for this quality, has two jobs: prepare the ankle, knee, hip, and calf tissue, and **wake up the balance and proprioceptive system** so your first precise placement or single-leg rep is controlled rather than clumsy. It has three stages: raise the pulse, mobilise and activate the lower limb, then prime balance and precision.

### Stage 1 — Pulse raise (3–5 min)
Get blood moving and body temperature up: easy cardio (brisk walk, light jog, skipping, easy cycling) or a few minutes of very easy traversing on big feet. You want warm ankles, calves, and hips — not tired legs. A little skipping is especially useful here: it gently primes the calf, ankle, and foot for the springy, balanced work to come.

### Stage 2 — Lower-limb mobility & activation (3–5 min)
- **Ankle circles and controlled ankle rocks** — draw the alphabet with each foot; slow ankle dorsiflexion/plantarflexion.
- **Ankle dorsiflexion mobility** — knee-to-wall rocks to open the front of the ankle (better ankle range = better smearing and rock-overs).
- **Calf and Achilles wake-up** — a few slow, controlled bodyweight calf raises (both legs), plus easy heel walks (tibialis).
- **Hip and knee prep** — bodyweight squats, walking lunges, hip circles, leg swings (front-back and side-side, holding support) to open the hip.
- **Foot/toe activation** — short-foot / toe-spread reps; scrunch and splay the toes to wake the small foot muscles you'll stand on.

### Stage 3 — Balance & precision priming (3–5 min)
This is the skill-specific part. Before loading or drilling, spend a few minutes reminding the nervous system how to balance and place accurately.

| Ramp step | Task | Duration / dose | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Two-leg to one-leg transitions**, eyes open, on firm floor | 3–4 per side | Find the quiet, centred single-leg stance |
| 2 | **Single-leg balance holds**, eyes open, near support | 2 × ~20 s per side | Touch support only if needed; aim for stillness |
| 3 | **Easy precise foot placements on the wall** — big/medium feet, silent, deliberate | 6–10 placements | Rehearse quiet, aimed, held-for-a-beat placements |
| 4 | **One near-task rehearsal** — the first drill/exercise of the day at an easy level | 1 easy set | Groove the pattern before the working sets |

> **Warm-up rule.** Your first working rep — whether a single-leg squat, a balance hold, or a precise on-wall placement — should feel *controlled and clean*, not wobbly and rushed. If an ankle or knee feels unstable, painful, or "off" during the ramp, or your balance is unusually poor that day (poor sleep and fatigue hit balance hard) — treat it as information. Reduce the day's difficulty (firmer surfaces, shallower loading, bigger feet, more support) or stop. A warm-up that doesn't come around cleanly is a reason to back off, not to push through.

## 6. The 6-Week Periodized Schedule

### Phase logic at a glance

| Phase | Weeks | Primary goal | Loading / practice character | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1 — Foundation: balance, proprioception & precise placement** | 1–2 | Build baseline balance and ankle proprioception; groove precise, quiet, trusted foot placements | Low-fatigue skill practice + easy stability holds on firm/stable surfaces; technique drills on the wall on generous feet | You can't build strong single-leg control or advanced footwork on a shaky base. This phase teaches the *pattern* — accurate placement, weighting the foot, staying still on one leg — before adding load or instability. |
| **Phase 2 — Single-leg strength & dynamic balance** | 3–4 | Build the leg, calf, and tibialis strength and the dynamic balance to *hold* precise positions under load | Progressive single-leg strength (step-ups, split squats, pistol progressions), calf/tibialis conditioning, unstable-surface balance, harder footwork drills (glue-foot, one-touch) | Once the pattern is grooved, add the physical capacity to express it — a leg that can hold a small foothold, and balance that survives movement and mild instability. |
| **Phase 3 — Climbing integration** | 5–6 | Transfer skill + strength onto real, technical climbing; trust the feet under mild pressure | Technical slab/vertical circuits, precise-feet and no-hands drills, footwork under time/route pressure; strength held at maintenance | Skill only counts when it survives real climbing. This phase moves the trained footwork onto varied terrain and stress-tests it, so it shows up when it matters. |

**Sessions per week:** this program has **two distinct kinds of work with different frequencies.**
- **Skill / footwork practice is low-fatigue and can be frequent** — up to **4–6 short doses per week**, including brief drills tacked onto warm-ups or normal climbing days. Quality attention matters more than volume; keep each dose short and crisp while fresh.
- **Single-leg *strength* work is fatiguing and needs recovery** — keep it to **2 dedicated strength sessions per week**, separated by at least **48 hours** (e.g., Mon/Thu), so the legs, calves, and connective tissue adapt.

> **Fit skill practice around climbing; keep strength sessions distinct.** Do footwork drills fresh — at the start of climbing days or as standalone short sessions — never at the pumped, sloppy end of a hard session, because tired reps ingrain tired patterns. Put strength work on its own or after (not before) skill practice, and keep an eye on total lower-limb load: if a week is unusually busy or under-slept, keep the skill drills (they're restorative) and trim the strength volume.

### Week 1 — Phase 1: Foundation (learn the pattern)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Skill + light stability A | Full warm-up → **balance/proprioception drills** (single-leg holds, eyes open) → **footwork technique drills** on the wall (silent feet, precise placement, weighting the foot) → light calf/tibialis |
| *(rest or easy day)* | | |
| Day 2 | Skill B | Warm-up → shorter **balance drills** → **technique drills** on the wall (precision placement, one-touch intro) — kept fresh and crisp |
| Day 3 *(optional)* | Skill micro-dose | Warm-up drills only: 5–10 min of single-leg holds + a few precise placements as part of a normal climbing day |
| Other days | Climb / rest | Normal easy-to-moderate climbing is fine; bring *attention* to your feet on every climb. At least 2 easier/rest days. |

**Focus:** learn what a precise, quiet, fully-weighted placement *feels* like, and find a still single-leg stance. Establish your baseline tests at the start of the week (see §9). Keep everything on firm, stable surfaces and generous feet — this phase is about pattern quality, not difficulty.

### Week 2 — Phase 1: Foundation (progressed)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Skill + light stability A | Warm-up → balance drills (add **eyes-closed** single-leg holds *only where safe, near support*) → technique drills on **slightly smaller feet** → light calf/tibialis |
| Day 2 | Skill B | Warm-up → balance drills → **glue-foot / one-touch** technique drills on the wall |
| Day 3 *(optional)* | Skill micro-dose | Warm-up drills + attentive footwork on a normal climbing day |

**Progression:** raise *skill* difficulty, not load — introduce eyes-closed balance (safely, near support), slightly smaller footholds, and the first quiet/one-touch drills. Keep it low-fatigue. If balance felt shaky in Week 1, repeat Week 1's difficulty — a solid pattern is the goal.

### Week 3 — Phase 2: Single-leg strength & dynamic balance

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Strength A | Thorough warm-up → **single-leg strength** (step-ups, split squats, pistol progression) → **calf raises + tibialis raises** → short balance/precision finisher |
| *(48h+ rest)* | | |
| Day 2 | Skill + dynamic balance B | Warm-up → **unstable-surface balance** (wobble/BOSU/foam, near support) → **harder footwork drills** (glue-foot, one-touch on smaller feet) |
| Day 3 *(optional)* | Skill micro-dose | Short attentive footwork on a climbing day |
| Other days | Easy climb / rest | Keep at least 2 easier/rest days; don't stack heavy leg days back-to-back. |

**Shift:** add physical load. Single-leg strength is now a dedicated session; footwork drills get harder and more dynamic, with graded instability. Strength effort target: hard but controlled — **RPE ~7–8, leaving 2–3 reps in reserve**, always with clean depth and alignment. Never grind a single-leg exercise to a wobbling, knee-caving failure.

### Week 4 — Phase 2: Single-leg strength & dynamic balance (progressed)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Strength A | Warm-up → single-leg strength: **+ light load or +1 set or a harder pistol progression** vs. Week 3 → calf + tibialis (add reps/load) → balance finisher |
| Day 2 | Skill + dynamic balance B | Warm-up → unstable-surface balance (progress surface **or** add eyes-closed/head-turns, safely) → footwork drills on **smaller/worse feet** |

**Progression:** add *one* variable at a time — a little load, one more set, a deeper pistol progression, a wobblier surface, **or** smaller feet — never several at once. If Week 3 left a knee or ankle achy or your control sloppy, hold Week 3's numbers. Clean, controlled single-leg reps are the win.

### Week 5 — Phase 3: Climbing integration

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | On-wall integration A | Warm-up → **technical slab / precise-feet circuits** (see §7) → **no-hands / feet-focus drills** → short strength *maintenance* (1 set each key lift) |
| *(48h+ rest before next strength/hard day)* | | |
| Day 2 | On-wall integration B | Warm-up → **footwork under mild pressure** (light time pressure, harder feet, or slightly higher-but-safe terrain) → balance maintenance |
| Day 3 *(optional)* | Skill micro-dose | Attentive precise footwork on a normal climbing day |

**Focus:** move the trained footwork and stability onto real climbing. Climb technical, foot-dependent terrain and *deliberately* apply precise placement, weighting, and balance. Add **mild** pressure — a stopwatch, an audience, harder feet — so the skill starts to hold up under stress. Keep strength at maintenance (one crisp set) so the legs stay fresh for quality movement.

### Week 6 — Phase 3: Integration, consolidation & re-test

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Integrate & sharpen | Light warm-up + **1–2 quality technical circuits** with full attention on precise, trusted feet. **Low volume, high quality. No fatigued, sloppy grinding.** |
| *(1–2 days easy)* | | Rest, sleep, normal easy climbing. Balance and skill express best when fresh. |
| Day 2 | **Re-test** | Full warm-up → repeat the **standardized tests** from §9 under the same conditions → record. Then normal climbing. |

> **Week 6 is a consolidation/deload built into the plan.** Do not add strength volume or chase new difficulty this week. Balance, precision, and single-leg control all express best on fresh legs and a rested nervous system; the light week lets the skill you built in Weeks 1–5 show up in the re-test — and lets you finish the block sharp and confident, not frazzled.

### Auto-regulation rule (all weeks)

Prescriptions are targets, not orders. Each session, gate difficulty and load on how the warm-up felt and your recent sleep/stress (balance and motor control are *very* sensitive to fatigue):
- **Feels stable, control is clean, joints quiet:** train the prescribed work (progress if scheduled).
- **Feels wobbly / under-recovered / balance poor today:** hold the load, drop to a firmer surface, use bigger feet, add support, cut a set. Don't progress.
- **Any ankle, knee, hip, or back pain, or the warm-up feels "off":** stop the loaded/unstable work. Rest, reassess next session. See §8.

## 7. Detailed Prescriptions by Method

This program has three families of work: **balance & proprioception drills**, **single-leg strength**, and **footwork technique drills**, plus **on-wall application**. Intensity for the *strength* work is gauged by control and reps-in-reserve (RIR); intensity for *skill* work is gauged by **precision and quality**, not effort. The most important idea below: **skill drills are judged by how *clean* they are, not how *hard*.**

> **How to read quality here:** a footwork rep "counts" only if it meets its quality marker — silent, first-time-accurate, held still, no re-adjust. **We do not train footwork to sloppy fatigue.** Stop a skill drill when quality drops, not when you're tired — the last thing you want to practise is a bad placement.

### Method 1 — Balance & proprioception drills (Phase 1–2: build the balance system)

Off-wall balance work to train the ankle and proprioceptive system. Low-fatigue; do fresh; always near something to touch and above a safe floor.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Core drill | **Single-leg stand.** Balance on one foot, other foot off the floor, hands relaxed. Aim for stillness, not rigidity |
| Progression ladder | (1) eyes open, firm floor → (2) eyes closed, firm floor *(near support)* → (3) eyes open, unstable surface (foam/wobble/BOSU) → (4) eyes closed or head-turns/limb-movements on unstable surface *(only where safe, spotter/pads)* |
| Hold time | **20–40 s per rep**, building toward the longer end; stop the rep when you lose the position |
| Reps / sets | **3–5 holds per leg**, both legs (train the weaker side to match) |
| Rest | As needed — this is not fatiguing; ~30–60 s |
| Intensity gauge | Difficulty comes from the **surface and vision**, not effort. Pick a level where you can *just* hold reasonably still |
| Safety | Always within reach of support; unstable surfaces above pads; eyes-closed and wobble work only if free of dizziness (see §2, §8) |
| Progression | Advance the ladder only when the current level is calm and controlled — never skip straight to the wobbliest, eyes-closed version |

### Method 2 — Single-leg strength (Phase 2: build the leg, calf & tibialis)

Progressive strength for the muscles that hold precise foot positions. Controlled load, clean depth and alignment; done fresh, not after sloppy fatigue.

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Intensity / control | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Box / bench step-ups** (drive through the top foot, control the descent) | 3 × 8–10 per leg | RIR 2–3; add light load once bodyweight is controlled | Don't push off the bottom foot — the *top* leg does the work |
| **Split squats / rear-foot-elevated split squats** | 3 × 8–10 per leg | RIR 2–3; front knee tracks over foot | Progress from floor split squat → rear foot elevated → light load |
| **Pistol-squat progression** (single-leg sit-to-box) | 3 × 5–8 per leg | Controlled; **depth limited to pain-free, aligned range** | Progress: high box → lower box → assisted (hold support) → free. **Never chase depth at the cost of a caving knee** |
| **Calf raises** (double-leg → single-leg, off a step for range) | 3 × 12–15 (single-leg: 8–12) | Slow, full range, brief pause at top; add load progressively | Trains the on-your-toes standing you do constantly on footholds |
| **Tibialis raises** (toes-up against a wall/with band) | 2–3 × 15–20 | Slow, full range | The neglected front-of-shin muscle; balances the calf, protects the ankle |
| **Optional: single-leg RDL / hip hinge** (balance + posterior chain) | 2–3 × 8–10 per leg | Controlled, hinge at hip, flat back | Builds hip control and single-leg balance under load |

> **Load progresses *after* control, never before.** Add weight only when you own the bodyweight version with clean depth, a stable knee, and no wobble. A deeper or heavier rep with a caving knee is a step backward, not forward. Train the weaker leg to match the stronger — climbing demands both.

### Method 3 — Footwork technique drills (all phases: the core skill practice)

The heart of the program. Low-fatigue, high-attention on-wall drills. Do these **fresh**, judged strictly on quality. Each has a concrete quality marker so you get honest feedback every rep.

| Drill | How to do it | Quality marker (how to measure) | Progression |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Silent feet** | Climb easy terrain placing every foot so quietly you can't hear it touch | **Zero audible slaps** across the climb | Smaller feet, steeper/harder terrain, faster pace while staying silent |
| **Precision placement** | Before weighting, place the toe *exactly* on the chosen spot of the hold; commit | **No re-adjust** — the foot lands right the first time and stays | Smaller, worse holds; specific target spots on a hold |
| **Glue foot (no re-adjust)** | Once a foot touches a hold, it may **not** move again until you step off it | **Foot never repositions** once placed | Smaller feet; longer sequences; on a slab |
| **One-touch** | Each foot gets exactly **one** attempt to land on its hold — aim, place, commit | **Every foot placed correctly on the first touch** | Smaller/worse feet; more moves per climb |
| **Weight-the-foot / rock-over** | Deliberately transfer full weight onto a foot and stand up on it, hands light | You can **momentarily lift a hand** or stand tall over the foot without slipping | Smaller feet; higher, more committing rock-overs; slab |
| **Flagging practice** | On steep-ish or balancy moves, use a trailing flagged leg to counter-balance instead of a foothold | Move stays **quiet and balanced**, no barn-door swing | Longer reaches; both inside and outside flags; harder positions |
| **Feet-only / no-hands slab** (where safe & low) | Move up easy slab using minimal or no hands, driving off precise feet and balance | Smooth, balanced movement with **weight genuinely on the feet** | Less hand use; smaller feet; steeper slab — kept low above pads |

> **How to practise these well.** Fresh, attentive, and *slow enough to be perfect*. Point your attention at the foothold and the *sound* of contact (an external focus), not at your leg. Do short doses — a few climbs per drill — and **stop the drill when quality slips**, because a tired, sloppy rep teaches the wrong pattern. Film occasionally; you'll see re-adjusts and slaps you can't feel.

### Method 4 — Dynamic & unstable-surface balance (Phase 2: robust balance)

Bridges static balance and moving climbing — balance that survives movement and mild instability.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Surfaces | Foam pad → wobble board → BOSU → (advanced, spotted) short/low **slackline** or **beam** |
| Tasks | Single-leg holds with **added challenge**: slow head turns, reaching a hand to targets, passing a light object, small controlled dips, catching/tossing a light ball |
| Dose | **3–5 rounds per leg**, ~20–40 s or ~5–8 controlled reaches each |
| Intensity gauge | Hard enough to challenge control, controlled enough to stay safe and clean |
| Rest | As needed; low-fatigue |
| Safety | **Pads down, support within reach, spotter for beam/slackline.** Never do this where a fall could injure you (see §8) |
| Progression | Advance surface, add vision challenge, or add reach distance — one at a time |

### Method 5 — On-wall application (Phase 3: integration & transfer)

Puts the trained skill and strength onto real, technical climbing and stress-tests it under mild pressure.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Terrain | **Technical slab / vertical** with lots of feet; foot-restricted or "hard-feet" versions of familiar problems |
| Core sessions | Precise-feet circuits (apply silent/glue-foot/one-touch on real routes); rock-over and flagging practice on real moves; no-hands/feet-focus laps on easy slab |
| Mild pressure (Wk 5–6 only) | Add **one** stressor: light time pressure (climb it clean under a stopwatch), an observer, harder footholds, or slightly higher-but-safe terrain — so the skill holds up when it counts |
| Volume | Kept sub-fatiguing — this is quality practice, not a pump session; stop when precision drops |
| Intensity gauge | Success = **clean, quiet, trusted feet on real climbing**, not "sent hard despite thrashing feet" |
| Safety | Keep it above pads / on a rope; "mild" pressure stays *mild* — never trade safety for a stressor |

### Method 6 — Antagonist, foot & ankle prehab (every strength session, ~8–10 min)

Because this block adds calf, foot, and ankle volume and stresses the ankle in balance work, targeted prehab protects exactly the tissues under new load. Do at the **end** of strength sessions (fine on easy days too).

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tibialis raises** | 2–3 × 15–20 | Front of shin / ankle balance | Balances the calf; protective for shin and ankle |
| **Ankle eversion (band) — peroneals** | 2–3 × 15–20 | Outer ankle stabilisers | Key defence against the outward-roll sprain mechanism |
| **Ankle inversion & dorsiflexion (band)** | 2 × 15 | Full ankle control | Round out ankle strength in all directions |
| **Foot / toe work** (short-foot, toe splays, towel scrunches) | 2 × 10–15 | Intrinsic foot muscles | The small muscles you stand on for smears and small edges |
| **Slow eccentric calf lowers** (off a step) | 2 × 10–12 | Achilles / calf tendon | Builds tendon tolerance to the new calf volume |
| **Hip abduction / glute-med (band walks, side-lying)** | 2 × 12–15 | Hip stability | A stable hip keeps the knee tracking and balance solid |

## 8. Injury Prevention & Tendon Health

> **This is the most important section in the program. Read it fully before your first session, and re-read it whenever an ankle, knee, or the balance surface feels off. The failure mode here is different from finger training: it is not slow tendon overuse but an *acute event* — a rolled ankle from a wobble, a tweaked knee from an uncontrolled single-leg rep, or a fall from a balance surface. No footwork gain is worth a rolled ankle.**

### The core principle: control the environment and progress control before difficulty

Where a finger block's danger is cumulative load, this block's dangers are **falls, ankle sprains, and knee strain** — mostly *sudden* events that happen when difficulty outruns control. Every rule below exists to keep the environment safe and to make sure control leads difficulty:

- **Never do balance or on-wall footwork work where a fall is dangerous.** Clear floor, pads/mats down, support within reach, and a spotter for raised implements (beam, slackline) and committing on-wall work. Keep on-wall drills **low, above pads, or on a rope.**
- **Progress control before difficulty.** Earn the wobblier surface, the eyes-closed hold, the deeper pistol, or the smaller foothold only when the current level is calm and clean. Skipping ahead is exactly how ankles roll and knees tweak.
- **Respect fatigue.** Balance and motor control degrade sharply when tired or under-slept — that's when wobbles become sprains and reps get sloppy. Do skill and balance work fresh; stop when control drops.
- **Add one variable at a time** (surface, vision, load, depth, or foot size) — never several. When in doubt, hold a level rather than jumping.

### Injuries this block can provoke (and how to avoid them)

**1. Ankle sprain — the signature risk.** The classic mechanism is the ankle rolling *outward* (inversion) as you lose balance or land awkwardly.
- Keep unstable-surface work **above pads, near support, and spotted** when raised.
- **Ramp instability conservatively** — firm floor → foam → wobble board → BOSU → beam/slackline, mastering each.
- Build the **peroneals and full ankle** (see §7 Method 6) — stronger ankle stabilisers resist the roll.
- **Landing and falling awareness:** know how you'll step off or bail *before* you start a balance rep or an on-wall drill. If you can't fall/step off safely, the difficulty or the setting is wrong.

**2. Knee strain — control the depth and the alignment.** Single-leg loading stresses the knee, and two things provoke it:
- **Excessive depth.** Deep single-leg squats and pistols load the knee heavily at the bottom. **Limit depth to your pain-free, controlled range** and progress it slowly. There is no prize for a deep rep with a wobble.
- **Knee caving inward (valgus) or twisting.** Keep the knee tracking **over the foot**, not collapsing in. Be especially cautious with **deep knee flexion under load and any drop-knee-style twisting positions** — deep, loaded, twisted knee flexion is a mechanism for **meniscus** irritation. Train drop-knee *movement* on the wall gently and unloaded before adding demand; don't hunt deep, torqued knee positions in the gym.

**3. Achilles, plantar, calf & tibialis overload.** The added calf-raise, foot, and standing-on-toes volume is new load for the calf–Achilles complex, the plantar tissue of the foot, and the tibialis.
- **Ramp calf and foot volume gradually** — this tissue adapts slowly; a big jump in calf work is a classic Achilles/plantar irritant.
- Include **slow eccentric calf lowers** (§7) to build tendon tolerance.
- Watch for **morning heel/arch stiffness** or Achilles tenderness — early signs of doing too much too soon.

### Warning signs — what to watch for
- **Sharp** ankle or knee pain during a rep or a wobble — stop immediately (see red rule).
- An ankle or knee that feels **unstable, loose, or "gives way"** — a stop signal, not something to train through.
- **Achilles or heel/arch ache or morning stiffness** that shows up after calf/foot work and takes longer to settle each week — back off the calf/foot volume.
- **Persistent shin, knee, or ankle soreness** across days — under-recovery; add rest, cut volume.
- **Any dizziness or light-headedness** during balance work — stop; balance drills are unsafe with these until assessed.

**A joint that feels unstable, or sharp pain in the ankle or knee, is not "part of the challenge" — it's a stop signal.** Acute joint injuries happen in a moment and can cost months.

### The green / amber / red pain rule
Use this every session to decide whether to train, modify, or stop:

| Signal | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 🟢 **Green** | Normal muscular fatigue/effort in the legs, calves, or feet. Wobble and challenge, but full control. No joint pain or instability. | Train as prescribed. |
| 🟡 **Amber** | Mild ache/stiffness/"awareness" in a knee, ankle, Achilles, or shin that isn't sharp and settles quickly; balance noticeably worse than usual today; a surface that feels a touch beyond control. | Regress: firmer surface, more support, shallower depth, less/no load, bigger footholds. Do **not** progress. Reassess next session; if it persists across sessions, treat as red. |
| 🔴 **Red** | **Sharp or localized ankle or knee pain**; a roll/twist with swelling; a joint that gives way; pain that lingers or worsens after sessions; Achilles/heel pain that changes how you walk; any dizziness, faintness, or fall. | **Stop the session immediately.** No balance, single-leg loading, or on-wall drilling until fully symptom-free. Seek a qualified professional. Do not self-diagnose. |

### Mandatory rest & recovery spacing
- **At least 48 hours** between dedicated single-leg *strength* sessions — always. (Skill drills, being low-fatigue, can be more frequent.)
- Keep **at least 2 easier/rest days** per week during the block.
- Don't stack heavy leg strength on top of a big bouldering or leg-intensive climbing day — that's a lower-limb load overload.
- If knees, ankles, calves, or shins feel persistently sore, stiff, or unstable across days, that's under-recovery — take extra rest. Missing a session costs nothing; a rolled ankle or an irritated knee costs a season.

### A brief note on return-to-loading
> This program is for healthy, pain-free ankles, knees, hips, and backs and is **not** a rehabilitation protocol. If you've had an ankle sprain, a knee injury, a meniscus problem, Achilles/plantar issues, or any lower-limb injury, return to balance and single-leg loading **only** when a qualified professional has cleared you and you are symptom-free. When you do return, restart at **Phase 1** difficulty (firm surfaces, shallow loading, generous support), progress even more conservatively than usual, and treat any recurrence of symptoms or instability as an immediate stop. Genuine ankle/knee rehab is individual and progressive and belongs with a physiotherapist — not with a generic download.

## 9. Testing & Benchmarking

A standardized set of tests at the **start** and **end** of the block turns "I think my feet are better" into numbers. Keep every variable identical across both tests: same surfaces, same shoes, same wall/holds, same protocol, same warm-up, same time of day, same rested state.

### Standardized tests (run the same set at start and re-test)

> **Prerequisite:** only test after a complete warm-up (§5), and only in a safe, padded, supported environment. If balance is unusually poor that day, or anything in a joint feels off, postpone. A test is not worth a rolled ankle. Do balance tests near support and above a safe floor.

**Test A — Single-leg balance time (eyes open and eyes closed).**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Setup | Firm floor, shoes (or barefoot — pick one and keep it fixed), hands on hips or relaxed, other foot off the floor, near support but not touching it |
| Task | Balance as long as possible until you must touch down, touch support, or hop. Test **eyes open**, then (safely) **eyes closed**, each leg |
| Score | **Time in seconds**, each leg, each vision condition (cap at, say, 60 s eyes-open to keep it practical) |
| Conditions | Same surface, shoes, arm position, and warm-up at start and re-test |

**Test B — Single-leg squat depth & quality.**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Setup | Free space, optionally filming from the front and side |
| Task | Perform a controlled single-leg squat to your best clean depth (to a box/target if needed), each leg |
| Score | **Best clean depth reached** (or box height) **plus a quality note** — knee stayed over the foot, no cave-in, no wobble, controlled descent. A shallower *clean* rep beats a deep sloppy one |
| Conditions | Same target/box, shoes, and warm-up; judge quality the same way both times (film helps) |

**Test C — Calf-raise endurance count.**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Setup | Single-leg, standing tall, off a step for full range if available, fingertip support for balance only |
| Task | Full-range single-leg calf raises at a steady tempo (e.g., ~1 up / 1 down) until you can no longer reach full height cleanly |
| Score | **Number of clean full-range reps**, each leg |
| Conditions | Same step, tempo, and support at start and re-test |

**Test D — Standardized on-wall footwork-precision drill.**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Setup | A **fixed** easy-to-moderate slab/vertical route or circuit you can repeat exactly |
| Task | Climb it applying **one-touch / silent feet** — each foot must land correctly on the first touch, silently, with no re-adjust |
| Score | **Number of foot faults** (audible slaps + re-adjusts + missed placements) over the fixed route — *lower is better*; optionally note time to climb it cleanly |
| Conditions | Identical route, shoes, pace, and warm-up at start and re-test |

**Record** your scores plus how they felt and any quality notes. **Re-test in Week 6** after the light consolidation days, under identical conditions. Longer balance times, deeper/cleaner single-leg squats, more calf reps, and fewer foot faults are your objective results. No change is still useful data — it tells you whether to repeat the block, adjust practice quality, or change the stimulus.

> **Balance and single-leg tests are genuinely demanding and can be tiring.** Run the initial tests as your first session of Week 1, and don't also do a full strength session the same day.

## 10. Recovery Essentials

Balance and motor skills consolidate during rest — much of skill learning is "baked in" by sleep between practice sessions — and single-leg tissue adapts during recovery, not during the reps. Brief, evidence-aligned basics:

- **Sleep.** The highest-leverage recovery *and* skill-consolidation tool. Aim for **7–9 hours** of quality sleep. Motor-skill memories consolidate during sleep, and balance/coordination are sharply degraded by under-sleeping — a poor night shows up directly as worse footwork and wobblier balance. Under-sleeping also raises injury risk on unstable-surface work.
- **Session spacing.** **48+ hours** between dedicated single-leg strength sessions; 2+ easier/rest days per week. Skill drills, being low-fatigue, can be more frequent — but even they benefit from the overnight consolidation gap, so short daily doses beat one exhausting marathon.
- **Fuel the work.** Strength and connective-tissue adaptation need adequate total energy and **protein** (a common general guideline for active people is roughly **1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight per day**, spread across meals). This is general educational information, not individualized dietary advice.
- **Hydration.** Be well-hydrated for sessions; balance and coordination are subtly impaired when dehydrated.
- **Feet and skin.** Precise footwork depends on shoes that fit and feet that feel. Manage shoe fit and foot skin/toenails; a painful foot ruins precision as surely as a weak leg.
- **Listen to trend, not just the day.** Persistent knee/ankle/Achilles soreness, poor sleep, or balance that's off across several days means back off. Motor learning and tissue adaptation both suffer when you're run down — and fatigue is when balance work gets dangerous.

## 11. After Week 6

Finish the block, bank the gains, and choose a deliberate next step. Motor skills are durable — well-learned footwork sticks around far longer than a peak of endurance or strength — but the *physical* stability qualities (single-leg strength, calf endurance, balance) fade if never touched. So maintain the skill lightly and keep a minimal dose of the strength.

**Option A — Maintain by embedding it in your climbing.** Because footwork is a durable skill, the best maintenance is to **keep practising it inside normal climbing** — a couple of silent-feet or precise-placement drills in every warm-up, and continued *attention* to your feet on every route. Add **one short single-leg strength / balance session per week** to hold the physical stability. This is the ideal follow-on for most climbers: footwork becomes a permanent habit, not a project.

**Option B — Take a lighter week, then a new focus.** Run a lighter week (easy climbing, warm-up drills only), then pivot to a complementary quality — finger strength (*Contact Strength*), power-endurance (*Anaerobic Capacity*), or body tension — while keeping a footwork drill or two in your warm-ups so the skill doesn't go stale. The lower body gets a relative break while you raise another quality.

**Option C — Repeat the block, progressed.** After at least a **lighter/recovery week**, re-run the 6 weeks from your **new** baseline (re-test numbers). Progress by using smaller/worse footholds, steeper terrain, harder balance surfaces, deeper or loaded single-leg strength, and more pressure in the integration phase — but keep the same conservative, one-variable-at-a-time discipline and the same rule that *control leads difficulty*. Don't chase novelty at the expense of clean movement.

> Whichever you choose, keep the footwork drills in your warm-ups and the ankle/foot prehab in your routine year-round — footwork is a skill you *keep* by practising, and the ankles especially will thank you. And keep testing periodically under the same standard — long-term trend is what tells you your training is working.

## 12. Sources & Further Reading

Genuine, well-known work in climbing technique, motor learning, and lower-limb training. Consult the original sources directly; the notes below are plain-language pointers, not exact quotations or specific numeric claims.

- **Dave MacLeod, *9 out of 10 Climbers Make the Same Mistakes*** and ***Make or Break*** — accessible, well-regarded books on climbing performance, technique, and injury prevention; MacLeod is especially strong on footwork, movement efficiency, and why over-gripping and poor feet limit climbers.
- **Eric Hörst, *Training for Climbing* and *Learning to Climb Indoors*, and the Training4Climbing resources** — widely used practical guides that cover footwork technique, balance, precise foot placement, and skill practice for climbers.
- **Steve Bechtel / Climb Strong** — applied strength-and-conditioning material for climbers, including single-leg and general lower-body strength within climbing programming.
- **Motor-learning literature (e.g., Richard Schmidt & Timothy Lee, *Motor Learning and Performance*; work by Gabriele Wulf on attentional focus)** — foundational, well-established sources on how motor skills are acquired: deliberate practice, quality of repetition, feedback, and the benefits of an *external* focus of attention for skill learning.
- **Balance & proprioception / ankle-stability research (e.g., work on proprioceptive and balance training for ankle-sprain prevention, and single-leg-balance testing)** — the general evidence base that balance and proprioception are trainable and that ankle-stability work reduces sprain risk.
- **Single-leg strength & the tibialis/calf** — general strength-and-conditioning principles on unilateral lower-limb training, progressive overload, and calf/tibialis conditioning for foot and ankle robustness (as covered in standard resistance-training references and CSEP resources).
- **Volker Schöffl and colleagues** — the leading body of clinical research on **climbing injuries**, including lower-limb and knee injuries in climbers (e.g., mechanisms around drop-knee/heel-hook loading) worth understanding before chasing deep, torqued knee positions.
- **PAR-Q+ (2023) and the ePARmed-X+, © the PAR-Q+ Collaboration** — the physical-activity readiness screening referenced throughout this program.
- **CSEP (Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology)** — evidence-based physical activity, resistance-training, balance, and sleep guidance underpinning the general training and recovery principles here.

> These are starting points for going deeper — not a claim that this program reproduces any specific study's protocol. Where you see a number in this manual, treat it as an educational default to individualize, not a research finding.
