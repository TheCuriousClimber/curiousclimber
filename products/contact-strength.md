## 1. Overview

**Contact strength** is the ability to generate near-maximal grip force the instant your fingers touch a hold — the difference between latching a small edge and sliding off it. **Finger power** and **maximal grip force** are its close cousins: how much force your finger flexors can produce, and how fast they can produce it.

This program develops those qualities through **structured, progressive isometric finger loading** (primarily hangboard-based), supported by pulling strength and antagonist/prehab work. Over six weeks it moves you through three deliberate phases — anatomical adaptation, maximal strength, and peaking/expression — so that connective tissue adapts before intensity climbs, and force is expressed on the wall by the end.

**Who it's for:** intermediate climbers with a solid training base who want a defensible, no-nonsense finger-strength block. It is scalable from lower-intermediate to advanced through objective load and edge-depth adjustments.

**Expected outcome (with consistent execution and adequate recovery):** measurable gains in maximal added-load hang capacity and minimum-edge capacity, improved recruitment and "snap" onto small holds, and a repeatable, safe framework you can re-run. Finger strength adapts slowly; think in blocks and seasons, not sessions.

> **What this program is *not*.** It is not a rehab plan, not a power-endurance/pump program, not a campus or dynamic-contact protocol, and not a substitute for climbing. It is one focused strength block that sits *inside* a broader climbing life.

## 2. Prerequisites & Readiness

Finger strength is the slowest-adapting and most injury-prone quality in climbing. Gating who runs this — and when — is the single most important safety decision in the whole program.

### Minimum training age before dedicated finger loading

| Criterion | Minimum standard before starting |
|---|---|
| **Consistent climbing history** | At least **12 months** of regular climbing (2+ sessions/week). Tendons and pulleys adapt on the order of months, and mostly through climbing itself, before dedicated hangboarding is justified. |
| **Grade base** | Comfortably climbing around **V3–V4 / 5.10+–5.11** or harder. This is a guideline, not a gate on ambition — it signals your tissues have seen meaningful finger load. |
| **General strength base** | Can perform controlled pull-ups and hang from a bar/large edge for 30+ seconds pain-free. |
| **Symptom-free** | No current finger, hand, wrist, elbow, or shoulder pain or "niggles." |
| **Age** | Skeletally mature. Finger loading is **not** appropriate for still-growing adolescents, whose growth plates are vulnerable to specific finger-loading injuries. |

### Readiness reminder (PAR-Q)

Before any new training block, complete a **Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q+)**. If you answer "yes" to any item — or you are over 40 and inactive, pregnant/postpartum, or have a heart, blood-pressure, metabolic, joint, or bone condition — clear vigorous exercise with a physician or qualified professional first.

### Who should NOT run this yet

> **Do not start this program if you:**
> - Have less than a year of consistent climbing, or are still building a base.
> - Are an adolescent who has not finished growing.
> - Have **any** current or recent finger, pulley, wrist, elbow, or shoulder injury or pain — even mild. Finger strength work loads the exact tissues that need to be quiet to heal. Seek a qualified professional; return to loading only when cleared and symptom-free.
> - Are in a high-volume climbing peak, competition prep, or an unusually stressful, sleep-deprived life period. Finger strength requires recovery you won't have.
> - Have never hangboarded and are unsure of grip mechanics — spend a few weeks doing light, submaximal hangs to learn the positions first.
>
> There is no rush. A finger injury costs months. Waiting costs weeks.

## 3. The Science in Plain Language

### Why contact/finger strength matters

Climbing force starts at the fingers. If your fingers can't hold the hold, nothing else — power, technique, tension — gets to contribute. Maximal finger strength is one of the most reliable physical predictors of climbing performance across the literature, and **contact strength** (getting to that force *fast*, on first touch) is what lets you hold small or bad edges in motion rather than only in a static hang.

### Neural adaptations vs. hypertrophy

Early strength gains — especially in the first weeks of a block — are largely **neural**: your nervous system learns to recruit more motor units, fire them faster, and coordinate the finger flexors more efficiently. This is why you can get meaningfully stronger without your forearms getting visibly bigger, and why heavy, low-volume, high-quality efforts (with full recovery between them) are the right tool for finger strength.

**Hypertrophy** (actual growth of muscle and connective-tissue cross-section) is slower and follows more sustained, higher-volume loading over months. It matters for the long run, but it is not the primary driver of a focused 6-week strength block. This program leans neural: heavy, precise, well-rested efforts, with just enough volume in the early phase to build tissue tolerance.

Crucially, **muscle adapts faster than tendon and pulley**. Your ability to *pull hard* can outrun your connective tissue's ability to *tolerate* that pull. This mismatch is the root of most finger injuries — and the reason the first two weeks of this program deliberately hold intensity back while tissues catch up.

### Relevant anatomy (the legible version)

- **Finger flexors.** Two muscle groups in the forearm do the gripping: the **flexor digitorum profundus (FDP)** bends the fingertip joint, and the **flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS)** bends the middle joint. Their long tendons run down the forearm and into each finger. When you crimp, these are the engines.
- **Flexor tendons.** These cords transmit force from forearm muscle to fingertip. They are strong but adapt slowly and don't like sudden spikes in load.
- **The annular pulleys (A2 and A4).** As the flexor tendons cross each finger joint, a series of fibrous bands — pulleys — hold them snug against the bone, like the guides on a fishing rod that keep the line against the pole. The **A2 pulley** (over the base of the finger) and the **A4 pulley** (over the middle segment) take the most load. In a **crimp**, the sharp finger-joint angle dramatically increases the force pulling the tendon away from the bone — and the A2 pulley bears the brunt. This is the single most commonly injured structure in climbing.

> **The one-sentence takeaway:** you get stronger mostly by teaching your nervous system to recruit hard, but you get *injured* by asking your pulleys and tendons to tolerate that force before they've adapted — so the whole art is loading hard **and** ramping conservatively at the same time.

## 4. Equipment Needed

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Hangboard / fingerboard** | A quality wooden or textured board with a range of edge depths. Wood is gentler on skin and joints and is preferred for repeated max work. |
| **A ~20 mm edge** | The standard training and testing edge. Comfortable, flat, positive. This is your default. |
| **Smaller edges (e.g., ~14–15 mm, ~10 mm)** | For minimum-edge work in the later phase. Only if you can use them in a clean half-crimp with no pain. |
| **A means to add load** | Weight belt / harness with hangboard pulley or weight-plate carabiner, a loading pin, or a backpack with plates. You must be able to add load in small, repeatable increments (2.5–5 kg / 5–10 lb steps). |
| **A means to remove load (assistance)** | A resistance band, pulley counterweight system, or the ability to use two feet on a chair/step — for scaling *down* on bad days or when you're not yet at bodyweight max hangs. |
| **A reliable timer** | Interval timer (phone app is fine) that clearly signals hang and rest windows. Consistency of timing is part of the stimulus. |
| **A scale** | To weigh yourself and your added load, so intensity is tracked honestly session to session. |
| **A notebook or app** | Log every session: edge, added/removed load, times, RPE, and how it felt. What isn't tracked can't be progressed safely. |
| *(Optional)* **A force/hangboard load-cell device** | If you own one, it makes testing and auto-regulation more precise. Not required. |

> **Setup note.** Mount the board solidly at a height where you hang with **arms straight or nearly straight and shoulders engaged** (not passively hanging off the joints). Have a chair/step positioned so you can load and unload without a jarring drop-on or drop-off.

## 5. The Warm-Up Protocol (Every Session)

Never do a hard finger effort cold. A full warm-up is non-negotiable and takes about 15–20 minutes. It has three stages: raise the pulse, mobilize, then progressively load the fingers.

### Stage 1 — Pulse raise (3–5 min)
Get blood moving and body temperature up: easy cardio (skipping, brisk walk/jog, jumping jacks) or a few minutes of easy traversing. You want to feel warm, not tired.

### Stage 2 — Mobility & activation (3–5 min)
- Wrist circles and gentle wrist flexion/extension stretches.
- Finger flexion/extension: make fists and fully open the hands, 15–20 times; splay fingers wide.
- Shoulder and scapular activation: band pull-aparts, scapular pulls / shrugs on the bar, a few slow scap pull-ups. Healthy shoulders protect the whole chain.
- Light forearm/wrist **extensor** activation (wrist curls with a light band or rubber-band finger extensions) to wake the antagonists.

### Stage 3 — Progressive finger-loading ramp (8–10 min)
This is the most important part. Bring the fingers up to working intensity in gradual, submaximal steps on your **half-crimp** or **open** grip, resting fully between. Never jump straight to a heavy effort.

| Ramp step | Load / effort | Duration | Rest after |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Very light — big hold or large edge, feet assisting, ~30–40% effort | 10 s | 1 min |
| 2 | Light — working edge (e.g., 20 mm), bodyweight or slightly assisted, ~50% effort | 8–10 s | 1–2 min |
| 3 | Moderate — working edge, near bodyweight, ~65–70% effort | 7–8 s | 2 min |
| 4 | Working ramp — working edge, add a little load, ~80% of the day's target | 5–7 s | 2–3 min |
| *(then)* | Begin the session's prescribed work sets | — | — |

> **Warm-up rule.** If anything in the ramp feels sharp, pinchy, or "off" in a finger, **stop the session.** A warm-up that doesn't feel clean is information, not an obstacle to push through.

## 6. The 6-Week Periodized Schedule

### Phase logic at a glance

| Phase | Weeks | Primary goal | Loading character | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1 — Anatomical adaptation & technique** | 1–2 | Build tissue tolerance; groove clean half-crimp positions; establish baselines | Moderate intensity, slightly higher volume, longer hangs (repeaters/density) | Muscle outpaces tendon/pulley; this phase lets connective tissue and grip mechanics catch up before heavy work. |
| **Phase 2 — Maximal strength** | 3–4 | Maximal recruitment and force | Heavy, low volume, near-maximal short hangs (max hangs), full recovery | Neural gains come from high-quality, well-rested max efforts. |
| **Phase 3 — Peaking & expression** | 5–6 | Express strength fast; contact strength on the wall; peak, then taper | Heavy but low total volume; minimum-edge + on-wall transfer; Week 6 deload/re-test | Convert raw strength into usable contact strength, then dissipate fatigue so gains show up. |

**Sessions per week:** **2 dedicated finger sessions**, always separated by at least **48 hours** (e.g., Mon/Thu or Tue/Fri). This spacing is a hard rule — connective tissue recovers slower than muscle.

> **Fit around climbing, don't stack on top of it.** Do finger sessions when fresh, ideally at the *start* of a climbing session (after warm-up) or on their own day. Keep total hard finger exposure — hangboard **plus** hard bouldering — modest, especially in weeks 1–2. If a week is unusually stressful or under-slept, take the lighter option or an extra rest day. Recovery is not optional; it is where the adaptation happens.

### Week 1 — Phase 1: Anatomical adaptation

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Finger A | Full warm-up → **Repeaters** (see §7), moderate intensity → light supportive pulling → antagonist/prehab |
| *(48h+ rest)* | | |
| Day 2 | Finger B | Full warm-up → **Repeaters**, same moderate intensity → light supportive pulling → antagonist/prehab |
| Other days | Climb / rest | Easy-to-moderate climbing OK; avoid maximal finger-intensive bouldering. At least 2 full rest days. |

**Focus:** learn the positions, keep loads conservative (RPE ~6–7), zero symptoms. Establish your baseline test at the start of the week (see §9).

### Week 2 — Phase 1: Anatomical adaptation (progressed)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Finger A | Warm-up → **Repeaters**, slightly higher intensity or +1 set vs. Week 1 → supportive pulling → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 2 | Finger B | Warm-up → **Repeaters**, matched → supportive pulling → antagonist/prehab |

**Progression:** add a small amount of load **or** one set relative to Week 1 — not both. Loads should still feel controlled (RPE ~7). If Week 1 left any soreness in the fingers/tendons beyond normal muscular fatigue, repeat Week 1 loads instead.

### Week 3 — Phase 2: Maximal strength

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Max A | Warm-up (thorough) → **Max hangs**, heavy, low volume (see §7) → heavier supportive pulling → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 2 | Max B | Warm-up → **Max hangs**, matched → supportive pulling → antagonist/prehab |

**Shift:** volume drops, intensity rises. Short near-maximal hangs with **long, full rests**. Quality over quantity — every rep should be crisp. Target RPE ~8 (RIR ~2), never grinding failure on the fingers.

### Week 4 — Phase 2: Maximal strength (progressed)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Max A | Warm-up → **Max hangs**, +small load vs. Week 3 if Week 3 was clean → supportive pulling → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 2 | Max B | Warm-up → **Max hangs**, matched → supportive pulling → antagonist/prehab |

**Progression:** add load before adding sets. Increase added weight by the smallest increment that keeps hangs at RPE ~8. If you can't add load while keeping quality, hold Week 3's numbers — maintaining heavy, clean work is a win.

### Week 5 — Phase 3: Peaking & expression

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Peak A | Warm-up → **Max hangs** (heavy, very low volume) *or* **minimum-edge hangs** at bodyweight → **on-wall contact-strength transfer** (see §7) → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 2 | Peak B | Warm-up → the *other* option from Day 1 → on-wall transfer → antagonist/prehab |

**Focus:** express strength. Keep total finger volume low and intensity high but clean. Add short, sharp on-wall work — hard moves on small holds, first-touch latching — to convert raw strength into contact strength. Do the on-wall work while fresh; stop while still crisp.

### Week 6 — Phase 3: Peak, taper & re-test

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Sharpen | Light-to-moderate warm-up + a few crisp, submaximal-to-moderate hangs to stay primed. **Low volume. No grinding.** |
| *(2–3 days rest)* | | Rest and eat and sleep well. This is the taper — fatigue drops, strength surfaces. |
| Day 2 | **Re-test** | Full warm-up → repeat the **standardized max-hang / added-weight test** from §9 under the same conditions → record. Then a couple of easy climbing days. |

> **Week 6 is a deload built into the plan.** Do not add load this week. The point is to *shed fatigue* so the strength you built in Weeks 1–5 shows up in the re-test — and so you finish the block fresh, not frayed.

### Auto-regulation rule (all weeks)

Prescriptions are targets, not orders. Each session, gate intensity on how the warm-up ramp felt and your recent sleep/stress:
- **Feels strong, ramp crisp:** train the prescribed load (progress if scheduled).
- **Feels flat / under-recovered:** hold load, cut a set, or drop to the lighter option.
- **Any finger symptom, or ramp feels "off":** stop. Rest, reassess next session. See §8.

## 7. Detailed Prescriptions by Method

All hangs use a **half-crimp** grip as the default (see §8 for why), on your working edge, with **shoulders engaged** (active, not passive hanging). "Added load" is expressed as a percentage of your bodyweight-plus-load working max where useful, but the honest day-to-day gauge is **RPE / RIR** on the warmed-up fingers.

> **How to read RPE/RIR here:** RPE 8 ≈ 2 reps/seconds in reserve (RIR 2) — hard, clean, you could have held a bit longer. **We never train finger hangs to true failure.** The last second of a max hang should be *controllable*, not a slipping, clawing grind.

### Method 1 — Repeaters (Phase 1: anatomical adaptation & density)

Sub-maximal, longer time-under-tension to build tissue tolerance and work capacity.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Grip / edge | Half-crimp on ~20 mm working edge |
| Work : rest per rep | **7 s hang / 3 s rest** |
| Reps per set | 6 reps (≈ one minute of work per set) |
| Sets | 3–5 sets (start 3 in Wk 1; progress toward 5) |
| Intensity | Moderate — RPE 6–7. Bodyweight, assisted, or lightly loaded so the *last rep of the set* is challenging but clean |
| Grips trained | Half-crimp primary; optionally add open-hand as a second, lighter block |
| Tempo | Controlled tension on, no dropping/snatching onto the edge |
| Rest between sets | **2–3 minutes** |
| Progression | Wk 1 → Wk 2: add one set **or** small load; keep RPE ≤ 7 |

### Method 2 — Max hangs (Phase 2 & 3: maximal strength)

Short, heavy, near-maximal isometrics with full recovery — the core strength driver.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Grip / edge | Half-crimp on ~20 mm working edge |
| Hang duration | **7–10 s** per effort |
| Reps (efforts) | 4–5 per session |
| Sets structure | Single efforts, each fully rested (i.e., 4–5 total hangs), OR 2 clusters of 2–3 |
| Intensity | Heavy — RPE 8 (RIR ~2). Add load so 7–10 s is genuinely hard but the last second is controlled |
| Added load | Whatever brings you to RPE 8 at 7–10 s (for many climbers this is +10–30% bodyweight; **your** number is whatever hits the target cleanly) |
| Tempo | Full, deliberate tension; no swinging or re-gripping mid-hang |
| Rest between efforts | **3 minutes minimum** (up to 5) — recovery *is* the method |
| Progression | Wk 3 → Wk 4: add the smallest load increment that keeps RPE 8; load before volume |

### Method 3 — Minimum-edge hangs (Phase 3: expression)

Bodyweight hangs on the smallest edge you can hold cleanly — trains recruitment and translates directly to small holds. **Only introduce if half-crimp on small edges is completely pain-free.**

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Grip / edge | Half-crimp on the **smallest edge** you can hold at RPE 8 for the duration (e.g., ~14–15 mm; ~10 mm only for advanced, symptom-free hands) |
| Load | **Bodyweight** (reduce edge size rather than adding weight) |
| Hang duration | 7–10 s |
| Efforts | 3–5 |
| Intensity | RPE 8, clean |
| Rest between efforts | **3 minutes minimum** |
| Progression | Reduce edge depth only when the current edge is clean and controlled at full duration |

> **Caution.** Small edges concentrate load on the pulleys. Progress edge depth **slowly** and never chase a smaller edge on a day the fingers feel off.

### Method 4 — Added-weight hangs (test & heavy strength)

Mechanically the same as max hangs but on the standard test edge with load as the variable — used for the benchmark test (§9) and as the heavy option in Weeks 3–5.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Grip / edge | Half-crimp, ~20 mm test edge |
| Hang duration | ~7–10 s (test uses a standardized duration — see §9) |
| Load | Progressively added weight |
| Efforts | Per test protocol, or 3–4 as a training set |
| Rest | 3–5 minutes between heavy efforts |

### Method 5 — Supportive pulling strength

Keeps the pulling chain (lats, arms, shoulders) matched to your growing finger strength, and reinforces the grip under a pulling demand. Done *after* finger work, never before.

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Intensity | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weighted or bodyweight **pull-ups** | 3–4 × 4–6 | RPE 8 (RIR 2), full ROM, controlled | 2–3 min |
| **Frenchies / lock-offs** (isometric holds at bent-arm angles) *(optional)* | 2–3 sets | Controlled, no straining through the fingers | 2–3 min |
| **Ring rows / heavy inverted rows** (horizontal pull) | 2–3 × 6–10 | RPE 7–8 | 2 min |

> Keep pulling volume modest — it supports the finger work, it doesn't compete with it. If pulling is eating your recovery, cut it back.

### Method 6 — Antagonist & prehab (every session, ~8–10 min)

Balances the intense finger flexion with the muscles that stabilize and protect the joints. Do at the **end** of every finger session (and it's fine on rest days too).

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wrist extensor curls** (light dumbbell/band) | 2–3 × 12–15 | Forearm extensors | Slow, full range |
| **Finger extensions** (rubber band around fingertips, open the hand) | 2–3 × 15–20 | Finger extensors / intrinsics | Balances the flexors |
| **Reverse wrist curls / pronation-supination** | 2 × 12–15 | Forearm balance | Light load |
| **Scapular / rotator-cuff work** (band external rotations, scap pull-aparts) | 2–3 × 12–15 | Shoulder health | Protects the whole chain |
| **Wrist / pronator prehab (e.g., wrist wide/curls)** *(optional)* | 2 × 12–15 | Elbow/wrist tendon health | Useful if you're prone to elbow tendinopathy |

## 8. Injury Prevention & Tendon Health

> **This is the most important section in the program. Read it fully before your first session, and re-read it whenever the fingers feel off. No performance goal is worth a torn pulley.**

### Safe load ramping — the core principle
Muscle gets strong faster than tendon and pulley adapt. Every rule below exists to stop your *pulling ability* from outrunning your *tissue tolerance*:
- **Never skip the progressive finger-loading ramp** (§5). Cold or half-warm fingers are how pulleys get hurt.
- **Progress load in small increments** (2.5–5 kg / 5–10 lb, or one edge size at a time), and only when the previous load felt clean.
- **Load before volume** for strength, but ramp *either* conservatively.
- **Honor the phase order.** Weeks 1–2 exist to prepare tissue for Weeks 3–6. Do not jump straight to max hangs because you feel strong.

### Grip-type cautions
- **Half-crimp is the default** for this program. It's strong, trainable, and — with the fingertip joint kept from collapsing — distributes load more sensibly than a full crimp.
- **Open-hand / three-finger drag** is generally the gentlest on the pulleys and a good option for a second, lighter grip block.
- **Full crimp (thumb wrapped, fingertip joint hyperextended)** places the **highest strain on the A2 pulley** of any common grip. **This program does not train full-crimp hangs.** Build your foundational finger strength in half-crimp and open-hand; let full-crimp strength come from careful climbing, not loaded hangs.

> **Never let a half-crimp collapse into a passive full crimp under fatigue.** If you can't hold the position actively, the effort is over.

### Pulley-strain warning signs — what a "tweak" feels like
A pulley injury often announces itself. Learn the signals:
- A sudden **sharp pain** at the base or middle of a finger, sometimes with an audible or felt **"pop" or "crack."**
- Localized **pain, tenderness, or swelling** over the front of a finger segment.
- Pain when you crimp or load that finger; sometimes a feeling of the tendon "bowstringing" away from the bone in severe cases.
- Even a mild, nagging soreness right over the A2 region that shows up during or after loading is a **yellow flag** — treat it seriously.

**If you feel a pop or sharp finger pain: stop immediately. Do not "test it" with another hang.** This program cannot diagnose or treat you — seek a qualified professional (physician or physiotherapist) before loading that finger again.

### The green / amber / red pain rule
Use this every session to decide whether to train, modify, or stop:

| Signal | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 🟢 **Green** | Normal muscular fatigue/pump in the forearm. No joint, tendon, or pulley pain. | Train as prescribed. |
| 🟡 **Amber** | Mild ache, stiffness, or "awareness" in a finger/tendon/pulley that is not sharp and settles quickly. | Reduce load, drop to the lighter/assisted option or a bigger edge, cut volume. Do **not** progress. Reassess next session; if it persists, treat as red. |
| 🔴 **Red** | Sharp, localized, or pinpoint finger/pulley pain; a pop; swelling; pain that lingers or worsens after sessions; pain that changes how you grip. | **Stop the session immediately.** No hangboarding until fully symptom-free. Seek a qualified professional. Do not self-diagnose. |

### Mandatory rest & recovery spacing
- **At least 48 hours** between finger sessions — always. Connective tissue recovers slower than muscle.
- Keep **at least 2 full rest days** per week during the block.
- Don't stack a hard finger session on top of a maximal bouldering session, or on top of poor sleep and high life-stress. Total finger *exposure* is what counts.
- If fingers feel persistently tired, stiff, or achy across multiple days, that's under-recovery — take extra rest. Missing a session costs nothing; training injured costs months.

### A brief note on return-to-loading
> This program is for healthy, pain-free fingers and is **not** a rehabilitation protocol. If you've had a finger injury, return to loading **only** when a qualified professional has cleared you and you are symptom-free. When you do return, restart at **Phase 1** intensities (or lower), progress even more conservatively than a healthy climber would, and treat any recurrence of symptoms as an immediate stop. Rehab and return-to-sport programming after a genuine pulley injury is individual and belongs with a physiotherapist — not with a generic download.

## 9. Testing & Benchmarking

A standardized test at the **start** and **end** of the block turns "I think I got stronger" into a number. Keep every variable identical across both tests: same edge, same duration, same grip, same warm-up, same time of day, same rested state.

### Standardized max added-load hang test

> **Prerequisite:** only test after a complete warm-up (§5), and only if the ramp felt clean. If anything feels off, postpone the test — it is not worth an injury.

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Edge | Your ~20 mm working/test edge |
| Grip | Half-crimp, shoulders engaged |
| Hang duration | **7 seconds** (hold this constant for both tests) |
| Goal | Find the **maximum added load** you can hold in a clean 7 s half-crimp |
| Method | After the warm-up ramp, do 7 s hangs adding load in small increments, resting **3–5 min** between attempts. Stop when you reach a load you can *just* hold cleanly for 7 s at ~RPE 9 — no grinding, no collapsing grip. That load is your score. |
| Attempts | Keep it to a few quality attempts; don't grind out ten near-max efforts and fry your fingers |
| Record | Bodyweight + added load (e.g., "72 kg BW + 20 kg = 92 kg total, 7 s @ 20 mm") plus how it felt |

**Optional secondary benchmark:** minimum edge you can hold at bodyweight for 7 s in a clean half-crimp (record the edge depth). Useful for climbers whose gains show up as edge size rather than added kilos.

**Re-test in Week 6** after the taper, under identical conditions. An improvement in maximum added load (or a smaller minimum edge held) is your objective result. No change, or a small change, is still useful data — it tells you whether to repeat the block, adjust recovery, or change the stimulus.

> **Testing is a hard finger effort.** Count the initial test as your first loaded session of Week 1, and don't also do a full repeaters session the same day.

## 10. Recovery Essentials

Strength is built during recovery, not during the hang. Brief, evidence-aligned basics:

- **Sleep.** The highest-leverage recovery tool. Aim for **7–9 hours** of quality sleep. Tissue repair and neural consolidation both depend on it; under-sleeping blunts adaptation and raises injury risk.
- **Session spacing.** Covered above and worth repeating: **48+ hours** between finger sessions, 2+ rest days per week. Respect it even when you feel fine.
- **Protein & nutrition.** Support adaptation with adequate overall energy and protein — a common general guideline for active people is roughly **1.6–2.2 g of protein per kg of bodyweight per day**, spread across meals. Eat enough total food to support training; chronic under-eating undermines both tissue health and strength gains. This is general educational information, not individualized dietary advice.
- **Hydration & general health.** Be well-hydrated for sessions; manage overall life stress, which competes for the same recovery capacity.
- **Skin & warmth.** Keep tips healthy (file glassy calluses, moisturize) and fingers warm before loading — cold tissue is more injury-prone.
- **Listen to trend, not just the day.** Persistent fatigue, poor sleep, or lingering finger stiffness across several days means back off. Recovery deficits accumulate silently in connective tissue.

---

## 11. After Week 6

Finish the block, bank the gains, and choose a deliberate next step rather than grinding on indefinitely — finger strength blocks are not meant to run back-to-back forever.

**Option A — Maintain.** Drop to **one lighter max-hang session per week** and let climbing express the strength. Maintenance takes far less volume than building. Ideal if you now want to focus on projecting, power-endurance, or a climbing trip.

**Option B — Take a down-week, then a new focus.** Run a lighter/recovery week (easy climbing, minimal or no hangboarding), then pivot to a complementary quality — power, power-endurance/capacity, or technique — so the fingers get a relative break while you keep progressing overall.

**Option C — Repeat the block, progressed.** After at least a **light/recovery week**, re-run the 6 weeks starting from your **new** baseline (re-test numbers). Progress by adding load, reducing edge size, or nudging volume in Phase 1 — but keep the same conservative ramp discipline. Don't run this or a similar heavy finger block continuously; cycle it with other qualities and lighter periods across your training year.

> Whichever you choose, keep antagonist/prehab work and warm-ups in your routine year-round. And keep testing periodically under the same standard — long-term trend is what tells you your training is working.

## 12. Sources & Further Reading

Genuine, well-known work in climbing science and training. Consult the original sources directly; the notes below are plain-language pointers, not exact quotations or specific numeric claims.

- **Eva López-Rivera and colleagues** — research and applied writing on hangboard training methods, comparing **maximal added-weight hangs** vs. **minimum-edge** approaches for finger strength and endurance. A foundational reference for structured hangboarding.
- **Volker Schöffl and colleagues** — the leading body of clinical research on **climbing injuries**, including the anatomy and grading of **annular pulley (A2/A4) injuries**, mechanisms, and epidemiology. The standard medical reference for understanding finger injury in climbers.
- **Dave MacLeod, *Make or Break: Don't Let Climbing Injuries Dictate Your Success*** — accessible, well-regarded book on climbing injury prevention and management, including fingers, pulleys, and load management.
- **Eric Hörst, *Training for Climbing* and the Training4Climbing resources** — widely used practical guides to periodized climbing training, including finger-strength methods and warm-ups.
- **Steven Low, *Overcoming Gravity*** — thorough reference on progression, programming principles, and connective-tissue adaptation for bodyweight and isometric-heavy training (broadly applicable to hangboarding).
- **Lattice Training and related applied-science practitioners** — publicly available educational material on finger-strength assessment, standardized testing, and structured training for climbers.
- **PAR-Q+ (2023) and the ePARmed-X+, © the PAR-Q+ Collaboration** — the physical-activity readiness screening referenced throughout this program.
- **CSEP (Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology)** — evidence-based physical activity, resistance-training, and sleep guidance underpinning the general recovery and training principles here.

> These are starting points for going deeper — not a claim that this program reproduces any specific study's protocol. Where you see a number in this manual, treat it as an educational default to individualize, not a research finding.
