## 1. Overview

**Body tension** is the quality that lets a climber stay *connected* to steep rock: the ability to keep the feet on the wall and transmit force in a straight, unbroken line from the hands, through a stiff trunk, and out through the toes and heels. On an overhang, gravity is constantly trying to peel your feet off and swing your hips out from the wall. Body tension is what resists that — it's the difference between weighting a small foothold and pushing off it, versus feeling your feet cut and your whole body sag into a barn-door swing.

This program develops that quality through **structured, progressive trunk-strength and whole-body-tension training**, layered from foundational core control up through maximal-tension strength to climbing-specific application on steep terrain. Over six weeks it moves you through three deliberate phases — core control and tension basics, maximal tension strength, and climbing-specific tension peaking — so that you build the raw trunk stiffness and straight-arm strength *before* you ask your body to express it on hard, steep, foot-cutting climbing.

**How this differs from its siblings.** This is **not** a finger-strength program (that is *Contact Strength & Finger Power*, which trains how *hard* your fingers can pull in a brief effort). Nor is it a power-endurance program (that is *Anaerobic Capacity for Long Routes*, which trains how long you can tolerate the forearm pump). Body-tension training targets the **trunk and the kinetic-chain connection** — the abdominals, obliques, hip flexors, and posterior chain — and the straight-arm strength that links hands to feet. It trains neither the fingers nor the pump. Think of finger strength as your grip, endurance as your staying power, and body tension as the *chassis* that connects them: without a stiff chassis, a strong grip and a fresh forearm still can't hold your feet on steep rock.

**Who it's for:** intermediate-and-up climbers who feel strong in the hands but "come off" on steep ground — whose feet cut unintentionally, whose hips sag away from overhangs, who struggle to hold body position on roofs, toe-hooks, and heel-hooks, or who can't seem to push through their feet when the wall tilts back. It is scalable through lever length, wall angle, foot-hold size, and range of motion.

**Expected outcome (with consistent execution and adequate recovery):** a measurably stiffer, more connected trunk; better ability to keep feet on and generate tension on steep terrain; stronger, more controlled straight-arm positions; and a repeatable framework you can re-run before a steep-climbing season. Trunk strength and tension are relatively durable qualities — they build steadily and detrain slowly compared with the pump — so a focused block pays off for a long time.

> **What this program is *not*.** It is not a rehab plan, not a finger-strength or power-endurance protocol, not a "six-pack aesthetics" routine, and not a substitute for climbing on real steep terrain. It is one focused body-tension block that sits *inside* a broader climbing life.

## 2. Prerequisites & Readiness

Body-tension training loads different tissues than a finger or endurance block. Its injury risk is not the pulley or the elbow tendon — it is the **lumbar spine** (from uncontrolled leg raises and loss of bracing), the **hip flexors** (from loaded straight-leg work), and the **shoulders** (from straight-arm, high-force front-lever positions). Gating who runs this, and on what base, is the most important safety decision in the program.

### Minimum training base before dedicated body-tension work

| Criterion | Minimum standard before starting |
|---|---|
| **Consistent climbing history** | At least **6–12 months** of regular climbing (2+ sessions/week), including some time on steeper-than-vertical terrain. Straight-arm and trunk loading needs a body already accustomed to hanging and pulling. |
| **Grade base** | Comfortably climbing around **V2–V3 / 5.10** or harder, and able to climb on gently overhanging terrain without immediately losing your feet. This signals the basic coordination the program refines. |
| **Baseline core control** | Able to hold a clean **front plank for 45–60 s** and a basic **hollow-body hold for ~20 s** with a flat lower back. If you can't yet brace and keep the lumbar spine neutral, start with the Phase-1 basics and progress slowly. |
| **Pulling & hanging base** | Able to **dead-hang from a bar for 30 s** and perform a few controlled pull-ups. Front-lever progressions demand healthy, capable shoulders and a functioning pulling base. |
| **Symptom-free** | No current lower-back, hip, groin, shoulder, elbow, wrist, or finger pain or "niggles" — especially no history of disc-related back pain, hip-flexor strain, or shoulder instability that flares with straight-arm loading. |
| **Age** | Skeletally mature. Heavy straight-arm and spinal-loading work is **not** appropriate for still-growing adolescents. |

### Readiness reminder (PAR-Q)

Before any new training block, complete a **Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q+)**. If you answer "yes" to any item — or you are over 40 and inactive, pregnant/postpartum, or have a heart, blood-pressure, metabolic, joint, bone, or back condition — clear vigorous exercise with a physician or qualified professional first.

### Who should NOT run this yet

> **Do not start this program if you:**
> - Have very little climbing experience, or cannot yet hold a basic braced plank and hollow-body position. Build that base first — the Phase-1 basics are a start, but if bracing itself is a struggle, spend longer there.
> - Are an adolescent who has not finished growing.
> - Have **any** current or recent lower-back, disc, hip, groin, shoulder, elbow, wrist, or finger injury or pain — even mild. This block loads exactly those tissues; heavy leg raises and front-lever work are provocative for an irritable back, hip, or shoulder. Seek a qualified professional; return to loading only when cleared and symptom-free.
> - Have a history of **low-back pain that flares with flexion, extension, or loaded core work** — get it assessed first, and treat any recurrence during this block as an immediate stop.
> - Are already deep in a heavy competition prep or an unusually stressful, sleep-deprived life period. Maximal-tension work adds meaningful systemic and connective-tissue fatigue.
>
> There is no rush. A tweaked lower back or a strained hip flexor can nag for weeks or months. A conservative ramp costs a few sessions.

## 3. The Science in Plain Language

### What "body tension" actually is

When climbers say someone has "great body tension," they mean the person can keep their feet on and stay tight on steep, holds-that-want-to-spit-you-off terrain. Underneath that feel is something specific and trainable: **the ability to make the trunk stiff and to co-contract the muscles that link your hands to your feet, so force passes through your body instead of leaking out of it.**

### Co-contraction and trunk stiffness (the legible version)

- **Co-contraction.** Your trunk muscles don't work one at a time. To make the middle of your body *rigid*, the muscles on the front (abdominals, hip flexors) and the muscles on the back (spinal erectors, glutes) contract **at the same time**, bracing the spine like guy-wires holding up a mast from all sides. This simultaneous, all-around contraction is called co-contraction, and it's what turns a floppy midsection into a stiff, force-transmitting beam.
- **Trunk stiffness.** A stiff trunk behaves like a solid rod: push on one end and the force comes straight out the other. A slack trunk behaves like a chain with a loose link — pull on your hands and the force dissipates as your hips sag and your feet swing off. Body-tension training is, fundamentally, training your trunk to *stiffen on demand* against the exact loads steep climbing throws at it.

### The four jobs the trunk does on steep rock

Body tension isn't one thing — it's the trunk resisting being pulled into several different bad shapes at once:

- **Anti-extension** — resisting being pulled into an arched-back, hips-sagging position. On an overhang, gravity pulls your hips down and back, arching your lower spine and peeling your feet off. Your **anterior core** (the abdominals, working with the hip flexors) resists that. This is the single most important quality for keeping feet on a roof, and it's why hollow-body and front-lever work are the backbone of this program.
- **Anti-rotation** — resisting being twisted open. On off-set moves, drop-knees, and one-arm-far positions, the wall tries to spin you off ("barn-dooring"). The **obliques** and deep trunk muscles resist that rotation and keep you square to the wall.
- **Anti-lateral-flexion** — resisting being bent sideways. On traverses and side-pulls, one side of the trunk must hold you from collapsing toward the pull. The obliques and lateral trunk handle this.
- **Force transmission** — actively *linking* a hard hand to a weighted foot, so pulling in with the hands drives your toes and heels *into* the holds rather than off them. This is co-contraction expressed dynamically, and it's what "pushing through your feet on an overhang" actually is.

### The kinetic chain, simply

Think of your body on steep rock as a chain of links: **fingers → hands → forearms → shoulders → trunk → hips → legs → toes/heels.** Force generated at your hands can only reach your feet if every link in between is stiff enough to pass it along. The **trunk is the biggest, most collapsible link in the middle** — and therefore the one most likely to leak force. When the trunk is stiff and connected, pulling with your hands drives your feet into the wall. When the trunk is slack, the chain breaks at the middle: your hands pull, but your hips sag and your feet cut, because the force never made it down to them.

> **The one-sentence version:** finger strength decides how hard your hands can pull; body tension decides whether that pull actually reaches your feet — or leaks out of a sagging middle before it gets there.

### Why steep terrain taxes this specifically

On a slab or a vertical wall, gravity mostly presses you *into* the rock, and your feet stay on with little trunk effort — friction and body-weight do the work. As the wall tilts past vertical, gravity increasingly pulls you *off* the wall and *down*, loading your hands and trying to swing your feet out from under you. The steeper the angle, the more the burden shifts from friction to *active tension*: your trunk must work harder and harder to keep the feet weighted and the body connected. This is why strong-fingered climbers can still flail on roofs — their grip is fine, but the chassis that would put that grip to use on steep ground was never built.

### Why straight-arm strength matters

Much of steep climbing is done with **straight or nearly straight arms** — hanging off a hold to keep the forearms fresh while the trunk and legs do the positioning. Holding tension with a straight arm is a specific strength: the **front lever** and its progressions train exactly this — the ability to hold your body rigid and horizontal off straight arms, driven by the lats and a braced trunk. Front-lever strength is the clearest, most measurable expression of straight-arm body tension, which is why it anchors the strength phase of this program.

> **The takeaway:** you build body tension by training the trunk to stiffen against the exact demands of steep rock — anti-extension, anti-rotation, and force-transmission — and then practising expressing that stiffness on steep terrain until keeping your feet on becomes automatic.

## 4. Equipment Needed

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Floor space & a mat** | For hollow/arch holds, planks, anti-rotation work, and controlled leg raises. A padded surface protects the tailbone and lower back during floor work. |
| **A pull-up bar or gymnastic rings** | The foundation for hanging leg raises and front-lever progressions. **Rings** are ideal — they let the shoulders rotate freely and are gentler on the elbows during levers — but a solid bar works. Ensure it's rated for dynamic loading. |
| **A steep wall, spray wall, or adjustable board with foot holds** | The specificity engine. You need overhanging terrain (roughly **20°–45°+ past vertical**) with a range of hand and **foot** holds so you can drill tension, toe-hooks, and heel-hooks. An adjustable-angle board (e.g., a home wall or commercial board) is ideal for dialling difficulty. |
| **A resistance band (long loop) & an anchor** | For Pallof presses and anti-rotation work, and for **assisting** front-lever progressions (band under the hips). One medium and one light band cover most needs. |
| **A hangboard or sturdy bar** *(optional)* | Useful as an anchor for some lever and tension drills, and for straight-arm hangs. Not essential — a pull-up bar covers most of it. |
| **A light dumbbell or kettlebell** *(optional)* | For loaded anti-rotation, weighted planks, and light posterior-chain work. Household weights work fine. |
| **A logbook or app** | Record hold times, lever progression level, reps, added load/band strength, RPE, and how connected you felt on the wall. Tension progress is subtle — a log makes it visible week to week. |
| **A timer or stopwatch** | Hold-based work (levers, hollow-body, planks) is timed. You need a clear, consistent way to measure time-under-tension. |

> **Setup note.** The two highest-value assets are **a solid bar/rings for levers and hanging leg raises** and **a steep wall with foot holds** for specific tension drills. The floor and band work builds the foundation; the wall is where it becomes climbing. Everything in the schedule below is built around having access to those.

## 5. The Warm-Up Protocol (Every Session)

Never launch into loaded core or straight-arm work cold. A full warm-up takes about 12–18 minutes and, for this quality, has a specific job: it must raise your temperature, mobilize the hips and shoulders, and — crucially — **switch on your bracing** so your trunk is actively stiff before you load it. A braced spine tolerates load far better than a passive one. It has three stages: raise the pulse, mobilize, then activate and ramp.

### Stage 1 — Pulse raise (3–5 min)
Get blood moving and body temperature up: easy cardio (skipping, brisk walk/jog, jumping jacks) or a few minutes of very easy traversing on big holds. You want to feel warm, not tired.

### Stage 2 — Mobility (3–5 min)
- **Hips:** leg swings (front-back and side-to-side), deep bodyweight squats, hip-flexor lunges, and a few cat-camel spinal rolls to gently mobilize the lower back through its range.
- **Shoulders:** band pull-aparts, shoulder circles, and a few slow scapular pull-ups / scap depressions on the bar to wake up the straight-arm control you'll need for levers.
- **Thoracic spine:** open-book rotations or a few segmental extensions to free up rotation before anti-rotation work.

### Stage 3 — Bracing activation & progressive ramp (5–8 min)
This is the tension-specific part. Rather than loading straight into a hard lever, you switch on the trunk and ramp the difficulty of each pattern you're about to train.

| Ramp step | Focus | Prescription |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Bracing wake-up** | Dead-bug (slow, opposite arm/leg, flat lower back) × 6–8/side; bird-dog × 6–8/side |
| 2 | **Anti-extension prime** | Short hollow-body holds × 2–3 × 10–15 s; front plank 20–30 s |
| 3 | **Straight-arm prime** | Active straight-arm hangs (shoulders engaged, not passive) × 2 × 10–15 s; a couple of scap pull-ups |
| 4 | **Wall prime** *(if training on the wall)* | Easy traversing on gently steep terrain, focusing on keeping feet on and staying tight, 2–3 min |
| *(then)* | Begin the session's prescribed work | — |

> **Warm-up rule.** The first working set should feel *hard but controlled*, with your trunk braced and your lower back neutral — not a cold shock. If your lower back, a hip flexor, or a shoulder feels tight, achy, or "off" during the ramp, treat it as information. Reduce the day's difficulty (shorten levers, regress the leg raise, drop load) or stop. A warm-up that doesn't come around cleanly is a reason to back off, not to push through. **Never do your first hard leg raise or lever of the day cold.**

## 6. The 6-Week Periodized Schedule

### Phase logic at a glance

| Phase | Weeks | Primary goal | Loading character | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1 — Core control & tension basics** | 1–2 | Build braced-trunk control, anti-extension endurance, and basic tension coordination | Moderate-difficulty holds and controlled reps: hollow/arch, planks, controlled leg raises, easy steep-wall drills | You can't safely load a trunk that can't yet brace. This phase teaches the spine to stay neutral under load and grooves the co-contraction the harder weeks demand — it also conditions hip flexors and shoulders for what's coming. |
| **Phase 2 — Maximal tension strength** | 3–4 | Build maximal trunk stiffness and straight-arm strength | Harder holds and progressions: front-lever tucks/advanced tucks, harder anti-extension/anti-rotation, harder tension drills on steep wall | Maximal tension strength is the raw material of body tension. Front-lever progressions and hard anti-movement work build the stiffness ceiling that steep climbing then expresses. |
| **Phase 3 — Climbing-specific tension & peaking** | 5–6 | Convert strength into on-the-wall tension; peak, then consolidate | Steep-wall tension application: linked steep sequences, toe/heel-hook tension, foot-cutting drills; lower off-wall volume; Week-6 lighter/consolidation week | Raw trunk strength only matters if it shows up on rock. This phase transfers it to the wall, then eases volume so the adaptation consolidates and shows up fresh. |

**Sessions per week:** **2–3 sessions**, depending on phase. In Phase 1, up to **3** moderate sessions are fine because intensity is low. In Phases 2–3, keep it to **2 hard sessions** separated by at least **48 hours** (e.g., Tue/Fri), because maximal-tension and straight-arm work is demanding on the trunk, hips, and shoulders and needs real recovery.

> **Fit around climbing, don't stack on top of it.** In Phases 2–3, the steep-wall tension work *is* hard climbing — don't also do a maximal steep bouldering session on top. Do the tension work when reasonably fresh. Keep an eye on total load to the **lower back, hip flexors, and shoulders**: those tissues also get worked by hard steep climbing, so count all of it. If a week is unusually stressful or under-slept, take the lighter option or an extra rest day.

### Week 1 — Phase 1: Core control & tension basics

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Foundation A | Full warm-up → **hollow-body & arch holds** (§7) → **front & side planks / anti-extension** → **controlled hanging knee raises** → easy **steep-wall tension drill** → antagonist/prehab |
| *(48h+ rest, or an easy day)* | | |
| Day 2 | Foundation B | Full warm-up → **anti-rotation (Pallof)** → **dead-bug / hollow variations** → **supportive pulling** → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 3 *(optional)* | Foundation C | Full warm-up → light repeat of core basics **or** easy steep-wall tension mileage → antagonist/prehab |
| Other days | Climb / rest | Easy-to-moderate climbing OK; avoid maximal steep bouldering or heavy core sessions. At least 2 full rest days. |

**Focus:** learn to brace. Keep the lower back neutral (flat) on every hold and rep — quality over duration. Establish your baseline tests at the start of the week (see §9). This phase is about control and coordination, not maximal effort.

### Week 2 — Phase 1: Basics (progressed)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Foundation A | Warm-up → progress **hollow-body / plank** holds by +5–10 s or a harder variation → **controlled leg raises**, +2 reps or slower tempo → **steep-wall drill**, slightly steeper → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 2 | Foundation B | Warm-up → **anti-rotation**, +1 set or a step further from anchor → hollow variations → supportive pulling → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 3 *(optional)* | Foundation C | Warm-up → light core basics or easy steep-wall mileage → antagonist/prehab |

**Progression:** add small increments of hold time, a modest tempo change, or a slightly harder variation — **not** big jumps in load. Keep the lower back neutral throughout. If Week 1 left your lower back or hip flexors achy beyond pleasant muscular fatigue, repeat Week 1 rather than progressing.

### Week 3 — Phase 2: Maximal tension strength

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Strength A | Thorough warm-up → **front-lever progression** (tuck/adv-tuck holds, §7) → **hard anti-extension** (e.g., ab-wheel/loaded plank) → **hanging leg raises** → antagonist/prehab |
| *(48h+ rest)* | | |
| Day 2 | Strength B | Warm-up → **steep-wall tension drills** (harder holds, toe-hook & heel-hook tension) → **anti-rotation, loaded** → **supportive pulling** → antagonist/prehab |
| Other days | Easy climb / rest | Keep other climbing easy. At least 2 full rest days. Skip separate maximal steep bouldering. |

**Shift:** difficulty rises and the work becomes maximal-tension strength. Front-lever holds are short, hard, and clean; leg raises become full and controlled; wall drills use worse holds and steeper angles. Target RPE ~7–8 — the last rep or hold of a set is genuinely hard but held with a neutral spine and no form breakdown. **Never grind a lever or leg raise into a kipping, arching failure.**

### Week 4 — Phase 2: Maximal tension strength (progressed)

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Strength A | Warm-up → **front-lever progression**, +2–3 s hold **or** a harder lever position vs. Week 3 → **anti-extension**, small load increase → **hanging leg raises**, +1–2 reps → antagonist/prehab |
| Day 2 | Strength B | Warm-up → **steep-wall tension drills**, harder holds or steeper angle → **anti-rotation**, small load increase → supportive pulling → antagonist/prehab |

**Progression:** add *one* variable at a time — a longer lever hold, a slightly harder lever position, a little more load, or a couple more reps — never several at once. If Week 3 left your lower back, hips, or shoulders achy, hold Week 3's numbers. Maintaining clean, hard tension work is a win; a tweaked back is not.

### Week 5 — Phase 3: Climbing-specific tension & peaking

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Application A | Warm-up → **steep-wall tension application** (§7): linked steep sequences emphasising keeping feet on, toe/heel-hook tension, controlled foot-cutting → short **front-lever** touch to stay sharp → antagonist/prehab |
| *(48h+ rest)* | | |
| Day 2 | Application B | Warm-up → **steep-wall tension application** on *different* terrain/problems (roofs, drop-knees, big spans), **or** hard **tension-board** problems → light anti-rotation → antagonist/prehab |

**Focus:** convert strength into on-the-wall tension. Climb hard, steep, tension-demanding sequences with quality and full recovery between efforts. Off-wall core volume comes *down*; specificity and intent go *up*. Do the hard efforts while fresh; stop while you can still climb tight and keep your feet on — sloppy, feet-cutting-because-you're-tired mileage builds bad habits, not tension.

### Week 6 — Phase 3: Peak, consolidate & re-test

| Day | Session | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Sharpen | Light-to-moderate warm-up + **a few short, sharp tension efforts** (a couple of clean lever touches, 1–2 quality steep sequences) to stay primed. **Low volume. No grinding to failure.** |
| *(2–3 days rest)* | | Rest, eat, and sleep well. This is the consolidation week — accumulated fatigue drops and the strength surfaces. |
| Day 2 | **Re-test** | Full warm-up → repeat the **standardized tests** from §9 under the same conditions → record. Then a couple of easy climbing days. |

> **Week 6 is a deload built into the plan.** Do not add volume this week. Maximal-tension and straight-arm blocks generate real connective-tissue and neural fatigue; the point of the lighter week is to *shed* it so the strength you built in Weeks 1–5 shows up in the re-test — and so you finish the block fresh and steep-climbing-ready, not frayed.

### Auto-regulation rule (all weeks)

Prescriptions are targets, not orders. Each session, gate difficulty and volume on how the warm-up ramp felt and your recent sleep/stress:
- **Feels strong, trunk braces well, back/hips/shoulders quiet:** train the prescribed work (progress if scheduled).
- **Feels flat / under-recovered / a hip flexor or the back feels tight:** hold the load, cut a set, regress the hardest movement, or drop to the easier option.
- **Any sharp lower-back, hip, or shoulder pain, or the ramp feels "off":** stop. Rest, reassess next session. See §8.

## 7. Detailed Prescriptions by Method

Difficulty in this program is gauged mostly by **RPE and form quality** on a braced, warmed-up trunk — and, for holds, by **time-under-tension**. "Hard-but-clean" means you could complete the set with a neutral spine and no kipping, arching, or form collapse; it does not mean grinding to a shaking, form-breaking failure. **On every movement below, the first thing to protect is a braced, neutral lower back.**

> **How to read effort here:** RPE 7–8 ≈ hard, but the last rep/hold is still *clean* — neutral spine, controlled, no kip. **We do not train tension work to form-breaking failure.** The moment your lower back arches, your hips kip, or a lever collapses, the set is over — that rep does not count and pushing past it is how backs and hips get hurt.

### Method 1 — Front-lever progression (Phase 2–3: straight-arm tension strength)

The clearest expression of straight-arm body tension: holding the body rigid and horizontal off straight arms, driven by the lats and a hard-braced trunk. Progress through positions — **tuck → advanced tuck → single-leg → straddle → full** — only advancing when the current level is clean and controlled.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Setup | Straight arms on a bar or rings; shoulders **actively engaged and depressed** (pulled down/back), lower back neutral, ribs down |
| Progression level | Work at the **hardest position you can hold with a flat back and still shoulders** — tuck, advanced tuck, single-leg, straddle, or full |
| Hold time | **5–10 s per rep** (quality holds, not maximal grinds) |
| Reps / sets | 3–5 holds per session; rest **2–3 min** between |
| Intensity | RPE 7–8 — hard but controlled; the hips do **not** sag and the back does **not** arch |
| Regression | Band under the hips for assistance; regress to an easier tuck; or do **negatives** (lower slowly from tuck) |
| Progression | Add 2–3 s of hold time, then advance to the next lever position — one change at a time |

> **Shoulder note.** Front-lever loads the shoulder in a straight-arm position. Keep the shoulders **actively packed** (depressed and slightly retracted), never hanging passively at end range. Any pinching or sharp shoulder pain = stop and regress. Build lever strength **conservatively** — this is a tendon-and-shoulder-intensive movement and rushing it is a classic route to shoulder and elbow strain.

### Method 2 — Hollow-body & arch (superman) holds (Phase 1–3: anti-extension base)

The foundational anti-extension and anti-flexion pattern — teaches the trunk to stiffen with a neutral spine, and directly trains the position you hold on an overhang.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Hollow-body | On your back, lower back **pressed flat to the floor**, legs and arms extended and lifted; hold with the back never arching off the floor |
| Arch / superman | On your front, chest and legs lifted, glutes and posterior chain engaged — the anti-flexion complement to the hollow |
| Hold time | **15–40 s per rep** (build over the block) |
| Reps / sets | 3–5 holds each; rest **60–90 s** |
| Intensity | RPE 6–8; the **instant** the lower back lifts off the floor in the hollow, the rep is over |
| Regression | Tuck the knees / bend the arms (shorter lever) to reduce difficulty while keeping the back flat |
| Progression | Straighten the levers (arms/legs extended), then add hold time; optionally add a light weight held overhead |

### Method 3 — Hanging leg & knee raises (Phase 1–3: anti-extension + hip flexion under load)

Trains the anterior core and hip flexors to lift and control the legs against gravity — directly transferable to pulling feet up onto high steep footholds.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Setup | Hang from a bar/rings, shoulders **actively engaged** (not passive), body still |
| Progression | **Knee raises → straight-leg raises → toes-to-bar** — advance only when controlled |
| Reps / sets | 6–12 controlled reps; 3–4 sets; rest **90 s–2 min** |
| Tempo | **Controlled up, controlled down — no swinging or kipping.** A 2–3 s lower is where the value is |
| Intensity | RPE 7–8 with clean form; stop the set when swing/kip creeps in |
| Regression | Knee raises; or lying leg raises on the floor (back flat) if hanging aggravates the shoulders |
| Progression | Extend the legs, then raise higher (toes-to-bar), then slow the tempo — one change at a time |

> **Lumbar & hip-flexor note.** Do **not** let the leg raise turn into an uncontrolled swinging kip — that both wastes the stimulus and repeatedly loads the lower back and hip flexors at end range. Keep the pelvis controlled and the movement deliberate. Straight-leg raises are demanding on the hip flexors; if a hip flexor gets sharp or achy, regress to bent-knee and reduce volume.

### Method 4 — Anti-rotation (Pallof press & variations) (Phase 1–3: oblique / anti-rotation strength)

Trains the trunk to resist twisting — the quality that stops you barn-dooring off off-set moves on steep terrain.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Setup | Band anchored at chest height to one side; stand braced, press the band straight out and resist its pull to rotate you |
| Hold / reps | **Press-and-hold 3–5 s × 8–12 reps per side**, or slow presses; 2–3 sets each side |
| Intensity | RPE 6–8; the trunk stays square — you resist rotation, you don't let it happen |
| Rest | 60–90 s |
| Regression | Step closer to the anchor (less lever), lighter band |
| Progression | Step further from the anchor, heavier band, or add a hold; progress to **half-kneeling** or **staggered** stances, then loaded carries |

### Method 5 — Steep-wall / tension-board drills (all phases: specific tension application)

The specificity engine — practising trunk stiffness and foot-retention on the exact terrain it's for. Difficulty scales with wall angle, hold size, and foot-hold quality.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Terrain | Overhanging wall / board (~20°–45°+) with hand **and foot** holds |
| Phase 1 drill | **Tension traverses** on easy steep terrain: keep both feet on, move slowly, feel the trunk stay tight; 3–5 × 30–60 s |
| Phase 2 drill | Harder holds / steeper angle: **static, controlled moves** off worse feet; hold each position 2–3 s; emphasise not letting feet cut |
| Phase 3 drill | **Linked steep sequences** and problems demanding real tension: roofs, drop-knees, big spans, keeping feet on through the move |
| Reps / sets | 4–8 drills/problems per session; rest to keep quality high (**2–4 min**) |
| Intensity | Phase 1 RPE 5–6; Phase 2–3 RPE 7–8 — hard, but you stay connected and keep your feet on |
| Progression | Steeper angle → worse hands → worse/smaller feet → longer, more sustained sequences |

### Method 6 — Toe-hook & heel-hook tension drills (Phase 2–3: specific foot-tension)

Trains the *active* pulling tension of toe-hooks and heel-hooks — where the whole posterior or anterior chain must engage to keep the foot loaded and the body connected.

| Parameter | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Toe-hook drill | On a roof/steep hold, set a toe-hook and **actively pull with it** while the trunk and hip flexors keep the body connected; hold 3–5 s; 3–5 reps/side |
| Heel-hook drill | Set a heel-hook and pull the body in with the hamstring/glute, keeping the trunk braced; hold 3–5 s; 3–5 reps/side |
| Intensity | RPE 7–8; the drill is holding *connected*, tight position — not a max effort |
| Rest | 90 s–2 min; alternate sides |
| Caution | Heel-hooks load the **hamstring and knee** at end range — set them **progressively**, never with a sudden violent pull, and stop if the hamstring or knee feels sharp |
| Progression | Steeper terrain, worse holds, longer holds, and linking hooks into moving sequences |

> **Heel-hook note.** Aggressive heel-hooking is a known cause of **hamstring (proximal) and knee strains** in climbers. Warm the hips and hamstrings first, engage the hook progressively, and never crank a maximal heel-hook cold. Sharp hamstring or knee pain = stop.

### Method 7 — Supportive pulling (all phases: kinetic-chain link)

A little pulling strength keeps the "hands-to-trunk" end of the chain strong so your tension has something to transmit *from*. Kept moderate; done when reasonably fresh.

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Intensity | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pull-ups** (strict, full ROM) | 2–3 × 5–8 | RPE 7–8 (RIR ~2), controlled | 2 min |
| **Ring / inverted rows** | 2–3 × 8–10 | RPE 7, trunk braced (row is also anti-extension) | 90 s |
| **Straight-arm lat work** (band or cable pull-downs, arms straight) | 2 × 10–12 | Moderate — grooves the straight-arm lat engagement levers need | 90 s |

> Keep this **supportive, not the main event** — its job is to keep the pulling link of the chain strong, not to compete with the tension work for recovery. If it's eating your recovery, cut it.

### Method 8 — Antagonist & prehab (every session, ~8–10 min)

Body-tension work is flexion- and pull-dominant; balancing it protects the shoulders, lower back, and hips. Do at the **end** of every session (fine on rest days too).

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Posterior-chain work** (hip hinges / Romanian deadlifts light, or back extensions) | 2–3 × 10–12 | Glutes, hamstrings, spinal erectors | Balances the heavily worked anterior core; protects the back |
| **Glute bridges / hip thrusts** | 2–3 × 12–15 | Glutes, posterior hips | Supports the hip and low-back that leg raises load |
| **Rotator-cuff & scapular work** (band external rotations, scap pull-aparts, prone Y-T-W) | 2–3 × 12–15 | Shoulder stability | Protects the shoulder through straight-arm/lever work |
| **Wrist/forearm extensor work** (light) | 2 × 12–15 | Forearm balance | Elbows take load in lever and hang work |
| **Hip-flexor & hamstring mobility** (gentle, controlled) | 2 × 20–30 s | Hip range | Keeps the tissues leg raises and heel-hooks load supple |

## 8. Injury Prevention & Tissue Health

> **This is the most important section in the program. Read it fully before your first session, and re-read it whenever your back, hips, or shoulders feel off. The failure mode of tension training is not a dramatic pop — it's a strained hip flexor, a tweaked lower back, or an overloaded shoulder from loading a position your trunk couldn't yet brace. No steep-climbing goal is worth a nagging back or hip.**

### The core principle: brace before you load

Where a finger block's danger is a single pulley overload, a body-tension block's danger is **loading the spine, hips, and shoulders in positions they aren't yet braced or strong enough to control**. Every rule below exists to keep those tissues inside what they can adapt to:

- **Protect the lumbar spine.** The single most important rule: keep the **lower back neutral and braced** on every hold and rep. In the hollow-body, the lower back stays *flat to the floor*; on leg raises, the pelvis stays *controlled*, never arching or swinging. Uncontrolled, kipping leg raises and arched, sagging planks repeatedly load the lower back at end range — that's how backs get tweaked. **If you can't hold a movement with a neutral, braced spine, regress it.**
- **Guard the hip flexors.** Loaded straight-leg raises are demanding on the hip flexors, which strain when overloaded suddenly or trained cold. Warm up, progress from bent-knee to straight-leg gradually, and treat any sharp or lingering hip-flexor ache as a stop signal.
- **Protect the shoulder in straight-arm work.** Front-lever and straight-arm tension put high force through the shoulder at length. Keep the shoulders **actively packed** (depressed, engaged), never hanging passively at end range, and **progress lever difficulty conservatively** — rushing front-lever levels is a classic cause of shoulder and elbow strain.
- **Ramp difficulty conservatively.** Add one variable at a time (hold time, lever position, load, or reps) — never several. When in doubt, hold a week rather than jumping.
- **Honor the phase order.** Weeks 1–2 build the bracing and control that make Weeks 3–6 safe. Don't skip the basics because they feel easy — they're what protects your spine under the heavier loads.

### Injuries this block can provoke

Heavy trunk and straight-arm work tends to aggravate:

- **Lower-back strain / irritation** — from loss of bracing: arched planks, uncontrolled kipping leg raises, or loading beyond what the trunk can stabilize. The classic mistake is chasing hold time or reps at the expense of a neutral spine.
- **Hip-flexor strain** — from loaded straight-leg raises done cold, too heavy, or too soon. Felt at the front of the hip/groin.
- **Shoulder strain / impingement-type irritation** — from straight-arm lever work done with passive, unpacked shoulders or progressed too fast.
- **Proximal hamstring or knee strain** — from aggressive heel-hooking, especially cold or with a sudden maximal pull (see §7, Method 6).
- **Elbow / medial-forearm irritation** — straight-arm levers and hangs load the elbow; balance and progress conservatively.

### Warning signs — what to watch for
- **Sharp or localized lower-back pain**, especially during or after leg raises or planks, or back pain that lingers into the next day or into daily tasks (bending, sitting).
- **Front-of-hip / groin pain** during or after leg raises — a hip-flexor warning.
- **Shoulder pinching, catching, or sharp pain** during levers or straight-arm hangs.
- **Hamstring-origin (sit-bone) or knee pain** during or after heel-hook drills.
- A movement that suddenly feels *unstable* or that you can only do by arching, kipping, or losing your brace — a sign the load is beyond your current control.

**A nagging back, hip, or shoulder ache is a yellow flag — treat it seriously and early.** Trunk and joint strains are far easier to prevent than to resolve, and they can linger for weeks once established.

### The green / amber / red pain rule
Use this every session to decide whether to train, modify, or stop:

| Signal | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 🟢 **Green** | Normal muscular fatigue and burn in the abs, obliques, hip flexors, or lats. No joint, spine, or tendon pain. | Train as prescribed. |
| 🟡 **Amber** | Mild ache/stiffness/"awareness" in the lower back, a hip flexor, or a shoulder that is not sharp and settles quickly; a movement that's getting hard to keep clean. | Reduce difficulty and volume, regress the movement, shorten holds, add extra recovery. Do **not** progress. Reassess next session; if it persists, treat as red. |
| 🔴 **Red** | **Sharp or localized lower-back pain; sharp hip-flexor/groin, shoulder, hamstring, or knee pain**; pain that lingers or worsens after sessions; any pain that changes how you move or persists into daily tasks. | **Stop the session immediately.** No loaded tension work until fully symptom-free. Seek a qualified professional. Do not self-diagnose. |

### Mandatory rest & recovery spacing
- **At least 48 hours** between hard (Phase 2–3) sessions — always.
- Keep **at least 2 full rest days** per week during the block.
- Don't stack maximal tension work on top of a maximal steep bouldering session — that's an overload on the same trunk, hips, and shoulders.
- If your back, hips, or shoulders feel persistently tired, stiff, or achy across several days, that's under-recovery — take extra rest. Missing a session costs nothing; a tweaked back costs a season.

### A brief note on return-to-loading
> This program is for healthy, pain-free backs, hips, and shoulders and is **not** a rehabilitation protocol. If you've had low-back pain, a hip-flexor or hamstring strain, or any shoulder issue, return to loaded tension work **only** when a qualified professional has cleared you and you are symptom-free. When you do return, restart at **Phase 1** difficulty (or lower), progress even more conservatively than usual, obsess over a neutral braced spine, and treat any recurrence of symptoms as an immediate stop. Genuine low-back, hip, or shoulder rehab is individual and progressive and belongs with a physiotherapist — not with a generic download.

## 9. Testing & Benchmarking

A standardized test at the **start** and **end** of the block turns "I think I feel tighter" into a number. Keep every variable identical across both tests: same equipment, same wall/holds, same protocol, same warm-up, same time of day, same rested state.

### Standardized tests (do all three; keep them fixed)

> **Prerequisite:** only test after a complete warm-up (§5), and only if the activation ramp felt clean. If anything feels off — especially the lower back, a hip flexor, or a shoulder — postpone. A test is not worth an injury.

**Test A — Front-lever hold (straight-arm tension strength).**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Setup | The **hardest lever position you can hold cleanly** (tuck / advanced tuck / single-leg / straddle / full), shoulders packed, back neutral |
| Task | Hold that position as long as form stays clean (no hip sag, no back arch) |
| Score | **Longest clean hold time** at that position (note the position) — or, if between levels, the hardest position held for ≥5 s |
| Conditions | Same bar/rings, same position, same warm-up at start and re-test |

**Test B — Hollow-body hold (anti-extension endurance).**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Setup | Full hollow-body (arms and legs extended), lower back **pressed flat to the floor** |
| Task | Hold until the lower back lifts off the floor (form failure) |
| Score | **Total hold time** with the back flat |
| Conditions | Same lever length (arm/leg position), same surface, same warm-up |

**Test C — Steep-wall "feet-cutting" tension benchmark (specific application).**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Setup | A **fixed steep problem or sequence** (fixed angle, fixed holds) that demands keeping feet on |
| Task | Climb it while **keeping both feet on the whole way** — or, on a chosen roof move, hold a set position without the feet cutting |
| Score | **Whether you complete it feet-on**, and/or the **hardest steep move/position you can hold with feet on** (record angle, holds, and how connected it felt) |
| Conditions | Identical problem, angle, holds, and warm-up at start and re-test |

**Record** each score plus how it felt and any back/hip/shoulder notes. **Re-test in Week 6** after the lighter week, under identical conditions. A longer lever hold, a longer hollow-body, or a harder steep sequence held feet-on is your objective result. No change is still useful data — it tells you whether to repeat the block, adjust recovery, or change the stimulus.

> **Testing is a genuinely hard effort.** Count the initial tests as your first loaded work of Week 1, and don't also do a full strength session the same day.

## 10. Recovery Essentials

Trunk-strength and tension adaptations are built during recovery, not during the hold. Brief, evidence-aligned basics:

- **Sleep.** The highest-leverage recovery tool. Aim for **7–9 hours** of quality sleep. Muscle and connective-tissue repair, neural adaptation, and strength gains all depend on it; under-sleeping blunts adaptation and raises injury risk.
- **Session spacing.** Covered above and worth repeating: **48+ hours** between hard sessions, 2+ rest days per week. Maximal-tension work taxes the trunk, hips, and shoulders even when you feel fine.
- **Fuel the work.** Strength and connective-tissue adaptation need adequate total energy and **protein** (a common general guideline for active people is roughly **1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight per day**, spread across meals). Include enough carbohydrate to fuel hard sessions. This is general educational information, not individualized dietary advice.
- **Hydration.** Be well-hydrated for sessions — connective tissue and muscle both perform and recover better when you're not dehydrated.
- **Move on rest days.** Gentle mobility for the hips, thoracic spine, and shoulders keeps the tissues this block loads supple and can aid recovery. Keep it easy — rest days are for recovering, not for extra hard core work.
- **Listen to trend, not just the day.** Persistent fatigue, poor sleep, or a lingering back/hip/shoulder ache across several days means back off. Strains and overload build silently in tissue that's asked to stabilize under load too often.

## 11. After Week 6

Finish the block, bank the gains, and choose a deliberate next step. Trunk strength and body tension are relatively durable — they detrain slowly compared with the pump — so you don't need to train them hard year-round to keep most of what you built.

**Option A — Maintain (peak for a steep-climbing season).** Drop to **one tension session per week** (a few lever holds, some leg raises, and steep-wall tension mileage) and go climb the steep routes and boulders you built this for. A weekly maintenance dose holds most of the adaptation while you focus on performance. This is the ideal follow-on if you built this block *for* a trip or a steep project.

**Option B — Take a down-week, then a new focus.** Run a lighter/recovery week, then pivot to a complementary quality — maximal finger strength (see the sibling *Contact Strength* program) or power-endurance (see *Anaerobic Capacity for Long Routes*) — while keeping a small dose of tension work so it doesn't fade. Rotating the emphasis gives the trunk, hips, and shoulders a relative break while you raise a different quality.

**Option C — Repeat the block, progressed.** After at least a **light/recovery week**, re-run the 6 weeks from your **new** baseline (re-test numbers). Progress by advancing your lever position, adding load to anti-movement work, or drilling tension on steeper terrain and worse feet — but keep the same conservative, one-variable-at-a-time ramp discipline, and keep obsessing over a neutral braced spine. Don't chase maximal tension work continuously; cycle it with finger, power, and endurance focuses across your training year.

> Whichever you choose, keep antagonist/prehab work, posterior-chain balance, and warm-ups in your routine year-round — the lower back and shoulders especially will thank you. And keep testing periodically under the same standard — long-term trend is what tells you your training is working.

## 12. Sources & Further Reading

Genuine, well-known work in climbing science, strength training, and core/trunk stability. Consult the original sources directly; the notes below are plain-language pointers, not exact quotations or specific numeric claims.

- **Dave MacLeod, *9 out of 10 Climbers Make the Same Mistakes*** and ***Make or Break*** — accessible, well-regarded books on climbing performance and injury prevention, including body tension, technique on steep ground, and the shoulder, back, and finger.
- **Eric Hörst, *Training for Climbing* and the Training4Climbing resources** — widely used practical guides to periodized climbing training, including core and body-tension work, front-lever progressions, and warm-ups.
- **Volker Schöffl and colleagues** — the leading body of clinical research on **climbing injuries**, including shoulder, elbow, and lumbar/soft-tissue injuries and the epidemiology of climbing-related strains (a useful reference for the injury-prevention framing here).
- **Stuart McGill, *Back Mechanic* and *Ultimate Back Fitness and Performance*** — foundational, widely cited work on **spinal stability, trunk stiffness, and the "big-three"/anti-movement approach to core training** (bracing, neutral spine, resisting motion rather than creating it) that underpins this program's emphasis on anti-extension and anti-rotation.
- **General strength-and-conditioning literature on "anti-movement" core training** — the principle that the trunk's primary athletic job is to *resist* extension, rotation, and lateral flexion and transmit force through the kinetic chain, rather than to produce reps of flexion. This is the conceptual backbone of Section 3.
- **Gymnastic-strength and calisthenics coaching resources on the front lever** — well-established progression frameworks (tuck → advanced tuck → single-leg → straddle → full) and the shoulder-packing and conservative-progression cues used in Method 1.
- **Lattice Training and related applied-science practitioners** — publicly available educational material on **assessment and structured programming** for climbers, including body-tension and core demands of steep climbing.
- **PAR-Q+ (2023) and the ePARmed-X+, © the PAR-Q+ Collaboration** — the physical-activity readiness screening referenced throughout this program.
- **CSEP (Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology)** — evidence-based physical activity, resistance-training, and sleep guidance underpinning the general recovery and training principles here.

> These are starting points for going deeper — not a claim that this program reproduces any specific study's protocol. Where you see a number in this manual, treat it as an educational default to individualize, not a research finding.
