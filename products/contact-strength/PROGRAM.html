1. Overview — what this develops and why it transfers

This is the classic low-rep, high-force finger block: the training that raises how much force your fingers and pulling chain can produce in a single, brief, near-maximal effort.

Three linked qualities are the target:

  • Contact strength — the force you can express almost instantly when you catch or latch a hold. It is limited by how quickly and completely your muscles switch on.
  • Maximal finger force — the ceiling of how hard your forearm flexors can pull through the finger tendons on a given edge.
  • Recruitment — how fully your nervous system activates the available muscle. Early strength gains are dominated by this neural side rather than by muscle size, which is exactly what climbers want: more force with very little added mass, so your strength-to-weight ratio improves.

The transfer, in plain terms. Hard moves are limited by how much force you can produce right now on the hold in front of you. Train that ceiling up, and smaller holds become usable and bigger moves become stickable. Because the stimulus here is high-intensity and low-volume, most of the adaptation is neural — you get stronger without getting heavier. This is the same logic laid out in the site's Strength ≠ size and Finger anatomy articles: finger "strength" is really forearm strength plus healthy tendons and pulleys, and low-rep loading is the honest way to raise the force ceiling.

What this program is not. It is not an endurance or "anti-pump" program (see Anaerobic Capacity for Long Routes for that), not a first lifting program, and not a rehab plan. It will not fix a tweaky pulley — it assumes healthy, ready fingers.

2. Who this is for — prerequisites and the honest "not yet" gate

This block is a good fit if you:

  • Have at least ~2 years of consistent climbing and a training age that includes some structured finger loading (you have hung a board before, or bouldered hard on small holds for a season or more).
  • Climb roughly V4/6c+ (5.11+) or harder, or can comfortably do controlled bodyweight hangs on a 20 mm edge in a half-crimp for 10+ seconds.
  • Can perform at least 3–5 strict pull-ups and have no current pain in fingers, elbows, or shoulders.
  • Have passed the readiness screening and are apparently healthy.

This is not for you yet if any of the following are true:

  • You have climbed for less than about 1 year, or have never trained fingers deliberately. Your tendons and pulleys need more time under progressive climbing load first. Start with general climbing volume and the free Antagonist & Shoulder Resilience and Off-the-Wall Foundations material. Muscle gets strong faster than tendon does; rushing this is the single most common way climbers pop a pulley.
  • You have a current or recent (within ~3 months) finger, pulley, elbow, or shoulder injury, or any lingering finger pain. This program adds load; it is not a return-to- climbing protocol. See a physiotherapist or sports physician and get cleared first.
  • You are an adolescent or your growth plates may still be open. This is a genuine safety issue, not a formality. In growing climbers the epiphyseal growth plate at the base of the middle phalanx is a recognised weak point, and epiphyseal (growth-plate) stress fractures of the fingers are well documented in young climbers, particularly with hard crimping and hangboarding. Growth plates typically close in the mid-to-late teens to around age ~18–19, but timing varies widely between individuals. If you are under 18, are unsure whether your growth plates have closed, or have any finger pain during growth, do not hangboard maximally — train fingers through climbing itself and seek guidance from a sports physician. When in doubt, wait. (This is general education, not a diagnosis; a clinician can assess skeletal maturity.)
  • You cannot yet hang your own bodyweight on a ~20 mm edge with control, or you are new to resistance training generally. Build that base first.

Bottom line: finger strength is worth chasing, but the connective tissue that carries the load adapts in months, not weeks. The prerequisites above exist so the training stimulus lands on tissue that is ready for it.

3. Safety & screening (mandatory)

Finger training carries real, specific injury risk — most commonly strains or tears of the A2 and A4 finger pulleys, and flexor tendon or growth-plate problems. Treat this section as part of the program, not a disclaimer.

Before you begin

  1. Complete the readiness screening — the site's PAR-Q (legal/par-q.html). If it flags anything, get medical clearance before training.
  2. Read the Health & Fitness Disclaimer (legal/disclaimer.html). You train voluntarily and at your own risk.
  3. Confirm you meet the prerequisites in Section 2, including the growth-plate gate.

This is education, not individualized medical advice

This program is general strength-and-conditioning information for apparently healthy adults. It does not create a coach–client, physician–patient, or physiotherapist–patient relationship, does not diagnose or treat any condition, and is not a substitute for in-person assessment. For anything specific to your body, work with a qualified professional.

Pulley-injury warning signs — know these

Stop and back off if you notice any of the following. If they persist, see a physiotherapist or sports physician before loading fingers again:

  • A sharp pain or an audible/palpable "pop" at the base or middle of a finger — this is the classic pulley-injury presentation and is a stop-now, seek-assessment event.
  • Swelling, tenderness, or a "bowstringing" sensation (the tendon lifting away from the bone) along the front of a finger.
  • Pain that is sharp, localized to a joint or the palm-side of a finger, as opposed to the diffuse muscular ache of a worked forearm.
  • Finger pain that worsens session to session, or morning stiffness/pain that lingers.

Pain rules — the stop criteria

  • Never push through sharp pain. Dull, symmetrical forearm fatigue is expected; sharp, localized, or one-sided finger pain is a stop signal.
  • Use a simple traffic-light rule during and after sessions:
    • Green (0–2/10, dull, diffuse): proceed as planned.
    • Amber (3/10, or any twinge that is localized but fades): reduce load, switch to open-hand, cut volume, and reassess next session. Do not add load.
    • Red (sharp pain, a pop, pain that lingers >24–48 h, or any warning sign above): stop the finger work, do not "test" it, and if it does not settle quickly, get it assessed.
  • When in doubt, do less. A missed session costs you nothing; a popped A2 costs months.

Consult a professional

If you have any finger, elbow, or shoulder pain, a history of pulley injury, or any doubt about your readiness, consult a physiotherapist or sports-medicine physician before starting. Nothing here replaces individual clinical judgement.

Notes on substitutions

  • No-hang / lifting-edge devices (a portable edge attached to a loading pin, lifted from the floor) are an excellent and often safer substitute for max-hang work: load is easy to dial precisely and you can bail instantly. Anywhere this program prescribes a max hang, you may substitute a no-hang lift of matched intensity and grip type.
  • If you cannot yet add weight to a bodyweight hang, that's fine — use smaller edges or band assistance to reach the target effort, and progress by removing assistance or reducing edge depth. See the assessment section.
  • Avoid tiny, aggressive "micro" edges and pockets for this block unless you are advanced; the 20 mm edge is the workhorse because it loads the fingers hard while keeping the pulleys in a kinder position.

5. Assessment & benchmarking — establishing your baseline

You cannot autoregulate load without a baseline. Do the assessment on a fresh day (not after hard climbing), fully warmed up (Section 8). Pick one protocol below and use it for the whole block so retests are comparable.

The benchmark grip and edge

  • Edge: flat 20 mm (record the exact depth you use).
  • Grip: half-crimp (fingers ~90° at the big knuckle, thumb off) as the primary test grip. If half-crimp is uncomfortable, use open-hand/open-drag and record that.
  • Do not test in a full crimp. (See the exercise library on full-crimp risk.)

Protocol A — Maximal Added Weight (MAW) (preferred if you can add load)

  1. Warm up thoroughly.
  2. Find the most added weight you can hold for a controlled ~10-second hang on the 20 mm edge in a half-crimp, with good form (shoulders engaged, no elbow lock, no shaking your way through it).
  3. Work up in small jumps (e.g. +5 kg, then +2.5 kg) with 3+ minutes rest between attempts. Stop when you can't hold a clean 10 s.
  4. Record: bodyweight + added weight = your effective hang load. This added-weight figure is your MAW. Training percentages below are taken from MAW where noted, or set by RPE.

Protocol B — Max hang time (if you can't/shouldn't add weight)

  1. Warm up thoroughly.
  2. On the 20 mm edge (or the smallest edge you can hold safely) in a half-crimp, time your maximum controlled hang to form failure (stop the instant form breaks — no clawing).
  3. Record the time. If bodyweight on 20 mm is easy (>20 s), use a smaller edge or add light load and record that setup.

Protocol C — Assisted (band/pulley) baseline (if you can't yet hang bodyweight)

  1. Loop a band over the bar and put a foot/knee in it to offload weight.
  2. Find the least assistance that lets you hold a clean 10 s hang on 20 mm.
  3. Record the band (or measured kg offloaded). Progress by removing assistance.

Turning the baseline into training loads

  • Preparatory work (Weeks 1–2): ~RPE 6–7 — you finish each hang feeling you had ~3–4 seconds / a solid rep or two in reserve. In MAW terms this is roughly 70–80% of MAW.
  • Max-strength work (Weeks 3–5): ~RPE 8 — ~1–2 in reserve; roughly 85–90% of MAW.
  • Peak-force work (Weeks 6–7): ~RPE 9 — very hard but never to failure or to form breakdown; roughly 90–95% of MAW on short holds.
  • Retest at the end (Section 13) using the identical protocol.

Honesty note: RPE for finger hangs is less precise than for barbell lifts, and MAW can drift a couple of kg day to day with sleep, skin, and warm-up. Treat the percentages as a starting map, then let RPE and form be the final authority each session.

6. How the block is built — the periodization, phase by phase

This is an 8-week mesocycle built on a simple, well-supported idea (see the Periodization 101 article): apply a stress, let the body adapt, then evolve the stress so adaptation keeps coming — and deload on purpose so the fitness surfaces. Intensity climbs across the block while volume drops, which is the standard shape of a strength block.

The logic in one paragraph. Weeks 1–2 make your fingers ready to be loaded hard. Weeks 3–5 do the main work of teaching your nervous system to pull harder. Weeks 6–7 sharpen that into peak, near-instant force. Week 8 lets it all surface so you can measure a real gain and set up the next block. Each phase is deliberately built to set up the next — skipping the prep phase to "get to the heavy stuff" is how tissue gets ahead of itself.

Frequency: finger-intensive sessions are kept to 2 per week with ≥48 h between them, plus one lighter pull-power/support session — three sessions total, which matches how much high-intensity finger loading most climbers recover from while still climbing.

7. Weekly structure & integrating with climbing

Golden rule of sequencing: do high-intensity finger work when you are fresh, not at the end of a pumpy session. Hard hangs go early in a session and early in the week, before or well-separated from hard climbing — never on trashed fingers.

Default week (3 training sessions + climbing)

  • ≥48 hours between the two heavy finger days (A and C). Never stack them.
  • Put your hardest climbing on Saturday, away from the heavy finger days.
  • Total hard-finger exposures per week (hangs + limit bouldering) should stay moderate — more is not better here; recovery is where fingers get stronger.

If you're short on time

Cut in this order, protecting the primary stimulus:

  1. First cut Session B (support work) — do a shortened version on a climbing day.
  2. Then trim the weighted pull volume in Sessions A/C.
  3. Never cut the warm-up, and never cut the rest periods to save time — long rests are part of the strength stimulus, not padding.
  4. A two-session week (A and C only) is a legitimate minimum-effective-dose week.

Managing total load & recovery

  • Sleep and food drive tendon and neural adaptation more than any extra set does.
  • If climbing volume is high that week, reduce hang volume, not intensity — keep the heavy stimulus, cut the number of sets.
  • Skin and joints are part of the system: shredded tips or achy A2s mean back off.

8. The warm-up (non-negotiable)

Never load fingers hard cold. Budget 15–20 minutes. The goal is warm tissue, raised heart rate, and progressively loaded fingers — not fatigue.

Stage 1 — Pulse raiser (3–5 min). Light cardio: skipping, jogging, jumping jacks, or arm swings until you're slightly warm.

Stage 2 — Mobility & tissue prep (3–5 min). Wrist circles, finger flexion/extension, gentle finger-tendon glides, band pull-aparts, and shoulder circles. A few easy scapular pull-ups.

Stage 3 — Progressive finger loading (8–10 min). This is the important part. Do 3–5 easy hangs, starting on a large, comfortable edge and progressing toward your working edge/grip, ramping load gradually:

10. Exercise library

Each entry: what it's for, setup, grips, tempo, common errors, and how to scale.

Max hang (the core exercise)

  • Develops: maximal finger force and recruitment on a fixed edge.
  • Setup: warm up fully. Grip the edge, take a strong active-shoulder position (shoulders down and slightly engaged — not hanging passively off the joints), soft elbows. Hang for the prescribed 7–10 s, then step or lower off under control.
  • Grips:
    • Open-hand / open-drag — fingers more extended; kindest to the pulleys; use for a large share of volume.
    • Half-crimp — fingers ~90° at the big knuckle, thumb off; the key trainable position for climbing and the primary benchmark grip.
    • Full crimp (fingers bent sharply, thumb clamped over) — trained cautiously, if at all. The full crimp places the highest load on the A2 pulley and is the position most associated with pulley injury. This program does not prescribe max full-crimp hangs. Advanced, healthy, experienced climbers may maintain some full-crimp exposure through climbing, but do not chase max loads in it here.
  • Tempo: static hold; no bouncing, no re-gripping mid-hang.
  • Common errors: hanging passively off the shoulders; letting the fingers drag/claw open toward a crimp under fatigue; holding too long and grinding out ugly seconds (stop at form failure); testing on cold fingers.
  • Regressions: larger edge; band/foot assistance; shorter holds; reduce load.
  • Progressions: add load; reduce edge depth (advanced); progress from Phase 1 → 3 intensities.

No-hang / edge-lift (equal-status alternative to max hangs)

  • Develops: same as max hangs, with precise, instantly-bailable load.
  • Setup: attach an edge to a loading pin/handle; stand tall, brace, and lift the load off the floor through the fingers only, matching the grip and hold time of the max hang it replaces.
  • Why use it: easy to load exactly, easy to drop, low whole-body demand — often the safer choice. Substitute anywhere max hangs appear.

Weighted pull-up

  • Develops: maximal pulling force in the arms and back that backs up the fingers.
  • Setup: add load via belt/vest/dumbbell. Full hang to chin-over; control the descent.
  • Grips: overhand on a bar or neutral on rings (rings are kinder to elbows).
  • Tempo: intentful up, ~2–3 s down. In peak phase, keep it snappy and low-rep.
  • Errors: kipping, partial range, shrugging into the ears at the top.
  • Regressions: bodyweight or band-assisted. Progressions: add load; slow eccentric.

Explosive pull-up / lock-off holds (pull-power)

  • Develops: rate of force development — turning strength into fast, forceful pulling.
  • Setup (explosive): from a dead hang, pull as fast as possible to chest height; lower under control. Quality over quantity — stop when speed drops.
  • Setup (lock-off): hold a static bent-arm position (e.g. 90°) for 3–5 s.
  • Errors: grinding slow reps and calling them explosive; sacrificing shoulder position.
  • Scale: band-assist explosive reps; shallower lock-off angle to start.

Support / antagonist work (elbow & shoulder health)

  • Wrist-extensor eccentrics / reverse curls — for "climber's elbow" prehab.
  • Shoulder external rotation (band/cable) — rotator-cuff health.
  • Anti-rotation core (Pallof press) — trunk tension that transfers to the wall.
  • These balance the pulling-dominant demands of climbing; keep them in even when time is tight. (See Antagonist & Shoulder Resilience for the full rationale.)

Contact strength on the wall (Phase 3)

  • Develops: expressing finger force dynamically — catching and latching under speed.
  • Setup: on a bouldering wall, do short, powerful sequences on good-to-moderate holds with full rest between attempts. Focus on snappy, precise latches, not volume and not tiny holds. This is a strength stimulus, not a pump session — if you're getting pumped, you're doing endurance by accident.

11. Autoregulation, progression & deload logic

Numbers on a page can't feel your fingers. Autoregulation means adjusting the plan to how you actually show up.

The daily readiness gate (before every finger session)

Run through this in the warm-up:

  • Any red-flag finger pain or a recent pop? → No max work. Reassess / seek advice.
  • Slept badly, sick, skin trashed, or fingers achy from the last session? → Drop to the lower end of the RPE range and cut a set or two. Keep intensity honest but volume small.
  • Warm-up primer hang feels heavier than usual at the same load? → That's your true strength for today; autoregulate load down to hit the target RPE, don't force the planned kilos.

Progressing load (the strength-first rule)

Because the goal is strength, progress intensity before volume:

  1. When the top working set finishes at or below the target RPE with clean form, add ~2.5–5% load (about 2.5 kg) next session, or drop edge depth slightly (advanced).
  2. If you hit the top of the RPE range but form was clean, repeat the same load next time until it drifts down to the target — then progress.
  3. If form broke, you hit RPE 9+ when 8 was the target, or pain appeared, reduce load.
  4. Do not chase progression by piling on extra sets. More volume is not the lever here.

When to deload early (don't wait for Week 8 if you see these)

  • Two sessions in a row where planned loads feel far harder than their target RPE.
  • Persistent finger, elbow, or shoulder achiness that doesn't clear between sessions.
  • Sleep/mood/motivation tanking, or plateaued/declining loads.

A mid-block deload = take 3–5 days of easy climbing and no max hangs, then resume where you left off. Backing off on purpose is part of the plan.

12. Transfer to the wall

Strength you can't use is wasted. To convert this block into climbing:

  • Keep climbing through the block. This is a supplement to climbing, not a replacement. The wall is where finger force becomes climbing skill.
  • Sequence smart: hard hangs when fresh, hard climbing on separate days (Section 7).
  • In the peak phase, spend time on your project or on hard, powerful boulders. New force needs to be rehearsed in real movement — latching, catching, and holding under body tension — for the gym numbers to show up as sends.
  • Expect the payoff to surface after the deload, when fatigue drops. Judge the block by your climbing in the following weeks, not mid-block when you're carrying fatigue.
  • Don't out-climb your tissue. Feeling strong is exactly when climbers latch a tiny crimp too enthusiastically and get hurt. Ramp on-the-wall crimping as patiently as you ramped the hangs.

13. Retest & what to do next block

Retest (end of Week 8, fresh day)

Repeat your exact baseline protocol from Section 5 — same edge, same grip, same warm-up, rested. Record the new number and compare.

  • MAW protocol: compare added weight for a clean 10 s hang.
  • Max-time protocol: compare hang time on the same edge.
  • Assisted protocol: compare assistance needed.

A meaningful gain over 8 weeks is realistic but modest — think a few kg of added load or a few seconds — and it's driven mostly by better recruitment. Don't measure success only by the retest; how your climbing feels over the next few weeks matters more.

What to do next

  • If you gained and feel good: take a lighter transition week (easy climbing, minimal hangs), then either (a) run a power / power-endurance block to express the new strength on longer sequences, or (b) repeat this block from a new, higher baseline after 3–4 weeks of general climbing.
  • If you stalled: you likely need either more recovery (were you deloading enough?) or a change of stimulus. Rotate to a different quality for a block — finger strength responds well to being left alone and revisited, not ground on continuously.
  • If anything got tweaky: stop finger-max work, let it settle, and get assessed before loading heavy again. Consistency over months beats any single hard block.
  • Don't run max finger blocks back-to-back indefinitely. Tendons and pulleys reward patience; alternate hard finger blocks with other qualities across the year.

14. FAQ & troubleshooting

My fingers feel "tweaky" mid-block — what do I do? Treat it as amber/red (Section 3). Stop max work on that finger, switch to open-hand at much lighter loads or take finger-rest days, and don't "test" it. If a sharp pain, a pop, swelling, or bowstringing appeared, that's a stop-and-get-assessed event. A few missed sessions cost nothing; a pulley tear costs months.

I've plateaued — the loads won't go up. First check recovery: sleep, food, and whether you're actually deloading. Then check honesty of intensity (are you truly at the target RPE, or grinding ugly reps?). If both are fine, you may simply need a change of stimulus — finish the block, take a transition week, and rotate to a different quality. Strength rarely climbs in a straight line.

Can I train fingers more than twice a week to speed things up? No — that's the fastest route to an overuse injury here. Two heavy finger days plus climbing is plenty of high-intensity finger exposure for most people. Recovery is the adaptation.

Should I use a full crimp? It feels stronger. Not for max hangs in this program. The full crimp loads the A2 pulley the hardest and is the grip most linked to pulley injury. Train half-crimp and open-hand; let any full-crimp exposure come from normal climbing.

I'm travelling / stuck without a hangboard for a week. Take it as a light week. A portable edge or no-hang device covers most travel; if you have nothing, do bodyweight climbing-specific work, keep antagonist/support work going with bands, and resume the plan when you're back. A missed week won't undo the block.

My skin is destroyed. Back off. You can't train fingers hard through split tips, and pushing on shredded skin changes your grip mechanics in ways that invite injury. Rest, file, moisturise, and return when the skin is intact.

I'm not sure my growth plates are closed. Then don't hangboard maximally, and see Section 2 and a sports physician. This is a real safety issue for younger climbers, not a formality.

How long until I see it in my climbing? Often after the deload, and over the following few weeks, as fatigue clears and you rehearse the strength on the wall. Judge the block by your climbing in the month after it ends.

15. References & further reading

These are foundational, widely-cited works in climbing science, sports medicine, and resistance training, listed by author/year and topic. Summaries throughout this program are simplified for a general audience. The principles below are high-consensus; a site owner who wishes to publish a formal bibliography can add full titles and page details from the originals — but nothing here depends on a specific edition or DOI.

Finger-strength & hangboard training

  • López-Rivera, E. & González-Badillo, J. J. (2012; and follow-up work). Comparisons of maximal-load ("max added weight") hangboard training against intermittent/repeater methods in sport climbers — the empirical basis for the max-hang, low-rep approach used here.
  • Medernach, J. P., Kleinöder, H., & colleagues. Fingerboard-specific training increases maximal finger strength in climbers — support for dedicated hangboard blocks over climbing alone.
  • Levernier, G., Laffaye, G., and related work. Maximal finger force and rate of force development distinguish higher- from lower-performing climbers — why "contact strength" transfers to harder climbing.

Pulley & crimp biomechanics; injury

  • Schöffl, V., Hochholzer, T., and colleagues. The standard body of work on finger pulley injuries in rock climbers — mechanism (crimp loading), grading, diagnosis, and management. Basis for the warning signs and stop rules in Section 3.
  • Vigouroux, L., Quaine, F., and colleagues. Biomechanical modelling of flexor-tendon and pulley loading in the crimp vs. open grip — why the full crimp places the highest load on the A2 pulley. Basis for the open-hand bias and full-crimp caution.
  • Schöffl, I., Schöffl, V., and colleagues. Epiphyseal (growth-plate) stress fractures of the finger in adolescent climbers — basis for the open-physis / under-18 gate in Section 2.

Tendon adaptation, progression & periodization (foundational principles)

  • Magnusson, S. P., Kjaer, M., and colleagues. Tendon and collagen adaptation to mechanical loading — connective tissue remodels over months, more slowly than muscle or neural drive. Basis for the "let the tissue catch up" prep phase and the patience caveats.
  • American College of Sports Medicine — Position Stand (2009). Progression models in resistance training for healthy adults — general overload, intensity-before-volume, and recovery principles.
  • Kraemer, W. J. & Ratamess, N. A. (2004). Fundamentals of resistance training: progression and exercise prescription — dose-response and how intensity/volume are manipulated across a block.
  • Issurin, V. B. (2010). Periodization principles (including block periodization) — the "apply a stress, adapt, then evolve the stimulus, and deload on purpose" logic of Section 6.
