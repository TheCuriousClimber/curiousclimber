Periodization 101: why great training changes over time

By David McWeeny, MSc Kinesiology·8 min read

Ever noticed how doing the same workout forever eventually stops working? At first you improve fast, then progress slows, then it flatlines. That plateau isn't bad luck — it's biology. The fix has a name: periodization.

Your body adapts, then stops responding

Training is a conversation with your body. You apply a stress (a workout), your body recovers and adapts to handle that stress better next time. But once it has adapted, the same stress is no longer challenging — so it stops driving change. To keep improving, the stimulus has to keep evolving. Periodization is simply the planned, deliberate way of changing your training over time so adaptation never runs out of road.

Periodization is just a fancy word for “having a plan that changes on purpose” — instead of doing the same thing until it stops working.

The levers you can pull

A program is built from a handful of variables. Periodization is the art of adjusting them over weeks and months:

VariableWhat it meansRough example shift
IntensityHow hard / heavy each effort isModerate → very heavy
VolumeTotal amount of work (sets × reps)High → lower as intensity rises
RepsRepetitions per set8–12 → 3–5 → 1–3
RestRecovery between setsShort → long for max strength
FrequencySessions per week for a qualityAdjusted to recovery
Exercise typeWhich movements you emphasiseGeneral → climbing-specific

A simple climbing example

One classic approach moves you through phases (mesocycles), each with a job:

Base

Higher volume, moderate intensity. Build work capacity, tendon resilience and muscle foundation.

Strength

Heavier loads, fewer reps, longer rest. Drive maximal force and recruitment.

Power

Fast, explosive efforts. Turn that new strength into quick, forceful movement.

Peak / Perform

Reduce fatigue, sharpen skill on the wall, and send when it counts.

Each phase sets up the next. The base makes you robust enough to lift heavy; the strength phase gives you force to convert into power; the peak lets that fitness surface when fatigue drops. Rotating focus this way keeps every phase feeling novel to your body — so adaptation keeps coming.

Don't forget deloads

Every few weeks, a lighter “deload” lets accumulated fatigue drain away so the adaptations you've built can surface. Backing off on purpose is part of the plan, not a failure of willpower.

Why this matters for buying a program

A good training program isn't just a list of exercises — it's those exercises arranged intelligently over time. That arrangement is where most of the value lives, and it's the hardest part to design yourself.
