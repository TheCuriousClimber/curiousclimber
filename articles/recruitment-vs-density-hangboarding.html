Max hangs vs. repeaters: recruitment vs. “density” hangboarding

By David McWeeny, MSc Kinesiology·8 min read

Search “how to hangboard for finger strength” and you'll hit the same fork in the road every time: heavy max hangs (a handful of near-maximal holds with long rests) versus repeaters — higher-volume, on-off intervals sometimes called “density” or work-capacity hangboarding. Climbers argue about which one is “best,” but that's the wrong question. They train different things. The useful question is which one does what, and when you should use each.

This article gives you the honest answer. We'll define both protocols, explain the physiology underneath them in plain language, look at what the evidence actually shows, and then lay out how to sequence them across a season without hurting yourself.

First principle: finger “strength” is forearm strength plus healthy tissue

Quick recap, because it changes everything. There are almost no muscles in your fingers — the muscles that curl them live in your forearm and pull on your fingertips through long tendons, which are held against the bone by small bands called pulleys (the finger anatomy article covers this in detail). So “finger strength” is really three things stacked together: how hard your forearm muscles can pull, how well your nervous system drives them, and how much load your tendons and pulleys can tolerate. Different hangboard protocols push on different layers of that stack.

What max hangs are — and why they build recruitment

A max hang is a short, near-maximal effort: hang a defined edge (a 20 mm flat edge is the standard benchmark) for roughly 7–10 seconds at a load that's genuinely hard, rest a long time (2–4 minutes), and repeat for only a few sets. Low reps, high force, full recovery.

The main thing this trains is recruitment — how fully and how fast your nervous system switches on the available muscle. Your muscles are controlled in chunks called motor units (a nerve plus the fibres it commands). Most people never voluntarily switch all of them on at once; there's a reserve. Heavy, brief efforts teach your nervous system to recruit more of those motor units and fire them faster, which is also what rate of force development — how quickly you can produce force — depends on. That's a neural adaptation, and it's why you can get meaningfully stronger fingers without your forearms getting visibly bigger. For climbers chasing a high strength-to-weight ratio, that's the dream: more force, almost no added mass (see Strength ≠ size).

Max hangs are about the ceiling of force — teaching your body to pull harder right now. That ceiling is what makes a small hold suddenly usable.

This is exactly the quality climbers call contact strength: the force you can express almost instantly when you latch a hold. Higher-performing climbers tend to have both greater maximal finger force and a faster rate of force development than lower-performing ones (Levernier & Laffaye and related work) — which is why this stimulus transfers so directly to harder moves.

What “density” / repeater work is — and what it actually adapts

Repeaters flip the dial. A classic set is something like 7 seconds on, 3 seconds off, repeated 6 times for one “rep,” then several sets, usually at a lower intensity than a max hang. The total time under tension is much higher, the load is lower, and the rests are shorter. “Density” protocols are a cousin: lots of submaximal hanging volume packed into a session to build work capacity — the ability to do more quality finger work before quality falls apart.

What does that build? Less maximal force, and more of everything that supports maximal force:

  • Work capacity and local endurance — your forearms tolerate more accumulated loading, so you can climb longer and train harder before fading.
  • Structural / tissue tolerance — higher volumes of submaximal loading are a gentler, repeatable stimulus for the tendons and pulleys, which is the layer that most often gets hurt.
  • Grip skill and technique — more reps means more practice holding position without your fingers clawing open under fatigue.

That last point deserves care and honesty. It's tempting to say repeaters “build tendon” the way heavy hangs “build muscle,” but the science is more modest than the internet suggests. Tendon and collagen tissue does remodel in response to mechanical loading — it gets stiffer and more tolerant — but it does so over months, not weeks, and much more slowly than muscle or neural drive adapt (Magnusson & Kjaer and colleagues). Repeaters aren't a magic tendon pill; they're a way to accumulate a lot of moderate, structured load that connective tissue can adapt to safely over time. The value is the gradual, repeatable dose, not a special tissue-only signal.

The one-line summary

  • Max hangs (recruitment): low reps, high force, long rest → raises your maximal finger force and how fast you can express it. Mostly a neural adaptation.
  • Repeaters / density (work capacity): higher volume, lower force, short rest → builds tolerance, local endurance, and a base of accumulated load your tissues can adapt to.

What the evidence actually says

The most-cited direct comparison here is the work of López-Rivera & González-Badillo, who compared maximal-load (“max added weight”) hangboarding against intermittent/repeater-style methods in climbers, including combined approaches. The honest takeaway from this and follow-up fingerboard research (e.g. Medernach, Kleinöder and colleagues, who found dedicated fingerboard training raises maximal finger strength beyond climbing alone) is roughly this:

  • Dedicated hangboarding beats climbing alone for building maximal finger strength — structure helps.
  • Maximal-intensity work is the more direct route to maximal-force gains, which matches the physiology: to raise the force ceiling you have to load near it.
  • Combining methods can work well, and the best choice depends on the athlete and the phase — which is the whole argument for sequencing them rather than picking a winner.

These are small-sample studies on a hard-to-measure quality, so treat the numbers as directional, not gospel. But the direction is consistent and it lines up with a century of resistance-training principles: intensity drives maximal strength; volume drives capacity and tolerance.

How to choose: training age and goal

Two questions decide it.

1. How much finger-training history do you have? If you've climbed for less than a year or two, or have never deliberately trained fingers, you should not start with heavy max hangs. Your muscles and nervous system adapt fast, but your tendons and pulleys are the slow link — muscle gets strong faster than tendon does, and rushing that mismatch is the single most common way climbers pop a pulley. Beginners get almost everything they need from climbing volume plus, at most, submaximal, higher-volume finger work that builds tolerance patiently.

2. What are you actually short on? If small holds feel impossible and you fail the instant you touch them, you're short on maximal force — that's a recruitment problem, and max hangs are the tool. If you can do the individual moves but your forearms balloon and give out over a long route or a session, you're short on capacity — that's a density/repeater and endurance conversation, not a max-strength one.

Match the protocol to the bottleneck

  • “I can't even hold it” → maximal force ceiling is low → max hangs (once you have the training base and healthy fingers).
  • “I can hold it once, then I'm cooked” → work capacity / endurance is the limiter → repeater / density volume, plus route-specific endurance work.
  • New to finger training → build tolerance first with submaximal volume and climbing; earn the right to go heavy.

How to sequence them across a season

You don't have to choose forever — you choose per block. This is periodization: apply a stress, let the body adapt, then evolve the stimulus, and deload on purpose (see Periodization 101). A sane finger year uses density work to build the base and max hangs to raise the ceiling, in that order:

  1. Base / preparation (weeks). Higher-volume, submaximal hanging — repeater-style or “density” work at moderate effort. This re-grooves technique and, over time, primes tendons and pulleys for heavier loading. It's the on-ramp, not the destination.
  2. Max strength / recruitment (weeks). Shift to low-rep, high-force max hangs with long rests. Intensity climbs while volume drops — the standard shape of a strength block. This is where the force ceiling actually moves.
  3. Peak & transfer (weeks). Very short, very heavy efforts to express the strength, plus powerful climbing on the wall so the new force becomes real movement.
  4. Deload, then rotate. Back off so the adaptation surfaces, then change the stimulus. Don't run maximal finger blocks back-to-back indefinitely — connective tissue rewards patience, so alternate hard finger blocks with other qualities across the year.

The throughline: density/tolerance work earns you the right to do heavy recruitment work, and heavy recruitment work is what raises the number. Skipping the base to “get to the good stuff” is exactly how tissue gets ahead of itself.

Non-negotiable safety (this is where climbers get hurt)

Finger training carries real, specific injury risk — most commonly strains or tears of the A2 and A4 pulleys. None of what follows is optional, and none of it is medical advice.

  • Bias toward open-hand and half-crimp. Biomechanical modelling shows the full crimp loads the A2 pulley the hardest (Vigouroux, Quaine and colleagues), and it's the grip most linked to pulley injury. Do the bulk of your hangboarding in open-hand and half-crimp; don't chase max loads in a full crimp.
  • Know the warning signs. A sharp pain or an audible/palpable “pop” at the base or middle of a finger, swelling, or a “bowstringing” sensation are stop-now, get-assessed events (Schöffl, Hochholzer and colleagues). Dull, symmetrical forearm ache is fine; sharp, localized, one-sided finger pain is not.
  • Respect the timeline. Tissue adapts in months, not weeks (Magnusson & Kjaer). Progress load slowly and patiently — intensity before volume, and never both at once.
  • Screen first, especially if you're young. If your growth plates may still be open (roughly under ~18–19, but it varies), do not hangboard maximally — epiphyseal growth-plate stress fractures of the finger are well documented in adolescent climbers (Schöffl & Schöffl and colleagues). When in doubt, wait and see a sports physician.
A missed session costs you nothing. A popped A2 costs months. When in doubt, do less.

The bottom line

Max hangs and repeaters aren't rivals; they're different tools for different jobs. Max hangs raise your maximal finger force through better recruitment — the ceiling that makes small holds usable. Repeater / density work builds the capacity and tolerance that let you train hard and give your tissues a gradual, repeatable dose to adapt to. Beginners build the base; stronger climbers periodize both across the year, going heavy only after they've earned it — and always with the pulleys' timeline, not the ego's, setting the pace.
