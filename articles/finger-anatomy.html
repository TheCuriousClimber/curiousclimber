The climber's fingers: pulleys, tendons & staying healthy

By David McWeeny, MSc Kinesiology·9 min read

Your fingers are the point where your whole body meets the rock. They're also where climbers get hurt most often. A little anatomy goes a long way toward training them hard and keeping them healthy.

The muscles aren't in your fingers

Here's a surprise: there are almost no muscles in your fingers. The muscles that curl your fingers live in your forearm. They connect to your fingertips through long tendons — think of them as strong, rope-like cables running down through your hand. When those forearm muscles contract, they pull the cables, and your fingers grip.

This is why climbing builds such prominent forearms, and why finger “strength” is really forearm strength plus the health of the tendons and their guides.

Pulleys: the rings that keep tendons on track

If those tendon cables ran in a straight line, they'd bowstring away from the bone every time you crimped. To stop that, each finger has a series of pulleys — small fibrous bands (named A1 through A5) that hold the tendons snug against the bones. The A2 and A4 pulleys are the workhorses, and the A2 is the one climbers most often injure. A “popped pulley” is usually a strain or tear of one of these bands, often felt as a sharp pain or even a pop at the base of a finger.

Crimp vs. open hand — why grip position matters

A full crimp (fingers bent sharply, thumb over) puts the highest load on the A2 pulley — it's powerful but risky. An open-hand / half-crimp grip spreads load more kindly across the tendons. Smart training builds strength in safer positions first and introduces full-crimp loading carefully.

Tendons adapt — but slowly

Here's the catch that injures so many climbers: muscle gets stronger faster than tendon and pulley tissue does. Your forearm muscles and nervous system can ramp up quickly, so you feel strong and start pulling on tiny holds — but the connective tissue that has to transmit that force hasn't caught up yet. Push too hard, too soon, and the weak link (a pulley) fails.

The most common finger injury isn't a lack of strength — it's a mismatch. Your muscles wrote a cheque your tendons couldn't cash.

The solution is progressive, patient loading: increasing the demand on your fingers gradually, over months, so tendons and pulleys thicken and toughen to match your muscle. This is exactly why structured finger training beats random hard bouldering for long-term progress — and why every good climbing program ramps intensity carefully.

Antagonists and the rest of the chain

Fingers don't work alone. Behind them sits a chain: forearm flexors, the wrist, elbow, shoulder and back. Overtraining the pulling side without also training the pushing and stabilising muscles is a recipe for cranky elbows (climber's elbow) and shoulders. Balanced resistance training — including wrist extensors, rotator cuff and pushing movements — keeps the whole chain healthy so your fingers have something strong to pull against.

Practical takeaways

  • Warm up your fingers thoroughly before hard loading.
  • Progress finger intensity slowly — tendons take months, not weeks.
  • Favour open-hand/half-crimp for a lot of your training volume.
  • Train antagonists (push, wrist extension, cuff) to protect elbows and shoulders.
  • Sharp pain or a “pop” means stop — pulleys don't respond well to being pushed through.
