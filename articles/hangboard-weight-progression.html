Hangboard Progression: How Much Weight to Add, When to Hold, and When to Stop

By David McWeeny, MSc Kinesiology·8 min read

You did a weighted hang, it felt manageable, and the obvious next move is to throw another five pounds on the belt. That instinct is exactly how climbers end up with a sore, clicky finger and a month off the wall. The problem isn't that you added weight. It's how much, and whether you'd earned it.

This article gives you three things: a percentage rule for how much load to add, a one-variable rule for when to add it, and a set of safety gates — clear pain criteria that tell you to hold or stop. Get these right and progression becomes almost boring, which, for fingers, is the goal.

Why "add 5 lb when it feels easy" injures pulleys

Here's the piece almost no forum answer mentions: the load you feel at your fingertips is not the load your finger structures actually see. Your finger bones are held to the flexor tendons by small bands of tissue called pulleys — the A2 pulley at the base of the finger is the one climbers rupture most. In a crimp the joints bend sharply, and that geometry acts like a lever: the tendon bowstrings against the pulley, so the force on the pulley can substantially exceed the force at the fingertip.

This isn't hand-waving. A biomechanical modelling study by Vigouroux and colleagues (2006) estimated tendon and pulley forces in climbing grips and found the crimp loads the A2 pulley dramatically more than an open "slope" grip — in their model, by a large multiple. The translation: a crimp is a force amplifier. Add "just five pounds" at the fingertip and the pulley behind it sees a bigger jump than the plate suggests — maybe 3% more at your hand, but a much larger spike at the tissue that fails.

That's why loading is so grip-dependent, and why max hangs and repeaters can't be progressed identically — the near-maximal crimping in a max hang stresses pulleys far more than lighter, open-handed repeater work. If that distinction is fuzzy, read how max hangs and repeaters differ first, and the anatomy is laid out in the climber's finger anatomy guide.

The number on the belt is the load you feel. The load that hurts you is bigger, and it hides inside the crimp.

The rule: how much to add

Because the tissue sees more than the plate says, the safe increment is smaller than most people think. The rule:

  • Add no more than about 2–5% of your total system weight per step. "Total system weight" is your bodyweight plus anything already hanging from you — so for a 70 kg climber, 2–5% is roughly 1.5–3.5 kg, often less than a single standard plate.
  • Use the smallest increment you actually have. If your smallest plate jumps you past 5%, use a lighter option: fractional plates, a length of chain, a loaded pack, or a counterweight pulley to remove a kilo. Micro-loading isn't fussy; it's the point.
  • Only add after a session you finished with reps in reserve. If the last session left you at your limit — grip breaking, fingers clawing open — you haven't earned more load. You add weight after a load feels controlled, not to find out whether the next one is possible.

"Reps in reserve" is a borrowed strength-training idea: finishing a set feeling you could have done another quality rep or two. On a hangboard it's the difference between a hang that ends because you chose to stop and one that ends because your fingers gave out. Progress off the first kind.

One variable per week: load or volume or duration

Weight is only one dial. You can also add volume (more sets or hangs), extend duration (longer holds), shrink the edge, or harden the grip. Every one of those raises the demand on the same tissue. Turn two at once and you've stacked two increments you can't tell apart — so if something flares, you won't know which to walk back.

So change one variable per week. Add a little load this week; hold everything else. Add a set next week; leave the load. This isn't caution for its own sake — it's the only way to get clean feedback about what your fingers tolerate. One dial, one week, one signal to read.

The progression rule in one box

  • How much: ≤2–5% of total system weight, or the smallest increment you own.
  • When: only after a session finished with reps in reserve.
  • What: one variable per week — load or volume or duration or edge, never several at once.

The pain-monitoring rule

Numbers on a belt are easy; reading your own fingers is the real skill. The best-known framework comes from tendon rehabilitation: Silbernagel and colleagues (2007) showed that people with Achilles tendon pain could keep loading — and recover as well as those who rested — as long as pain stayed within a set limit and returned to baseline by the next day. That "pain-monitoring model" is now standard load management.

Applied to fingers, and deliberately more conservative than the original (their model allowed up to 5/10; for pulleys I use a tighter ceiling), the working rule is:

  • Loading is acceptable at 3/10 pain or less — mild, tolerable, not sharp.
  • It must settle within 24 hours. Waking up the next day with the finger back to normal is the green light.
  • There is no upward trend week to week. If the same session hurts a little more each week, that trend matters, not any single day.

Dull, symmetrical forearm fatigue is normal. Sharp, localized, one-sided pain at a finger joint is not — that's the signal to hold or back off, never to "push through."

When training stops and a clinician starts

Some signs mean you're done loading until a professional has looked at it — not something to monitor:

  • Pain that persists beyond 48 hours, or trends upward week over week.
  • Any audible or felt pop.
  • Swelling around a finger joint.
  • A bowstringing sensation — the tendon lifting away from the bone when you flex against resistance.

Any of these → stop loading and see a physiotherapist or sports physician before you train fingers again. This article is educational, not a diagnosis or a treatment plan.

Safety gates: green, yellow, red

Put the pain rule and the progression rule together and every session sorts into one of three lanes. Read the lane before you touch the plates.

  • Green — progress. Last session felt controlled with reps in reserve, any ache stayed ≤3/10 and cleared inside 24 hours, and there's no upward trend. This is the only lane in which you add your one small increment.
  • Yellow — hold. The load felt near your limit, an ache lingered but is settling, or you can't tick every green box. Repeat the same session unchanged — same load, same volume — until it drops back to green. Holding a number is a decision, not a failure.
  • Red — stop. Any clinician-route sign above: a pop, swelling, bowstringing, or pain past 48 hours. Stop loading and get it looked at.

Most weeks are yellow, and that's correct. Green weeks — the ones where you actually add weight — are rarer than climbers expect, which is exactly why the "add 5 lb every session" habit does damage.

When to stop progressing and deload

Progression isn't a line that goes up forever. Adaptation stalls, small aches accumulate, and the tissue that adapts most slowly — the collagen in tendons and pulleys, which remodels over months, not weeks — falls behind the fast-adapting nervous system. When that happens, more load is the wrong answer. You deload: cut finger volume to roughly half at an easy intensity, or take the fingers off the board and climb easy for about a week, so the slow tissue catches up and the adaptation surfaces.

Watch for the tells: your working load stops moving for two or three sessions despite good recovery, sessions feel systemically flat, or a low-grade ache keeps flirting with that 3/10 line. None of that means you're injured — it means the well is dry and needs refilling. This is why progression lives inside a periodized structure rather than running open-loop: the 8-week finger strength block shows where deloads sit between phases, and the periodization primer explains why the quiet weeks are where you actually get stronger.

A held or deloaded week costs you nothing you'll remember. A ruptured A2 pulley costs a season. When the signals are mixed, add less.

FAQ

Should I use a pulley system to remove weight? Yes — a counterweight or pulley that takes weight off is a legitimate tool, especially if bodyweight on your edge is already near-maximal or you're easing back after time off. Treat removed weight like any other variable: reduce the assistance in small steps, only after a session finished with reps in reserve.

How often should I re-test my max? Rarely. A true max test is itself a near-maximal pulley load, so frequent testing adds risk without adding training. Most climbers only re-test at the end of a block — roughly every 6–8 weeks, ideally in a fresh peaking week. Between tests, the reps-in-reserve feel of your sets already tells you when to nudge the load up.

Is a 20 mm edge the standard? It's the common reference edge in research and testing because it loads the fingers hard without forcing a full crimp and makes results comparable between people. It's a sensible default, not a law — shrinking the edge is a valid way to add intensity without adding weight. With a history of finger pain, progressing through load rather than edge size is the more conservative route.
