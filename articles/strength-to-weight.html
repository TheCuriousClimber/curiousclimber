Strength ≠ size: the neural side of getting stronger

By David McWeeny, MSc Kinesiology·7 min read

If you only remember one idea from this whole site, make it this one: you can get much stronger without getting much bigger. Understanding how that works is the key that unlocks smart climbing training.

Your muscles are controlled by motor units

A muscle isn't one on/off switch. It's controlled in chunks called motor units — a single nerve and the bundle of muscle fibres it commands. When you need a little force, your body switches on a few small motor units. When you need a lot, it recruits bigger ones and fires them faster. Most people never come close to using all of their motor units at once; there's a reserve you can learn to tap into.

Strength training teaches your nervous system to do exactly that: recruit more motor units, fire them faster, and coordinate them better. These are called neural adaptations, and they're responsible for the bulk of the strength you gain in the early weeks and months of a program — long before your muscles get visibly bigger.

Early strength gains are mostly your brain learning to drive the muscle you already own. Size comes later, slower, and only if you train specifically for it.

Why this is perfect for climbers

Climbing rewards a high strength-to-weight ratio — force produced relative to bodyweight. Neural adaptations are the dream scenario for that ratio: the “strength” side goes up sharply while the “weight” side barely moves. You become the same size, but you can pull, hang and lock off with more authority.

This is especially true for the tissue climbers care about most — the fingers and forearms. A big part of finger strength is your ability to recruit the forearm muscles that flex your fingers, and to do it with the tendons and pulleys tolerating high load. Train that, and you gain grip strength with essentially zero weight penalty.

How to train for neural gains (the short version)

  • High force, low reps: heavy sets of ~1–5, or short maximal finger holds.
  • Full recovery between sets: long rests (2–4+ min) so each effort is high quality.
  • Intent: move (or pull) with maximal effort even when the weight allows more reps.
  • Avoid the growth recipe: you're not chasing high-volume “pump” work here.

Does size ever help?

Sometimes, yes — a little muscle can be useful, especially for beginners, for injury resilience, or for very powerful bouldering. The point isn't that muscle is bad; it's that you control the outcome through how you train. Choose low-rep, high-force work and you steer adaptations toward strength; choose high-volume work in a calorie surplus and you steer them toward size. Climbers simply pick the first dial.
