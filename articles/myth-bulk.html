“Lifting weights will make me bulky.” It's the biggest myth in climbing.

By David McWeeny, MSc Kinesiology·10 min read

Walk into almost any climbing gym and you'll hear the same warning: “Don't lift weights — you'll get bulky, and all that extra muscle will just weigh you down.” It sounds reasonable. Climbing is a fight against gravity, and heavier climbers do have to pull more weight up the wall. So more muscle must be bad… right?

Not so fast. This belief rests on a single mistaken assumption: that getting stronger and getting bigger are the same thing. They aren't. Once you understand the difference, the fear falls apart — and the case for adding resistance training to your climbing gets very strong.

The one-sentence version

Well-designed resistance training can make you a lot stronger while adding very little muscle mass, which improves your strength-to-weight ratio — the number that actually matters for climbing.

Strength and size are two different things

When a muscle produces force, two separate factors decide how much force it can make:

  1. How big the muscle is (its cross-sectional area). Bigger muscles have more force-producing machinery.
  2. How well your nervous system uses that muscle. Your brain controls muscle through “motor units” — a nerve plus the muscle fibres it activates. How many motor units you switch on, how fast they fire, and how well they coordinate all determine how much of your muscle's potential you actually express.

Here's the key point: for the first several weeks and months of a new strength program, most of your improvement comes from that second factor — the nervous system — not from muscle growth. This has been shown repeatedly in exercise science since at least the 1970s. Your muscles learn to “turn on” more completely, so you produce more force with essentially the same amount of tissue.

You can think of it like a car. Hypertrophy (muscle growth) is a bigger engine. Neural adaptation is a better driver who finally knows how to use the engine you already have. Climbers can get a lot faster just by hiring the better driver.

Why big muscle growth needs a specific recipe

Muscles don't grow just because you touched a dumbbell. Meaningful muscle growth (hypertrophy) generally requires a particular combination of ingredients:

  • Enough training volume — a lot of hard sets, repeated over weeks, usually in the moderate rep ranges (roughly 8–15 reps) that maximise growth stimulus.
  • Enough calories — you can't build much new tissue in a meaningful energy deficit. Building mass typically needs a calorie surplus.
  • Enough time and consistency aimed at that goal.

Climbing-focused strength programs are built to avoid that recipe. They lean on low reps at high force (think heavy, crisp sets of 1–5, or short maximal finger loads), with plenty of rest between sets. That combination is fantastic for building strength and improving how your nervous system recruits muscle — and it's a relatively weak stimulus for large hypertrophy. The result is the climber's sweet spot: more force, minimal added mass.

✕ The myth

“Any lifting = big muscles = more bodyweight = worse climbing.”

✓ What actually happens

Low-rep, high-force training → mostly neural gains → much more strength, little mass → better strength-to-weight.

The number that matters: strength-to-weight

Climbing performance is closely tied to your strength-to-weight ratio — how much force you can produce relative to how much you weigh. Adding a kilogram of muscle only hurts you if it doesn't buy you more than a kilogram's worth of pulling power. In practice, climbing-style strength training gives you a large jump in force for a tiny change in weight, so the ratio moves in your favour.

And crucially, some of the most important strength gains for climbing happen in tissue that barely weighs anything: your fingers and forearms. Stronger finger flexors and better recruitment let you hold smaller edges and steeper holds. There's essentially no “bulk” penalty there at all — just more grip, more contact strength, and more moves you can hang on to.

What about weight classes in other sports?

Powerlifters and gymnasts routinely get remarkably strong while staying in the same weight class for years. They do it by training for strength (neural, low-rep) rather than size (high-volume, surplus). Climbers can borrow exactly the same approach.

Resistance training does more than make you strong

Even setting the strength-to-weight argument aside, lifting earns its place in a climber's week for reasons that have nothing to do with sending harder today:

  • Injury resistance. Progressively loading tendons, pulleys and connective tissue makes them more resilient. A lot of climbing injuries come from a body that only ever pulls; balanced resistance work is some of the best insurance you can buy.
  • Antagonist balance. Climbing is overwhelmingly a pulling sport. Training the pushing muscles, rotator cuff and wrist extensors keeps your shoulders and elbows healthy so you can keep training at all.
  • Longevity. Strength you build now protects joints and keeps you climbing for decades, not just seasons.

“But climbing already makes me strong”

It does — up to a point. The problem is that climbing loads you in a narrow, self-limiting way: you can only pull as hard as the holds and your skill allow, and your weaker links (a tweaky shoulder, an under-strong pulling pattern, cranky elbows) quietly cap your progress. Resistance training lets you target those weak links directly, with a load you control precisely, and drive adaptations that climbing alone can't. That's why the strongest climbers in the world train off the wall as well as on it.

So should every climber lift?

Almost every climber past the beginner stage benefits from some structured resistance training — but the right dose depends on your goals, experience and history. A boulderer chasing max power, a sport climber fighting the pump on long routes, and someone coming back from a finger injury all need different plans. That's exactly what the rest of this site is for.

The question was never “will lifting make me bulky?” The real question is “how do I train for force without training for size?” — and that has a clear, evidence-based answer.
