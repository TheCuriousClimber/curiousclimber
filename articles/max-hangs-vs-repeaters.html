Max Hangs vs. Repeaters: What Each Actually Trains, and How to Choose

By David McWeeny, MSc Kinesiology·8 min read

Every climber who starts hangboarding hits the same fork in the road: max hangs or repeaters? The internet treats it like a rivalry with a winner. It isn't. Max hangs and repeaters push on different layers of the thing you call finger strength — and once you see which layer each one trains, the choice stops being opinion and becomes a matter of what you're actually short on.

Here's the one-line version: max hangs mostly train your nervous system; repeaters mostly train your tissue and your capacity to work. Below, we define both protocols, explain the physiology in plain language, look at what the studies compared, and give you a decision rule you can use this week.

The two protocols in one table

First, definitions, because half the arguments online come from people picturing different things. A max hang is a short, near-maximal hold with long rest. A repeater is a set of short on-off intervals — classically 7 seconds on, 3 seconds off, repeated about six times — at lower intensity. Both usually reference a 20 mm edge, and both lean on the safety detail in the climber's finger anatomy guide.

What max hangs change: recruitment and rate of force development

There are almost no muscles in your fingers. The muscles that curl them sit in your forearm and pull on your fingertips through long tendons. So when you hang heavy, you're mostly asking your nervous system a question: how much of the muscle you already own can you switch on, and how fast?

Muscles are controlled in chunks called motor units — a nerve plus the fibres it commands. Most people never voluntarily fire all of them at once; there's a reserve. Brief, near-maximal efforts train two neural qualities: recruitment (switching on more motor units) and rate coding (firing them faster). Together those drive rate of force development — how quickly you can produce force, which matters when you latch a small hold with a split second to hold it. This is why you can get much stronger fingers without your forearms getting visibly bigger — largely a wiring upgrade, not a size one. The strength-is-not-size explainer unpacks this. In climbers, higher maximal finger force and faster rate of force development track with higher performance levels, and dedicated finger training has been shown to raise both (Levernier & Laffaye, 2019).

Max hangs raise the ceiling of force — teaching your body to pull harder right now. That ceiling is what makes a tiny hold suddenly usable.

What repeaters change: capacity, time under tension, and tissue

Repeaters flip the dial. The load is lower, the total time under tension is far higher, and the rests are short. That combination trains a different set of qualities:

  • Work capacity and local endurance — your forearms tolerate more accumulated load, so you can climb longer and train harder before quality falls apart.
  • Tissue tolerance — higher volumes of submaximal loading are a gentler, repeatable stimulus for the tendons and pulleys, the layer that most often gets hurt.
  • Grip skill under fatigue — more reps means more practice holding position without your fingers clawing open as you tire.

It's tempting to say repeaters "build tendon" the way heavy hangs "build muscle," but the honest picture is more modest. Tendon and collagen tissue does remodel in response to loading — becoming stiffer and more tolerant, which transmits force more efficiently. But a systematic review of tendon adaptation found these changes accrue over months of consistent loading, not weeks, and more slowly than muscle or neural drive (Bohm, Mersmann & Arampatzis, 2015). Repeaters aren't a magic tendon pill; their value is a gradual, repeatable dose tissue can adapt to safely over time.

The one-line summary

  • Max hangs (neural): low reps, high force, long rest → raise maximal finger force and how fast you express it.
  • Repeaters (structural): higher volume, lower force, short rest → build capacity, endurance, and a base of load your tissues can adapt to.

What the studies actually compared

The most-cited direct comparison in this space is the work of López-Rivera & González-Badillo (2012), who compared maximal-load ("max added weight") hangboarding against intermittent/repeater-style methods in trained climbers, including a combined approach, over roughly eight-week programs. Alongside the tendon and finger-force research above, the picture that emerges is consistent:

  • Structured hangboarding beats climbing alone for building maximal finger strength — deliberate loading helps.
  • Maximal-intensity work is the more direct route to maximal-force gains, which matches the physiology: to raise the force ceiling you have to load near it.
  • Combining methods works well, and the best choice depends on the athlete and the training phase — which is the whole argument for sequencing rather than picking a permanent winner.

These are small-sample studies of a hard-to-measure quality, so treat the specifics as directional. But the direction is stable, and it lines up with a century of resistance-training principles: intensity drives maximal strength; volume drives capacity and tolerance.

The decision rule: what are you actually short on?

Two questions settle it for most climbers.

1. How much finger-training history do you have? If you've climbed for less than a year or two, or have never deliberately trained fingers, don't start with heavy max hangs. Muscle and nervous system adapt quickly, but tendon and pulley are the slow link — and rushing that mismatch is a classic route to injury. Beginners get almost everything they need from climbing volume plus, at most, submaximal higher-volume finger work. (Dedicated hangboarding isn't designed for climbers under about 16 at all.)

2. Where does the move actually fail? If small holds feel impossible and you're off the instant you touch them, your maximal force ceiling is the limiter — that's a recruitment problem, and max hangs are the tool. If you can do the individual moves but your forearms balloon and give out over a route or a session, your work capacity is the limiter — that's a repeater and endurance conversation, not a max-strength one.

Match the protocol to the bottleneck

  • "I can't even hold it" → low force ceiling → max hangs (once you have the base and healthy fingers).
  • "I hold it once, then I'm cooked" → capacity is the limiter → repeaters / density volume, plus on-wall endurance.
  • New to finger training → build tolerance first with submaximal volume; earn the right to go heavy.

Why sequence beats choice

Here's the shift that ends the debate: you don't choose max hangs or repeaters forever — you choose per block. This is periodization, and the sane order is capacity first, intensity second. A base of submaximal, repeater-style volume gives your tendons and pulleys time to adapt; only then do you shift to heavy max hangs to raise the ceiling. Running maximal blocks back-to-back asks connective tissue to keep pace with a far faster-adapting nervous system — a bad trade. That is exactly what the periodization primer walks through: accumulate before you intensify, then deload so the adaptation surfaces.

Progression inside either protocol

Whichever protocol you're running, progress it the same disciplined way. Change one variable per week — load, or volume, or duration, never all at once — and when you add load, add no more than about 2–5% of total system weight, or the smallest increment you have, and only after a session you completed with something left in reserve. For the full rule — how much to add, when to hold, and the pulley safety gates that say stop — see how much weight to add to hangboard hangs. The companion piece on recruitment versus density hangboarding goes deeper on protocol detail.

And treat these as non-negotiable, because this is where climbers get hurt:

  • Watch the pain signal. Loading is acceptable at ≤3/10 pain that settles within 24 hours and does not trend upward week to week. Dull, symmetrical forearm ache is normal; sharp, localized, one-sided finger pain is not.
  • Know the stop-now signs. Pain that persists beyond 48 hours, any audible or felt pop, swelling, or a bowstringing sensation → stop and see a physiotherapist or sports physician before loading again.
  • Bias toward open-hand and half-crimp for the bulk of your hangboarding, and respect the tissue timeline — it adapts in months, not weeks.
A missed session costs you nothing. A popped pulley costs months. When in doubt, do less.

FAQ

Can I do both in one week? Yes, but not in equal measure or on tired fingers. Make one quality the block's priority and use the other as a lighter dose, with at least 48 hours between finger-intensive sessions. Stop if anything sharp appears or pain doesn't settle within 24 hours.

How long should a max hang be? Roughly 7–10 seconds at a genuinely hard load, with 2–3 minutes of rest and only a handful of hangs. If you can hold much longer than 10 seconds, the edge is too big or the weight too light to be training maximal force.

Are repeaters safer for beginners? Lower-intensity repeater or density work is usually a gentler on-ramp, because it accumulates moderate, repeatable load. But hangboarding of any kind isn't designed for climbers under about 16 or with under ~1 year of consistent climbing — build the base through climbing first, then progress one variable at a time.
